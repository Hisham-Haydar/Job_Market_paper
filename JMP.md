# Project JMP: Core Question, Motivation, Progress, and Direction

**Working title:** *Opportunity Constraints as a Driver of Welfare Inequality: A Latent-Jobs Structural Decomposition Approach*

## Central question

Project JMP asks a precise question: **how much of observed inequality in money-metric well-being is due to unequal job opportunities rather than to heterogeneous preferences?** The project studies labor supply not as a free choice over hours on a common budget set, but as a choice among **latent job packages**. Under this perspective, people do not simply choose how much to work; they choose among the jobs that are actually available to them, and these opportunities may differ across individuals and households.

This leads to three connected questions. First, to what extent do standard structural labor supply models overstate preference heterogeneity because they absorb opportunity differences into tastes? Second, how should well-being be measured when individuals face different feasible job sets rather than a universal choice set? Third, under a responsibility-sensitive view in which unequal opportunities are not fully the individual’s responsibility, how much of welfare inequality should be attributed to opportunity differences and how much should remain attributed to preferences?

The paper therefore aims to move beyond a simple descriptive comparison of incomes or labor supply choices. Its objective is to identify a **mechanism** behind welfare inequality: whether inequality reflects what people prefer, or whether it reflects the fact that some people face much more restricted labor market opportunities than others.

## Why this matters

This question matters for both positive economics and normative economics. On the positive side, observed labor market outcomes are often ambiguous. The same observed bundle of hours, earnings, and disposable income can mean very different things for different people. For one person, it may be the preferred outcome among many attractive options; for another, it may be only the least bad option in a very constrained opportunity set. If these distinctions are ignored, standard models can misread constrained outcomes as preferred outcomes and can therefore bias the estimated distribution of tastes, labor supply elasticities, and welfare effects.

On the normative side, the distinction between opportunities and preferences is central. If welfare inequality partly reflects unequal access to jobs, hours packages, or earnings opportunities, then it should not automatically be interpreted as the result of individual choices or tastes. A project that takes this seriously can speak directly to questions of compensation and responsibility. In that sense, the paper is not only about measuring welfare inequality; it is about measuring it in a way that distinguishes between what people choose and what the labor market makes feasible for them.

This is why the project is centered on **decomposition** rather than on a simple ranking exercise. A ranking tells us who appears better or worse off under a given metric. A decomposition tells us **what drives the inequality in that metric**, and therefore what could change it. That makes the contribution more scientific, more policy-relevant, and more defensible as a job market paper.

## Work already done in the project

A substantial amount of conceptual and preparatory work has already been completed. The project has moved beyond a broad interest in jobs and welfare and has now locked a specific JMP identity. The core question, supporting subquestions, preferred direction, narrower fallback, and boundaries of the paper are already clearly defined. In particular, the project has established that the JMP should be a **single-country structural paper** on latent jobs, money-metric welfare, and the decomposition of welfare inequality into opportunity and preference components, rather than a generic cross-country welfare ranking, a standard tax-reform simulation, or a microsimulation-led paper.

A second major component already completed is the literature synthesis. The project has assembled and mapped a broad corpus spanning four central literatures: constrained structural labor supply, latent jobs and identification, welfare analysis for discrete choice, and inequality of opportunity and fairness. This work has already clarified where the strongest contribution lies. The project does not simply replicate existing welfare comparisons. Instead, it builds on them to make **inequality decomposition** the central object, with the opportunity mechanism treated as a first-class economic object rather than as a technical detail.

A third part already done is the design and communication infrastructure. The project now includes a concept note, decision memo, roadmap, prototype memo, and a set of mock seminar materials. These materials include slide outlines, scripts, Q&A files, and draft figures and tables related to model fit, elasticities, opportunity parameters, responsibility sensitivity, and welfare decomposition. This means the project is no longer at the stage of abstract brainstorming; it already has a coherent seminar-ready skeleton. In parallel, a separate theory note on jobs and well-being has helped clarify the normative language of opportunity, ability sets, compensation, and responsibility. That theory work informs the empirical paper, while remaining distinct from it.

## Methods used and empirical strategy

Methodologically, the project is designed as a structural labor supply paper with an explicit opportunity mechanism. The baseline empirical model is a **RURO or latent-jobs discrete choice model** in which individuals or households choose among job packages rather than choosing hours continuously on a common budget line. In the first empirical prototype, job packages are defined parsimoniously by hours categories and wage or earnings categories, with the possibility of adding a limited number of job attributes such as sector if the data can support it.

For each possible job package, disposable income is computed using **tax-benefit simulation**. Microsimulation is therefore important, but only as an input into the budget mapping; it is not the headline contribution of the paper. The key econometric task is to estimate separately, as far as identification allows, two distinct objects: preferences over consumption and leisure, and the **opportunity mechanism** that governs which job packages are feasible for which individuals. This opportunity mechanism is intended to vary with observable circumstances such as region, education, or demographic type.

Once the model is estimated, the project computes a **money-metric welfare measure** that is defined relative to the constrained feasible set rather than to a universal choice set. This is crucial: welfare should be evaluated conditional on what people can actually choose. The final step is the decomposition of welfare inequality. The project proposes counterfactual equalizations of the opportunity mechanism across circumstance groups, along with comparable counterfactual treatments of preference heterogeneity, and then attributes inequality shares using an **order-independent decomposition rule**, most naturally Shapley-style. The core output is therefore not only a behavioral model, but a welfare decomposition that isolates the contribution of opportunities.

## What remains to be done

What remains is now relatively concrete. The first task is to implement the minimal prototype. That involves fixing the welfare object, choosing the welfare inequality index, selecting the circumstance groups, and estimating the smallest defensible opportunity mechanism. The prototype should be simple enough to be interpretable, but rich enough to show whether opportunity heterogeneity has quantitative bite.

The next step is to produce a first decomposition output. Ideally, this would be one table or figure showing baseline welfare inequality, welfare inequality under opportunity equalization, welfare inequality under preference neutralization or referencing, and welfare inequality under both. This would immediately show whether the central claim of the project is plausible.

After that, the project needs robustness and identification work. This includes varying the definition of circumstance groups, testing alternative parameterizations of the opportunity mechanism, and writing a clear identification note explaining what is learned from observed choices, what is pinned down by structure, and where the limits of separation between opportunities and preferences remain. The roadmap also suggests clean counterfactuals that separately equalize wage offers and hours offers, which would allow the paper to identify which dimension of labor market opportunity matters most.

## Possible results and overall direction

The most direct possible result is that a **nontrivial share of welfare inequality is opportunity-driven**. If that happens, the paper would show that standard models exaggerate taste heterogeneity because they ignore unequal feasible job sets. A second possible result is that once opportunities are modeled explicitly, estimated preferences become less dispersed and some previously accepted welfare comparisons change substantially. A third possibility is that opportunity inequality matters strongly for some groups or some margins, but not for others. For example, it may matter more across regions, education groups, or between full-time and part-time opportunity structures.

Even a weaker empirical result would still be useful. If the opportunity share turns out to be small or unstable, that would still clarify when opportunity-sensitive modeling matters and when standard approaches are more robust than expected. In other words, the project remains informative even if the final quantitative effect is smaller than initially anticipated.

The direction of the paper is therefore now well defined. This is a structural welfare and inequality paper whose distinctive contribution is to treat job opportunities consistently across **estimation, welfare evaluation, and decomposition**. It is an empirical paper with a clear normative backbone, not a pure theory paper and not a descriptive ranking exercise. The immediate objective is to show, as cleanly as possible, whether unequal job opportunities are an important driver of welfare inequality once labor supply is modeled as constrained job choice. If the prototype yields a stable and interpretable opportunity component, then Project JMP has a distinctive and defensible job market paper contribution.
