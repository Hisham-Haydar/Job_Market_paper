# Full citation

Fleurbaey, M. (2009). Beyond GDP: The Quest for a Measure of Social Welfare. *Journal of Economic Literature*, 47(4), 1029--1075.

# One-sentence contribution

Critically surveys four approaches to measuring social welfare beyond GDP -- corrected GDP/monetary aggregates, subjective well-being (happiness), capabilities, and synthetic indicators -- and argues that the equivalence approach (equivalent incomes grounded in the theory of fair allocation) is the most promising framework for constructing a rigorous, individualistic, preference-respecting alternative to GDP.

# Why this paper matters

This is the definitive survey connecting welfare economics theory to the practical project of measuring social welfare. It provides the intellectual architecture for understanding how different welfare measurement approaches relate to each other and to the axiomatic results from social choice and fair allocation theory. For labour supply welfare analysis, the paper establishes why equivalent income -- not GDP, not happiness, not raw capabilities -- is the theoretically best-grounded metric for interpersonal welfare comparisons that respect individual preferences while accounting for non-income dimensions of well-being.

# Core research question

What does state-of-the-art welfare economics say about the prospects for constructing better alternatives to GDP as measures of social welfare, and which approaches are most promising?

# Economic setting and context

Survey paper covering theoretical and applied welfare economics. No specific country or empirical application, but discusses examples from international comparisons (HDI, OECD countries) and from policy evaluation contexts.

# Model / theoretical framework

The paper does not develop a single model but reviews and connects several frameworks:

**1. Monetary aggregates / Corrected GDP (Sections 2--3):**
- Revealed preference argument: social welfare $W^*(X) = \max W(u_1(x_1), \ldots, u_n(x_n))$ s.t. $\sum x_i = X$. Under optimal redistribution, $W$ increases with $X$. But this requires optimal redistribution and identical preferences.
- Decomposition (eq. 2): $e(m_1, \ldots, m_n) = \frac{1}{n}\sum m_i \times \frac{e(m_1, \ldots, m_n)}{\frac{1}{n}\sum m_i}$ (mean income $\times$ equity index).
- Slesnick decomposition (eq. 3): $V(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \min p^*(y_1 + \ldots + y_n)$ s.t. $W(u_1(y_1), \ldots, u_n(y_n)) \geq W(u_1(x_1), \ldots, u_n(x_n))$.
- Differential decomposition (eq. 5): $dW = \bar{\beta} \, pdX + \sum (\beta_i - \bar{\beta})(pdx_i - pdX/n)$ where $\beta_i$ is the social marginal value of money for $i$.
- Green accounting / NNP (eq. 15): $\delta Z(S_t) = C_t(S_t) + p_t \frac{dS_t}{dt}$ where $Z$ is discounted welfare, $S_t$ is the stock vector, $p_t$ are accounting prices.

**2. Equivalent income / equivalence approach (Section 4):**
- Money-metric utility: $e(p^*, u(x))$ -- minimum expenditure at reference prices $p^*$ to achieve utility level $u(x)$.
- Equivalent income: the income $m^*$ such that individual $i$ is indifferent between $(m_i, z_i)$ and $(m^*, z^*)$ where $z^*$ is the reference vector for non-income dimensions.
- Axiomatic foundation from fair allocation theory (Fleurbaey 2005): maximin/leximin on "healthy-equivalent incomes" $I^*$ where $I^*$ makes agent $i$ indifferent between current situation and perfect health with income $I^*$.
- Key axioms: (1) Ordinalism retained, (2) Independence weakened, (3) Weak Pareto retained, (4) Pigou-Dalton fairness axiom added. Result: maximin/leximin over equivalent incomes is the social ordering.
- WTP decomposition (eq. on p. 1051): $WTP_i = dm_i + \frac{1}{\partial u_i / \partial m_i} \nabla_z u_i \, dz_i$, and $dW = \bar{\beta} \sum WTP_i + \sum (\beta_i - \bar{\beta})(WTP_i - \frac{1}{n}\sum WTP_j)$.

**3. Happiness / Subjective well-being (Section 6):**
- Welfarist vs. liberal conceptions of SWB
- Hedonic treadmill vs. aspiration treadmill
- Easterlin paradox and its interpretations
- Model: $S(H, F)$ where $H$ = hedonic states, $F$ = functionings. Equivalence approach transforms $S$ into $U(H, F)$ measured in income units, eliminating the satisfaction treadmill.

**4. Capabilities (Section 7):**
- Functionings (achievements) vs. capabilities (opportunity sets)
- Index problem: dominance principle vs. nonpaternalism are incompatible (Figure 1)
- Equivalent income resolves this: it respects preferences while providing a complete ranking

# Key objects

- **Equivalent income ($m^*$ or $I^*$):** Income at reference non-income conditions that yields the same welfare as actual situation. Central welfare metric advocated by the paper.
- **Money-metric utility $e(p^*, u(x))$:** Expenditure function evaluated at reference prices -- the monetary equivalent of welfare.
- **$\beta_i$:** Social marginal value of money for individual $i$ -- determines social weight in welfare decompositions.
- **Accounting prices $p_t = \nabla Z(S_t)$:** Shadow prices of capital stocks for green accounting.
- **WTP (willingness-to-pay):** Equivalent variation for changes in income and non-income dimensions.

# Treatment of preferences

This is a central theme. The paper distinguishes three positions:
1. **Welfarist:** Preferences matter only through utility/satisfaction; interpersonal comparisons via hedonic utility or satisfaction levels.
2. **Liberal (resourcist/equivalence):** Individual preferences should be respected as sovereign guides to what individuals value; interpersonal comparisons via equivalent incomes that respect each individual's own ranking. No paternalism: "obtaining what one wants" rather than "being satisfied."
3. **Capabilities:** Preferences determine what individuals can do with their resources; interpersonal comparisons via capability sets or refined functionings.

The paper argues that the equivalence approach best handles the "adaptation" problem: satisfaction adapts to circumstances (hedonic/aspiration treadmill), making SWB an unreliable welfare metric, while equivalent income is immune to adaptation because it is based on ordinal preferences over life circumstances, not satisfaction levels.

Key insight on preferences (Section 6.3): the liberal approach distinguishes "obtaining what one wants" from "being satisfied." If an individual adapts their aspirations but would still prefer better circumstances if given the choice, the equivalence approach correctly reflects this preference while SWB would miss it.

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

The paper discusses opportunities primarily in the capabilities section (Section 7). Key points:
- Sen proposes capabilities (opportunity sets) as the metric of advantage
- The dominance principle (more capability is better) conflicts with nonpaternalism (respect individual preferences) when preferences differ (Figure 1, p. 1066)
- The egalitarian-equivalent approach resolves this by evaluating opportunities through each individual's own preferences
- Equivalent income can be computed as the income that would yield the same welfare if all non-income dimensions were at the reference level -- effectively converting opportunity differences into income equivalents

For labour supply: "considering the risk of unemployment, it is natural to compute the certainty equivalent income by deducting a risk premium from ordinary income. Considering leisure, [...] a simple option is to take a reference quantity of labor" (p. 1052), with footnote 58 listing three options for the reference: (1) tax-free equivalent budget at reference wage, (2) equivalent budget at market wage for unskilled work, (3) leisurely equivalent income at zero net wage.

# Welfare / normative object

The paper advocates for social welfare measured as an aggregation (maximin or leximin) of individual equivalent incomes. The equivalent income for individual $i$ is:

$$m_i^* \text{ such that } u_i(m_i^*, z^*) = u_i(m_i, z_i)$$

where $z^*$ is the reference vector of non-income dimensions (health, leisure, etc.) and $z_i$ is $i$'s actual non-income situation. Social welfare is then $W = f(m_1^*, \ldots, m_n^*)$ where $f$ embodies inequality aversion (maximin/leximin justified axiomatically from fair allocation theory).

# Main findings

1. **Corrected GDP is not well-founded (Sections 2--3):** The theoretical justification for GDP-like monetary aggregates as welfare measures requires either optimal redistribution (unrealistic), a representative agent (discredited), or identical preferences (restrictive). Decompositions into efficiency and equity terms are possible but require welfare functions. NNP/green accounting is theoretically elegant but practically intractable (requires accounting prices for all capital stocks).

2. **Equivalent income is the most promising monetary approach (Section 4):** Money-metric utilities and equivalent incomes, grounded in the theory of fair allocation, can provide rigorous interpersonal comparisons across individuals with different preferences. The approach is axiomatically justified (Section 4.3): weakening Independence and adding a fairness axiom yields maximin/leximin over equivalent incomes as the unique social ordering.

3. **Happiness is informative but insufficient (Section 6):** SWB is measurable but normatively problematic due to adaptation (hedonic/aspiration treadmills), social comparisons, and the distinction between hedonic utility and life satisfaction. The liberal approach argues that "obtaining what one wants" matters more than "being satisfied." SWB data are useful as inputs for estimating preferences but should not be the welfare metric itself.

4. **Capabilities need an index (Section 7):** The capability approach provides valuable flexibility but faces an unsolved indexing problem. The dominance principle and nonpaternalism are incompatible (Figure 1). The egalitarian-equivalent approach offers a resolution: it evaluates opportunities through individual preferences, providing a complete ranking without paternalism.

5. **Three serious alternatives to GDP (Section 8):** (1) Corrected GDP with equivalent income adjustments for non-market goods, (2) Subjective well-being indicators (with attention to adaptation), (3) Capability-based indicators. Synthetic indicators (HDI) are inferior because they are not individualistic and offer no guide to weights. The three approaches share substantial common ground and may converge.

6. **Key convergence insight (Section 8):** The equivalence approach can incorporate any dimension that the capabilities or happiness approaches care about -- health, leisure, social relations, environment -- as elements of $z$ in the equivalent income computation. It adds the ability to respect individual preferences about the relative importance of these dimensions.

# Main limitations

- Survey paper, no original theoretical results or empirical application
- The equivalent income approach requires estimating individual preferences over multiple life dimensions, which is data-demanding
- The choice of reference vector $z^*$ affects the ordering (reference-dependence)
- Intertemporal extension is difficult (sustainability analysis remains largely separate)
- The paper does not address dynamic preferences, habit formation, or preference endogeneity in detail
- Does not consider labour supply or job quality as a dimension of well-being in detail

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing
This is the key survey paper establishing the theoretical case for equivalent income as the welfare metric. The $W(z, R, A; y)$ framework can be understood as implementing the equivalent income approach in a labour supply context: $z$ = realized bundle (income, leisure), $R$ = preferences, $A$ = feasible job set (non-income dimension / opportunity), $y$ = pay schedule. Equivalent income at reference opportunity set $\tilde{A}$ and reference pay schedule $\tilde{y}$ provides the welfare metric.

## possible use for welfare metric justification
The paper's axiomatic argument (Section 4.3) -- that weakening Independence and adding a Pigou-Dalton fairness axiom yields maximin/leximin over egalitarian-equivalent incomes -- provides the normative foundation for the welfare metric in my JMP. The three options for the reference quantity of labour (footnote 58) map directly to different specifications of the equivalent income in the RURO framework.

## possible use for positioning relative to happiness and capabilities
The paper's critique of SWB (Section 6: adaptation, treadmills, liberal vs. welfarist) and capabilities (Section 7: indexing problem) justifies why a structural preference-based approach (RURO + equivalent income) is preferable to either alternative. The equivalent income approach resolves the indexing problem that plagues capabilities by using individual preferences as weights.

## possible use for non-income dimensions
The paper argues that equivalent income should incorporate non-income dimensions of well-being (health, leisure, unemployment risk, household size). In the RURO framework, the key non-income dimensions are: (1) leisure/hours, (2) job availability (the $A$ component), (3) working conditions. The equivalent income computation should account for differences in all of these.

# Research questions this paper inspires

1. How should the reference vector $z^*$ be chosen in the labour supply context? The paper suggests three options (footnote 58): (a) tax-free budget at reference wage, (b) budget at unskilled wage, (c) zero-wage leisurely income. Each gives a different equivalent income and potentially different policy rankings.

2. Can equivalent income be decomposed into opportunity and preference components? The paper discusses equivalent incomes and fair allocation separately. Combining them (as in Bargain et al. 2013) enables the decomposition: how much of an individual's equivalent income shortfall is due to limited opportunities ($A$) vs. preferences ($R$)?

3. The paper argues that the dominance principle and nonpaternalism are incompatible for capabilities (Section 7.2, Figure 1). Does this tension arise in the RURO framework when comparing individuals with different opportunity densities $g(h,w)$?

# Challenge to this paper

The paper strongly advocates for the equivalence approach but acknowledges (Section 4.4) several criticisms: (1) money-metric utilities may not be quasi-concave in resources, so the Pigou-Dalton principle can be violated; (2) the reference vector $z^*$ affects the ordering; (3) estimating individual preferences is demanding. For labour supply, criticism (1) is potentially serious: if a Pigou-Dalton transfer reduces both workers' equivalent incomes (because the donor values leisure more at lower income), the maximin criterion might reject the transfer even though it reduces inequality in market income. The theory of fair allocation shows that maximin/leximin are the only acceptable aggregators (Section 4.3, p. 1053), but this result depends on the axiom that equivalent incomes are the right individual welfare measure -- which is precisely what the quasi-concavity criticism questions.

Moreover, the paper's argument that SWB should not be used as the welfare metric (because of adaptation) implicitly assumes that "deep preferences" (what people would choose with full information) are the right basis for welfare. But if deep preferences are unobservable and must be estimated from observed choices, the structural model used for estimation (e.g., the RURO model) determines the welfare metric. The welfare metric is then only as good as the structural model -- a point the paper does not emphasize sufficiently.

# Relation to my jobs_and_wellbeing framework

[Explicit in paper] The paper establishes the theoretical case for equivalent income as the welfare metric. Equivalent income = income at reference non-income conditions that yields the same welfare. In $W(z, R, A; y)$, this translates to: the income at reference opportunity set $\tilde{A}$ and reference pay schedule $\tilde{y}$ that makes agent $i$ indifferent to their actual situation.

[Reasonable inference for my project] The paper's three options for the reference quantity of labour (footnote 58, p. 1052) map to different specifications of the RURO equivalent income: (1) tax-free budget at reference wage = equivalent income if the tax schedule were replaced by no taxes and the wage were set to a reference level; (2) budget at unskilled wage = equivalent income at the wage any unskilled worker can earn; (3) zero-wage leisurely income = the transfer income needed to make the agent as well off as they are with their current job. Option (3) is closest to the "rent from working" metric in Bargain et al. (2013).

[Unclear from paper] How the equivalence approach handles the endogeneity of opportunity sets. If $A_i$ depends on $i$'s choices (e.g., human capital investment, job search effort), then the opportunity set is partly a function of preferences and past choices. The clean $R$-$A$ separation assumed in the fairness theory may not hold in a dynamic setting.

The paper is closest to: **the theoretical foundation for equivalent income as a welfare metric** and **a roadmap for constructing alternatives to GDP using the equivalence approach**.

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

Bargain et al. (2013) directly implement the equivalent income approach advocated in this survey. Their three welfare metrics correspond to different choices of reference in the equivalence framework: $W^{eq}$ uses a reference wage, $W^{ce}$ uses reference preferences, $W^{rent}$ uses reference leisure (no work). This survey provides the theoretical justification for why these metrics are well-founded (Section 4.3), why they are superior to SWB or raw income as welfare measures (Sections 6--7), and what the key choices and limitations are (Section 4.4).

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

The paper is centrally about the distinction between what people have (resources, opportunities, capabilities) and what they want (preferences, values, satisfaction). The equivalence approach mediates between these by using preferences to evaluate opportunities: equivalent income = the income that, with reference non-income conditions, would provide the same preference satisfaction as the actual situation. This means that both $R$ (preferences) and $A$ (opportunities) enter the welfare metric, but $R$ determines the weights while $A$ determines the feasible set.

The paper's discussion of the dominance-nonpaternalism tension (Section 7.2) is directly relevant: the RURO framework resolves this by using individual preferences ($v(C, T-h)$) to evaluate individual opportunity sets ($g(h,w)$), producing equivalent incomes that respect both dominance (more opportunities = better, for given preferences) and nonpaternalism (individuals rank their own situations).

# Useful quotations / formulas

**On equivalent income (p. 1052):**
"One can compute the equivalent income that would give the member of a household the same individual satisfaction if he belonged to a reference type of household (e.g., if he were single)."

**On the reference for leisure (footnote 58, p. 1052):**
"A simple option is to take a reference quantity of labor. In order to compare the standard of living across OECD countries, Fleurbaey and Gaulier (forthcoming) have used the equivalent approach [...] for the corrections concerning leisure, health, the risk of unemployment, and household size."

**Three equivalent income options for labour (footnote 58):**
"One option consists in computing a tax-free equivalent budget [...] Another option consists in computing an equivalent budget at the market wage rate for unskilled work. A third option involves computing an equivalent budget corresponding to a zero net wage rate (with monotonic preferences, this boils down to computing a leisurely equivalent income)."

**On why maximin/leximin (p. 1053):**
"Any approach that obeys the Pareto principle and that evaluates individual situations by the indifference sets at the contemplated allocation [...] will fail to be quasi-concave unless it gives absolute priority to the worse off. The literature on fair allocation deduces from this observation that the maximin and leximin criteria are the only acceptable aggregators."

**On happiness vs. equivalence (Section 6.3, p. 1060):**
"The key objection from the liberal approach against satisfaction welfarism, perhaps, is that satisfaction welfarism fails to distinguish 'obtaining what one wants' from 'being satisfied.'"

**On convergence of approaches (p. 1070):**
"Serious alternatives to GDP are around the corner."

# My quick takeaway

This is the master survey connecting the dots between social choice theory, fair allocation, happiness research, capabilities, and the practical project of measuring welfare beyond GDP. The paper's central argument -- that equivalent income (grounded in fair allocation theory) is the most promising approach -- directly motivates my JMP's welfare metric. The equivalent income framework respects individual preferences (unlike capabilities), is immune to adaptation (unlike happiness), and provides a complete individualistic ordering (unlike GDP). For my $W(z, R, A; y)$ framework, the paper establishes that: (1) the welfare metric should be equivalent income at a reference opportunity-tax combination, (2) the aggregator should be maximin/leximin (axiomatically justified), (3) non-income dimensions (job availability, hours constraints = the $A$-component) should be incorporated via willingness-to-pay adjustments, and (4) the structural model (RURO) provides the preference estimates needed to compute these metrics. The three reference options for labour (footnote 58) give three versions of the JMP welfare metric.
