# Full citation

Aaberge, R., Colombino, U., & Wennemo, T. (2009). Evaluating Alternative Representations of the Choice Sets in Models of Labor Supply. *Journal of Economic Surveys*, 23(3), 586--612.

# One-sentence contribution

Compares 16 discrete-choice labour supply model variants — crossing fixed vs. sampled alternatives, number of alternatives, and treatment of heterogeneous job availability — and shows that choice-set specification has negligible effects on in-sample fit but dramatic effects on out-of-sample policy predictions, with models that incorporate opportunity-density features (job dummies and hours-peaks dummies) producing far more accurate simulations.

# Why this paper matters

Discrete-choice labour supply models (van Soest 1995 style) are widely used for tax-reform microsimulation, but the literature lacks systematic guidance on how choice-set specification affects results. This paper provides the first controlled comparison using a known data-generating process (the RURO model with opportunity densities), showing that the standard practice of using a small number of fixed alternatives without accounting for job availability can produce prediction errors exceeding 50% in policy simulations, even when in-sample fit is excellent. The results provide a strong empirical case for the RURO framework over simpler discrete-choice alternatives.

# Core research question

How sensitive are labour supply elasticities and tax-reform simulations to the specification of the choice set in discrete-choice models? Specifically, does it matter whether one uses (i) fixed vs. sampled alternatives, (ii) few (6) vs. many (24) alternatives, and (iii) whether one accounts for heterogeneous job availability through "job dummies" and "peaks dummies"?

# Economic setting and context

Norway 1995: a context with moderate female labour force participation (~75%), flexible part-time availability, progressive taxation with top MTR ~49.5%. The institutional setting features both part-time and full-time peaks in the hours distribution, reflecting the opportunity structure of the Norwegian labour market.

# Model / theoretical framework

The paper uses a **controlled experiment** design. The "true" data-generating process is the RURO model (Dagsvik 1994; Aaberge et al. 1999) estimated on Norwegian data, which generates labour supply through the interaction of preferences and opportunity densities. Sixteen alternative discrete-choice models are then estimated on data generated by this true model, and their out-of-sample prediction accuracy is compared.

**True model (RURO):** A household chooses a job from a continuum of latent opportunities, each characterized by hours $h$, wage $w$, and non-pecuniary attributes. The opportunity density $p(h, w)$ has peaks at part-time (910--1066 annual hours) and full-time (1898--2106 annual hours), capturing the institutional structure of job availability.

**Choice probability (true model):**
$$\varphi(h, w) = \frac{v[f(wh, I), h] \cdot p(h, w)}{\int\int v[f(xy, I), y] \cdot p(y, x) \, dx \, dy}$$

**Alternative models:** 16 variants crossing:
- **Alternatives:** 6 fixed, 24 fixed, 6 sampled, 24 sampled
- **Job availability:** (a) no correction, (b) job dummy $d_0$ only, (c) peaks dummies $d_1, d_2$ only, (d) both job dummy and peaks dummies

**Framework:** Positive (prediction accuracy), with implications for normative (welfare evaluation) applications.

# Key objects

- **Opportunity density** $p(h, w) = p^0 \cdot g_1(h) \cdot g_2(w|h)$: decomposes into scale ($p^0$, ratio of market to non-market opportunities), hours density ($g_1$ with peaks at part-time and full-time), and conditional wage density ($g_2$, log-normal).
- **Job dummy** $d_0$: a dummy variable equal to 1 for market work ($h > 0$), capturing the overall probability of having a market job vs. non-participation — an approximation to $\log p^0$.
- **Peaks dummies** $d_1, d_2$: dummies for part-time hours (910--1066) and full-time hours (1898--2106), approximating the peaks in $g_1(h)$.
- **Prediction error:** $\Delta_k = |\bar{h}_k^{\text{predicted}} - \bar{h}_k^{\text{true}}|$ for decile $k$ under a counterfactual flat-tax reform.

# Data

Norwegian 1995 data from Statistics Norway: 1,842 married/cohabiting women aged 20--62. Administrative tax records merged with survey data. The "true" model is estimated on this data, and then 100 Monte Carlo samples of size 1,842 are generated from the estimated true model for the comparison exercise.

# Identification logic

The paper does not estimate a new model for identification purposes. Rather, it uses the **already-identified** RURO model (from Aaberge & Colombino 2006) as the data-generating process and asks: can simpler models recover the true model's predictions? Identification in the true model follows the standard RURO approach: utility parameters identified from tax-function non-linearities; opportunity density parameters identified from hours/wage distributions and participation rates.

# Estimation / empirical strategy

1. Estimate the true RURO model on Norwegian 1995 data (parameters from Aaberge & Colombino 2006).
2. Generate 100 Monte Carlo samples of 1,842 observations from the true model.
3. Estimate 16 alternative discrete-choice models on each sample.
4. For each estimated model, simulate labour supply under (a) the 1995 tax system (in-sample) and (b) a revenue-neutral flat tax at 25% (out-of-sample).
5. Compare predicted hours distributions (by income decile) against the true model's predictions.
6. Regress prediction accuracy on model features (number of alternatives, fixed vs. sampled, job dummy, peaks dummies) to identify which features matter most.

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled via a Box-Cox utility function over disposable income (consumption) and leisure, with taste-shifters for age and children. The functional form is the same across all 16 model variants — the comparison isolates the effect of choice-set specification, not preference specification. Preferences are heterogeneous through observed demographics and the random taste component $\varepsilon$ (Type I extreme value).

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

**This is the paper's central concern.**

The true model has explicit opportunity densities $p(h, w)$ with:
- $p^0 < 1$: rationing (not all desired jobs are available)
- $g_1(h)$: hours distribution with peaks at part-time and full-time
- $g_2(w|h)$: log-normal conditional wage distribution

The 16 alternative models approximate these features to varying degrees:
- **No correction (type a):** Ignores heterogeneous job availability entirely — the standard van Soest approach
- **Job dummy only (type b):** Adds $d_0$ to utility, capturing the overall market vs. non-market opportunity ratio — approximates $\log p^0$
- **Peaks dummies only (type c):** Adds $d_1, d_2$ for part-time/full-time hours, approximating the shape of $g_1(h)$
- **Both (type d):** Full approximation with $d_0, d_1, d_2$ — closest to the true model

**Key finding:** Only models with both job dummy and peaks dummies (type d) produce accurate out-of-sample predictions. The job dummy alone is insufficient; the peaks dummies alone are insufficient; both are needed.

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is primarily **positive** (prediction accuracy), but the implications are directly normative: if a model cannot accurately predict labour supply responses to tax reforms, it cannot be used for welfare evaluation. The paper does not compute welfare measures directly, but the flat-tax simulation exercise is the standard first step in the tax-reform evaluation pipeline used in Aaberge & Colombino (2013) and related papers.

# Main findings

**In-sample fit (Tables 4--7):**
- All 16 models fit the observed hours distribution similarly well under the 1995 tax system.
- Differences in log-likelihood are small and not systematically related to model features.
- "In-sample fit is an unreliable guide to model quality" (p. 604).

**Out-of-sample predictions (Tables 8--9, Figures 3--5):**
- Under the flat-tax reform, prediction errors vary dramatically across models.
- Models without job dummies or peaks dummies produce mean prediction errors of 30--55% (relative to the true model's predicted hours by decile).
- Models with both job dummy and peaks dummies (type d) reduce errors to 5--15%.
- The improvement from adding peaks dummies is larger than from adding job dummies alone.
- Sampled alternatives with 24 points + job dummy + peaks dummies (model S24d) is the best performer, with mean absolute prediction error < 5%.

**Regression analysis of prediction accuracy (Table 9):**
- "24 alternatives" reduces prediction error (significant).
- "Job dummy" alone has moderate effect.
- "Peaks dummies" alone have moderate effect.
- "24 alternatives $\times$ Job dummy" interaction is highly significant — the combination matters more than either alone.
- Sampled vs. fixed alternatives: sampled slightly better but not dramatically so when other features are included.

**Key mechanism:** The flat tax changes the budget constraint in ways that interact with the opportunity structure. Without peaks dummies, models incorrectly predict that women shift smoothly along the hours distribution; with peaks, the model correctly predicts bunching at institutionally prevalent hours levels. Without the job dummy, models overpredict participation responses.

# Main limitations

- The true model is itself a model — the comparison is against the RURO, not against actual reform outcomes.
- Only female labour supply is modeled (male hours treated as fixed).
- Norwegian-specific opportunity structure (strong part-time peak) may not generalize.
- Only one counterfactual (flat tax) is tested; other reforms might produce different rankings.
- The continuous RURO model is approximated by discrete alternatives — even the best discrete model is an approximation.
- No welfare measures are computed; the focus is entirely on labour supply prediction.

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing
The paper provides strong empirical evidence that choice-set specification matters enormously for policy predictions, even when in-sample fit is identical. This motivates using the full RURO framework (with opportunity densities) rather than simpler discrete-choice models when the goal is welfare evaluation under the $W(z, R, A; y)$ framework.

## possible use for model design
The comparison of 16 model variants provides a systematic guide for specifying the discrete-choice component of the labour supply model. The finding that "job dummy + peaks dummies + 24 sampled alternatives" is the best specification directly informs the empirical implementation of the RURO model in my framework.

## possible use for identification
The paper shows that identification of preference parameters is robust to choice-set specification (in-sample fit is similar), but identification of opportunity parameters requires explicit modeling of job availability features ($p^0$, peaks in $g_1(h)$). This means that for my framework — where $A$ is a key object — the choice-set specification is critical.

## possible use for decomposition
The paper implicitly demonstrates that the "opportunity" component ($A$) of the model carries most of the out-of-sample predictive content. Preferences ($R$) are well-identified regardless of choice-set specification, but the interaction of $R$ and $A$ through the opportunity density is what drives accurate policy predictions.

## possible use for comparative application
The Norwegian institutional context (with strong part-time peak) provides a specific case where the opportunity structure $A$ has clear institutional correlates. The finding that peaks dummies are crucial suggests that cross-country applications need country-specific opportunity density specifications.

# Research questions this paper inspires

1. If choice-set specification has dramatic effects on labour supply predictions, what are the implications for welfare evaluation? Does a model that mispredicts hours also mispredict equivalent income or the $W(z, R, A; y)$ measure?

2. Can the "job dummy + peaks dummies" approach be extended to capture richer features of the opportunity set — e.g., sector-specific availability, geographic variation, or skill-specific rationing?

3. How sensitive are the results to the assumption that the true model is RURO? If the true DGP has different features (e.g., search frictions, dynamic considerations), would the ranking of models change?

4. In countries with different institutional structures (e.g., Italy with no part-time peak, or the US with continuous hours flexibility), which choice-set features matter most?

# Challenge to this paper

The paper evaluates models against the RURO as the "true" data-generating process, but the RURO itself is a model with specific assumptions (Type I extreme value taste shocks, multiplicative separability of utility and opportunity density, specific functional forms for $g_1(h)$ and $g_2(w|h)$). If the true DGP is not RURO — for instance, if there are search frictions, fixed costs of work, or dynamic considerations — the ranking of model variants might differ. The paper's conclusion that "peaks dummies are essential" is conditional on the true model having peaks in the opportunity density; in a labour market without such institutional features, the ranking could reverse. The paper would be strengthened by validation against actual reform outcomes rather than against the RURO's own predictions.

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

The paper provides methodological foundations for the discrete-choice models used in Bargain et al. (2013). Bargain et al. use a van Soest-style discrete-choice model for welfare evaluation with heterogeneous preferences; this paper shows that the choice-set specification in such models (particularly the treatment of job availability via dummies) is crucial for out-of-sample prediction accuracy. The finding that models without opportunity-density features mispredict policy effects suggests that Bargain et al.'s welfare results could be sensitive to their choice-set specification — a point that Bargain et al. do not systematically investigate. However, Bargain et al.'s use of a "job dummy" (participation dummy) in the utility function partially addresses this concern.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

The paper provides the clearest empirical demonstration in this literature that **opportunities and preferences play distinct roles** in determining labour supply outcomes. Preferences ($R$) are well-identified regardless of how the choice set is specified — all 16 models produce similar in-sample fit. But opportunities ($A$, captured by the opportunity density) are essential for out-of-sample prediction. This asymmetry — preferences identified in-sample, opportunities identified out-of-sample — has profound implications for the $W(z, R, A; y)$ framework: it suggests that welfare evaluation (which requires out-of-sample counterfactuals) is more sensitive to the specification of $A$ than to the specification of $R$.
