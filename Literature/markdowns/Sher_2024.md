# Full citation

Sher, I. (2024). Generalized social marginal welfare weights imply inconsistent comparisons of tax policies. arXiv working paper, version dated June 25, 2024.

# One-sentence contribution

Proves that Saez--Stantcheva (2016) generalized social marginal welfare weights yield consistent tax policy comparisons if and only if they are structurally utilitarian (determined by utility levels), and otherwise imply social preference cycles over equal-revenue tax policies.

# Why this paper matters

This paper is a fundamental critique of the most influential recent methodological innovation in normative public economics. Saez & Stantcheva (2016) proposed that one can incorporate non-utilitarian ethical principles (libertarianism, poverty alleviation, equality of opportunity) into optimal tax formulas simply by replacing standard utilitarian welfare weights with "generalized" weights that depend on arbitrary functions of consumption, income, and characteristics. Sher shows this approach is incoherent unless the weights have a utilitarian structure. For my JMP, this is a strong argument for building welfare evaluation on an explicit global well-being measure $W(z, R, A; y)$ rather than relying on ad hoc local welfare weights.

# Core research question

Can generalized social marginal welfare weights provide a coherent way to evaluate tax policies while incorporating non-utilitarian ethical principles, without relying on a global social welfare objective?

# Economic setting and context

Stylized optimal income tax model with a continuum of agents $i \in [0,1]$, each with observable characteristics $x_i$ and unobservable characteristics $y_i$. Quasilinear utility: $U_i(c_i, z_i) = u(c_i - v_i(z_i))$ where $v_i(z_i)$ is the cost of earning income $z_i$. Tax policy $T(z, x)$ determines consumption $c_i = z_i - T_i(z_i)$. Agents choose income optimally. The paper generalises to non-quasilinear utility in Section 7.

# Model / theoretical framework

**Generalized social marginal welfare weights (GSMWW):** A function $g(c_i, z_i; x_i, y_i) > 0$ assigning marginal social value of consumption to each agent. Tax reform $\Delta T$ is locally desirable at $T$ if:
$$\int g_i(T) \Delta T_i(z_i(T)) \, di < 0$$

**Global improvement principle:** If, along a parameterised family of tax policies $(T^\theta)_{\theta \in \Theta}$, the welfare weights indicate improvement at every $\hat{\theta} \in [\theta_0, \theta_1]$, then $T^{\theta_0} \prec^g T^{\theta_1}$.

**Global indifference principle:** If welfare weights indicate zero net welfare change at every $\hat{\theta}$, then $T^{\theta_0} \sim^g T^{\theta_1}$.

**Structural utilitarianism (Definition 1):** Welfare weights $g$ are structurally utilitarian if: whenever $c_i - v_i(z_i) = c_i' - v_i(z_i')$ (same utility), then $g_i(c_i, z_i) = g_i(c_i', z_i')$ (same weight). Under quasilinear utility, this is equivalent to: $U_i(c_i, z_i) = U_i(c_i', z_i') \Rightarrow g_i(c_i, z_i) = g_i(c_i', z_i')$.

**Theorem 1:** Welfare weights are structurally utilitarian if and only if they arise from a generalized utilitarian social welfare function $W(T) = \int F_i(U_i(c_i(T), z_i(T))) \, di$, where $F_i$ is an agent-specific increasing transformation.

**Inconsistency Theorem (Theorem 3):** If welfare weights are NOT structurally utilitarian, there exist equal-revenue tax policies $T_0, T_1, T_2, T_3$ such that:
$$T_0 \prec^g T_1 \sim^g T_2 \prec^g T_3 \sim^g T_0$$
a social preference cycle.

# Key objects

- **Generalized welfare weights $g_i(c_i, z_i)$:** The Saez-Stantcheva innovation -- weights that can depend on outcomes, not just marginal utility.
- **Structural utilitarianism:** The necessary and sufficient condition for consistency. Weights must be determined by utility levels.
- **Illustrative inconsistent weights:** Libertarian weights $g_i = \hat{g}(z_i - c_i)$ (increasing in taxes paid); poverty-alleviation weights $g_i = 1$ if $c_i < \bar{c}$, else $0$; counterfactual weights depending on income relative to no-tax benchmark. All shown to be inconsistent.

None. Purely theoretical paper.

# Identification logic

Analytical: starts from local comparisons made by welfare weights, derives implied global comparisons via the global improvement and indifference principles, and proves that non-structurally-utilitarian weights generate cycles.

# Estimation / empirical strategy

None. Method is theorem-proof analysis with illustrative constructions.

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences represented through utility in the standard optimal-tax way. The paper does not study preference heterogeneity per se, but preference structure determines what a coherent welfare-weight system must look like. The key claim: if welfare weights vary in ways not anchored to utility structure, local comparisons cannot be integrated into a consistent global ranking.

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

No explicit modelling of opportunities. There are no feasible job sets, no latent job offers, no hours restrictions, no demand-side constraints. The feasible set is just the tax-constrained income-consumption choice problem. The paper is about the consistency of welfare evaluation, not about opportunity-set modelling.

# Welfare / normative object

The paper's normative object is the coherence of tax policy evaluation through generalized welfare weights. The central conclusion: one cannot dispense with a global social welfare objective and rely solely on local weighted assessments unless those weights arise from a generalized utilitarian SWF $\int F_i(U_i) \, di$. Prominent non-utilitarian weight systems (libertarian, poverty-alleviation, equality-of-opportunity) fall outside this class and are therefore inconsistent.

# Main findings

1. **Local comparisons imply global comparisons:** Welfare weights implicitly commit one to global social rankings $\prec^g$ and $\sim^g$ over tax policies, even though Saez & Stantcheva present them as purely local.

2. **Structural utilitarianism is necessary and sufficient for consistency:** Consistent weights are exactly those generated by $\int F_i(U_i) \, di$ (Theorem 1).

3. **Non-structurally-utilitarian weights are inconsistent:** They imply social preference cycles $T_0 \prec^g T_1 \sim^g T_2 \prec^g T_3 \sim^g T_0$ (Theorem 3).

4. **Prominent examples are inconsistent:** Libertarian weights (depending on taxes paid), poverty-alleviation weights (depending on consumption vs poverty line), and counterfactual weights all fall into the inconsistent class.

5. **The GSMWW approach adds nothing beyond generalized utilitarianism:** When consistent, it reduces to what Mirrlees (1971) already had -- a generalized utilitarian SWF with agent-specific transformations $F_i$.

# Main limitations

- Does not model labour market opportunities, job sets, or structural labour supply beyond abstract income choice.
- Negative and methodological: shows what does not work, but does not construct a complete non-utilitarian alternative.
- Stylised tax model: interaction with richer structural models (extensive margin, hours, occupations, latent offers) is unexplored.
- The result requires the global improvement and indifference principles, which some practitioners might reject.

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for normative architecture
Very high. The paper provides a strong argument for building welfare evaluation on an explicit global well-being measure $W(z, R, A; y)$ -- as in the equivalent income approach from Fleurbaey & Maniquet -- rather than encoding fairness concerns through ad hoc local welfare weights. My framework's use of equivalent income as a global welfare object avoids the Sher critique entirely, because equivalent income generates a complete social ordering from which local policy comparisons can be derived.

## possible use for positioning relative to Saez-Stantcheva
The paper helps position my JMP's normative approach. Saez & Stantcheva (2016) is a prominent alternative to the Fleurbaey-Maniquet approach: both try to go beyond standard utilitarianism, but Saez-Stantcheva does so through local weights while Fleurbaey-Maniquet does so through a global well-being measure (equivalent income). Sher shows the local-weights approach is fundamentally flawed. This supports my choice of the equivalent income framework.

# Research questions this paper inspires

1. Is the equivalent income approach immune to the Sher critique? Equivalent income generates a global welfare object, from which one can derive welfare weights. These derived weights are necessarily structurally utilitarian (they arise from a global SWF), so they are consistent by Theorem 1. But the equivalent income SWF is non-standard (it is not $\int F_i(U_i) \, di$ but rather maximin over equivalent incomes). Does the maximin aggregation remain consistent under the Sher framework?

2. Can one axiomatise conditions under which local comparisons across changes in opportunity sets are integrable into a consistent global social ordering? The Sher result is for tax policies; an analogue for opportunity-set comparisons would be relevant for the RURO framework.

# Challenge to this paper

The paper's result is compelling but its constructive implications are limited. It tells us that non-utilitarian local weights are inconsistent, but it does not address whether non-utilitarian global welfare measures (like equivalent income with maximin) can be consistently used. The equivalent income approach in Fleurbaey & Maniquet is explicitly non-utilitarian -- it does not maximise $\int F_i(U_i) \, di$ -- yet it provides a complete social ordering. Sher's framework, by restricting attention to generalized utilitarian SWFs, may be too narrow to capture the full space of coherent non-utilitarian welfare evaluation.

Not directly related. Bargain et al. use a standard maximin SWF over equivalent incomes, which is a global welfare object immune to the Sher critique (it does not rely on local welfare weights). The Sher paper indirectly supports Bargain et al.'s approach by showing that the alternative (Saez-Stantcheva weights) is incoherent.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

The paper does not distinguish opportunities from preferences. Its contribution is meta-methodological: if one wishes to construct a fairness-sensitive framework that distinguishes opportunities from preferences (as in my JMP), the distinction must be embedded in a globally coherent welfare object, not merely in locally assigned marginal weights.

# My quick takeaway

A powerful negative result showing that the Saez-Stantcheva approach of encoding non-utilitarian ethical values through local welfare weights is fundamentally incoherent: non-structurally-utilitarian weights imply social preference cycles over tax policies. For my JMP, this strongly supports the equivalent income approach (a global welfare object) over the welfare-weights approach. The paper's limitation is that it does not address whether non-utilitarian global measures (like equivalent income with maximin) can themselves be consistently used -- but since equivalent income generates a complete ordering, it is immune to the specific inconsistency Sher identifies.
