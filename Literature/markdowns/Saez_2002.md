# Full citation

Saez, E. (2002). Optimal Income Transfer Programs: Intensive versus Extensive Labor Supply Responses. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 117(3), 1039--1073.

# One-sentence contribution

Shows that the optimal shape of income transfer programs -- Negative Income Tax (NIT) with high guaranteed income and steep phaseout vs Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) with negative marginal rates at the bottom -- depends critically on whether behavioural responses are concentrated along the intensive margin (hours/effort) or the extensive margin (participation), deriving optimal tax formulas in terms of participation elasticities $\eta_i$, intensive elasticities $\zeta_i$, and marginal social welfare weights $g_i$.

# Why this paper matters

This paper fundamentally changed the optimal income tax literature by incorporating the extensive margin (participation decision) alongside the classical intensive margin (hours/effort). The key result -- that negative marginal tax rates (EITC-type subsidies) can be optimal when the extensive margin dominates -- overturned the classical Mirrlees (1971) result that marginal tax rates must be nonnegative. The paper's sufficient statistics approach ($\eta_i$, $\zeta_i$, $g_i$) became the template for modern optimal tax analysis, directly influencing Saez & Stantcheva (2016), Fleurbaey & Maniquet (2018), and the entire empirical optimal tax literature.

# Core research question

How does the nature of labour supply responses -- intensive (hours/effort adjustment) versus extensive (labour force participation) -- affect the shape of the optimal income transfer schedule, and specifically whether the optimal program resembles a NIT or an EITC?

# Economic setting and context

United States, late 1990s. The paper is motivated by the NIT-vs-EITC debate: traditional optimal tax theory (Mirrlees 1971) implies NIT-type programs with nonnegative marginal tax rates, but the US has shifted toward EITC-type programs with negative marginal rates at the bottom. Empirical evidence shows that participation elasticities are large for low-income groups (especially single mothers: $\eta \approx 0.5$--$1.0$) while intensive elasticities are small ($\epsilon \approx 0.25$). The paper resolves this tension by showing that incorporating the extensive margin can justify EITC-type subsidies.

Nonlabour force participation rate: ~14.2% (March 1997 CPS, adults 18--60, non-students).

# Model / theoretical framework

**General framework (Section II.1):** Discrete occupational choice model. $I + 1$ occupations: unemployment ($i = 0$, earnings $w_0 = 0$) and $I$ jobs paying $w_1 < \ldots < w_I$. Population normalised to one: $\sum_{i=0}^{I} h_i = 1$. Each $h_i$ depends on all after-tax rewards $(c_0, c_1, \ldots, c_I)$ where $c_i = w_i - T_i$. Government maximises weighted sum of utilities subject to budget constraint:

$$\sum_{i=0}^{I} h_i T_i = H$$

**Marginal social welfare weights** $g_i$: value in public funds of giving \$1 to occupation $i$. With no income effects: $\sum h_i g_i = 1$ (eq. 2).

**Three models:**

**1. Pure extensive model (Section II.2):** Binary participation decision. Each individual works in their skill-corresponding occupation or is unemployed. Participation elasticity:

$$\eta_i = \frac{c_i - c_0}{h_i} \cdot \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial(c_i - c_0)}$$

**Proposition 1:** At the optimum:

$$\frac{T_i - T_0}{c_i - c_0} = \frac{1}{\eta_i}(1 - g_i)$$

**2. Pure intensive model (Section II.3):** Each individual can only choose between adjacent occupations $i$ and $i-1$. Mobility elasticity:

$$\zeta_i = \frac{c_i - c_{i-1}}{h_i} \cdot \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial(c_i - c_{i-1})}$$

**Proposition 2:** At the optimum:

$$\frac{T_i - T_{i-1}}{c_i - c_{i-1}} = \frac{1}{\zeta_i} \left[\frac{(1-g_i)h_i + (1-g_{i+1})h_{i+1} + \ldots + (1-g_I)h_I}{h_i}\right]$$

Key result: marginal tax rates are **never negative** in the intensive model (when $g_i$ are decreasing).

**3. Mixed model (Section II.4):** Combines extensive and intensive responses. Each individual can choose among three occupations: $0$, $i$, and $i+1$.

$$\frac{T_i - T_{i-1}}{c_i - c_{i-1}} = \frac{1}{\zeta_i h_i} \sum_{j=i}^{I} h_j \left[1 - g_j - \eta_j \frac{T_j - T_0}{c_j - c_0}\right]$$

The effective weights become $\hat{g}_j = g_j + \eta_j(T_j - T_0)/(c_j - c_0)$. Adding participation raises the effective weight on groups with large $T_j - T_0$ (those receiving more than the unemployed), potentially making $\hat{g}_j > 1$ and producing negative marginal rates.

# Key objects

- **$\eta_i$:** Participation elasticity for occupation $i$ (fraction leaving labour force when $c_i - c_0$ decreases by 1%)
- **$\zeta_i$:** Intensive/mobility elasticity for occupation $i$ (fraction switching from job $i$ to $i-1$ when $c_i - c_{i-1}$ decreases by 1%)
- **$g_i$:** Marginal social welfare weight for occupation $i$ (value in public funds of \$1 to individual in occupation $i$)
- **$g_0$:** Weight on the unemployed -- the critical parameter determining NIT vs EITC. $g_0 > g_1 \Rightarrow$ NIT; $g_0 < g_1 \Rightarrow$ EITC
- **EITC "bubble" (Figure IIa):** The region where $c_i > c_0 + (w_i - w_0)$ -- working poor receive more than the unemployed
- **$\nu$:** Redistributive tastes parameter; $g(c) = 1/(p \cdot c^\nu)$; $\nu = 0$ utilitarian, $\nu \to \infty$ Rawlsian

# Data

March 1997 Current Population Survey (CPS). Annual earnings for 1996. Non-student individuals aged 18--60. Earnings = total wage + self-employment income. 14.2% nonparticipation rate. 17 discrete income levels from \$0 to \$100,000 (Table III). Distribution calibrated to match empirical earnings CDF.

# Identification logic

Not an empirical identification paper. The optimal tax formulas are derived analytically and expressed in terms of sufficient statistics ($\eta_i$, $\zeta_i$, $g_i$). The simulations calibrate these elasticities from the empirical literature rather than estimating them. The key identification insight: the margin of response (extensive vs intensive) is a sufficient statistic for optimal tax design -- the detailed structure of individual utility functions is irrelevant.

# Estimation / empirical strategy

Calibrated numerical simulations using:
1. **Earnings distribution:** 17-point discrete grid from CPS (Table III)
2. **Participation elasticity $\eta$:** 0, 0.5, or 1 for incomes below \$20,000; 0 above
3. **Intensive elasticity:** $\epsilon_L = 0$, 0.25, or 0.5 for low incomes; $\epsilon_H = 0.25$ or 0.5 for high incomes
4. **Redistributive parameter $\nu$:** 0.25, 1, or 4
5. **Government revenue $H$:** \$5,000 per capita
6. **No income effects**

Results presented in Tables I--II and Figure IV. Each simulation solves $I + 2$ simultaneous equations for optimal tax levels.

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are heterogeneous within each skill level but are not explicitly modelled. The sufficient statistics approach bypasses individual utility functions: the aggregate behavioural response functions $h_i(c_0, \ldots, c_I)$ and their derivatives (elasticities) are the only preference-related inputs. The welfare function is parameterised by marginal weights $g_i$ rather than by individual utilities.

The paper acknowledges that $g_0$ (the weight on the unemployed) embeds a normative judgment about voluntary vs involuntary unemployment: "Whether the unemployed really cannot work or are lazy is of critical importance to assess whether they deserve to get transfers" (p. 1046). Liberals set $g_0 > g_1$ (unemployed are needy); conservatives set $g_0 < g_1$ (unemployed are lazy). The optimal program is highly sensitive to this judgment.

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

**Not explicitly modelled.** Labour supply responses are captured by reduced-form elasticities $\eta_i$ and $\zeta_i$. The paper notes that "the extensive elasticity is not an intrinsic parameter but depends on the institutional features of the labor market" (p. 1043) -- if employers offer more flexible hours, the extensive margin shrinks and the intensive margin expands. But the model does not distinguish voluntary from involuntary unemployment: all nonparticipants are pooled in occupation 0, regardless of whether they choose not to work or cannot find a job.

The paper also notes (p. 1043) that the time period matters: over a lifetime, almost everyone participates (extensive margin irrelevant, intensive dominates); at daily frequency, participation is the main margin.

# Welfare / normative object

Weighted utilitarian SWF:

$$W = \int_M \mu^m u^m(w_{i^*} - T_{i^*}, i^*) \, d\nu(m)$$

where $\mu^m$ are Pareto weights. The paper works directly with the marginal social welfare weights $g_i$ (eq. 10):

$$g_i = \frac{1}{ph_i} \int_{M_i} \mu^m \frac{\partial u^m(c_i, i^*)}{\partial c_i} \, d\nu(m)$$

For simulations: $g_i = g(c_i)/p = 1/(p \cdot c_i^\nu)$, a decreasing function of disposable income. No equivalent income or money-metric utility -- this is a standard welfarist approach.

# Main findings

1. **Pure extensive model: negative marginal tax rates are optimal when $g_0 < g_1$ (Proposition 1).** When the government values giving \$1 to low-wage workers more than to the unemployed, it is optimal to set $T_i < T_0$ for low $i$ (working poor receive larger transfers than unemployed). This produces the EITC "bubble" with negative marginal rates at the bottom.

2. **Pure intensive model: marginal tax rates are always nonnegative (Proposition 2).** The classical Mirrlees result. Negative rates would create incentive distortions at the intensive margin without any offsetting participation benefit.

3. **Mixed model: the extensive margin generates effective weights $\hat{g}_j = g_j + \eta_j(T_j - T_0)/(c_j - c_0)$ (eq. 8).** The participation margin raises the effective social weight on groups receiving more than the unemployed. When $\eta$ is large relative to $\zeta$, negative marginal rates can be optimal even when $g_i$ are decreasing (government values unemployed more than working poor).

4. **Numerical simulations (Tables I--II, Figure IV):**
   - **Benchmark case** ($\eta = 0.5$, $\epsilon_L = \epsilon_H = 0.25$, $\nu = 1$): Guaranteed income ~\$7,300. Modest tax rate (~10%) on first \$4,000. High phaseout rate (~60%) from \$4,000--\$15,000. Tax rate ~50% for middle/high incomes. Unemployment rate ~14%.
   - **$\eta = 0$:** Large NIT with guaranteed income ~\$9,900 and 70% phaseout rate.
   - **$\eta = 1$:** EITC-like: guaranteed income ~\$4,500, negative rates at bottom, high phaseout.
   - **$\nu = 4$ (Rawlsian):** EITC bubble disappears: large NIT (\$12,900) with 80% phaseout.
   - **$\nu = 0.25$ (mild redistribution):** Small guaranteed income (<\$2,000), small EITC effect.

5. **The optimal program is very sensitive to the participation elasticity.** When $\eta = 0$, a NIT is always optimal. When $\eta$ is large (e.g., 1), the optimal program shifts toward EITC with negative marginal rates and smaller guaranteed income.

6. **US policy interpretation (Section III):** The combined EITC + TANF + Food Stamps system for single mothers is close to the optimal simulated schedule when $\eta = 0.5$, $\epsilon = 0.25$, $\nu = 1$. This provides a positive justification for the US welfare-to-work approach for groups with high participation elasticities.

# Main limitations

- Individual taxation only: ignores secondary earner decisions (acknowledged as important extension)
- No demand-side constraints: all nonparticipation is potentially voluntary
- Static model: no dynamics, human capital scarring from unemployment, or job search
- No income effects (simplification for tractability)
- Constant elasticities across the income distribution (except low vs high split)
- $g_0$ (weight on unemployed) is a normative parameter, not identified from data -- but the optimal program is very sensitive to it
- Simulations are illustrative, not precise policy recommendations
- Perfect substitution of labour types (no general equilibrium wage effects)

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for the theoretical framework linking margins of response to program design
The paper's central insight -- that the extensive margin justifies EITC-type subsidies while the intensive margin justifies NIT-type programs -- is directly relevant to my RURO framework. In the RURO model, participation decisions are driven by both preferences and opportunity constraints ($\theta$, $g(h,w)$). The participation elasticity $\eta_i$ in Saez's model confounds voluntary non-participation (preferences) with involuntary non-participation (no jobs available). The RURO framework can decompose $\eta_i$ into a preference component and an opportunity component, leading to different optimal tax implications.

## possible use for the treatment of $g_0$ and the deserving/undeserving poor
The paper's discussion of $g_0$ (pp. 1045--1046) -- whether the unemployed "deserve" transfers -- maps directly to the RURO decomposition. If non-participation is involuntary (demand-side constraint), the agent deserves compensation (high $g_0$); if voluntary (preference-driven), the laissez-faire principle may imply lower $g_0$. The RURO opportunity density provides the empirical basis for this decomposition.

## possible use for the sufficient statistics approach
The paper's sufficient statistics approach ($\eta$, $\zeta$, $g$) is widely used in the literature. My JMP extends this by adding the opportunity parameters ($\theta$, $g(h)$) as additional sufficient statistics. The RURO framework shows that the same reduced-form elasticity $\eta$ can correspond to different structural decompositions (preference-driven vs opportunity-driven participation), and the optimal tax depends on the decomposition.

# Research questions this paper inspires

1. How would the optimal tax formula change if $\eta_i$ were decomposed into a preference component (agents who choose not to work because $c_i - c_0$ is too low) and an opportunity component (agents who cannot find a job at wage $w_i$)? The mechanical effect of $dT_i$ on welfare would differ: for involuntary non-participants, the welfare effect of lowering $T_i$ is zero (they can't take the job anyway), so the EITC result may be weakened.

2. The paper treats the extensive elasticity as a fixed parameter. In the RURO framework, the effective participation elasticity depends on the opportunity density: if many non-participants face no job offers ($\theta = 0$), the effective $\eta$ is zero regardless of preferences. How does this affect the NIT-vs-EITC comparison?

3. The paper notes that the extensive elasticity "depends on the institutional features of the labor market" (p. 1043). Can the RURO opportunity density $g(h,w)$ provide a structural model of these institutional features?

# Challenge to this paper

The paper's optimal tax formulas are derived under the assumption that all labour supply responses are voluntary: agents choose between occupations based on relative after-tax rewards. If some non-participation is involuntary (agents cannot find jobs at their skill level), two problems arise: (1) the participation elasticity $\eta_i$ overestimates the true behavioural response to tax changes, because some non-participants would not respond even with higher $c_i - c_0$; (2) the welfare weight $g_0$ conflates genuinely needy (disabled, demand-constrained) with voluntarily idle agents, making the normative judgment about "deserving poor" inseparable from the empirical question of opportunity availability. The RURO framework resolves both issues by separating the opportunity density from preferences.

# Relation to my jobs_and_wellbeing framework

[Explicit in paper] The paper models the $y \to z$ mapping: how the tax schedule $(T_0, \ldots, T_I)$ affects labour supply distributions $h_i$. The welfare function $W$ aggregates weighted individual utilities. $R$ (preferences) is captured by the utility functions $u^m$; $z$ (realised bundle) is the occupation-income pair $(i^*, c_{i^*})$; $y$ (tax schedule) is $(T_0, \ldots, T_I)$. $A$ (opportunities) is not modelled: all $I + 1$ occupations are available to all agents.

[Reasonable inference for my project] The participation elasticity $\eta_i$ in Saez's model is the aggregate behavioural response at the extensive margin. In my framework, this elasticity confounds the preference response (agents re-optimising given opportunities) and the opportunity response (agents unable to take up the EITC because no suitable jobs exist). The RURO decomposition would produce a preference-based $\eta_i^R$ and an opportunity-based $\eta_i^A$, and the optimal tax formula would need to account for both.

[Unclear from paper] Whether the EITC result survives when opportunities are explicitly modelled. If many low-income non-participants face demand constraints ($\theta \approx 0$), the effective participation elasticity may be near zero, and the optimal program would revert to an NIT regardless of preference-based participation responses.

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

Bargain et al. (2013) use structural discrete-choice models to compute welfare metrics (equivalent income) for tax reform evaluation. Saez (2002) takes a reduced-form sufficient statistics approach. The two are complementary: Saez derives optimal tax formulas in terms of elasticities and weights; Bargain et al. compute the welfare effects of specific reforms using estimated preference parameters. My JMP extends both by incorporating the RURO opportunity decomposition: the sufficient statistics ($\eta$, $\zeta$) from Saez can be structurally decomposed, and the welfare metric from Bargain et al. can account for opportunity restrictions.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

The paper does not distinguish opportunities from preferences. All non-participation is treated as resulting from the participation decision (comparing $c_i - c_0$ to tastes for work). The paper acknowledges the importance of this distinction (pp. 1045--1046: "Whether the unemployed really cannot work or are lazy") but does not model it. The weight $g_0$ becomes a normative parameter that absorbs both the welfare judgment about the unemployed and the empirical question of whether they face opportunity constraints.

# Useful quotations / formulas

**On the central result (p. 1042):**
"It shows that an NIT with a substantial guaranteed income level and high phasing-out rates is optimal when labor supply responses are concentrated along the intensive margin. However, when labor supply responses are concentrated along the extensive margin, then the optimal transfer is similar to an Earned Income Tax Credit."

**Proposition 1 (extensive model, p. 1048):**
$$\frac{T_i - T_0}{c_i - c_0} = \frac{1}{\eta_i}(1 - g_i)$$

**Proposition 2 (intensive model, p. 1052):**
$$\frac{T_i - T_{i-1}}{c_i - c_{i-1}} = \frac{1}{\zeta_i}\left[\frac{(1-g_i)h_i + \ldots + (1-g_I)h_I}{h_i}\right]$$

**Mixed model (eq. 8, p. 1055):**
$$\frac{T_i - T_{i-1}}{c_i - c_{i-1}} = \frac{1}{\zeta_i h_i} \sum_{j=i}^{I} h_j \left[1 - g_j - \eta_j \frac{T_j - T_0}{c_j - c_0}\right]$$

**On the extensive elasticity as institutional (p. 1043):**
"The extensive elasticity is not an intrinsic parameter but depends on the institutional features of the labor market."

**On $g_0$ and the deserving poor (p. 1046):**
"Whether the unemployed really cannot work or are lazy is of critical importance to assess whether they deserve to get transfers. Liberals tend to hold the former view, and conservatives the latter."

**On the envelope theorem (p. 1049):**
"There is no change in welfare due to the behavioral response because workers leaving the labor force on the margin are indifferent between becoming employed and remaining employed."

# My quick takeaway

The foundational paper showing that the optimal transfer program shape depends on the margin of behavioural response. The extensive margin (participation) can justify EITC-type negative marginal rates; the intensive margin (effort) always yields NIT-type nonnegative rates. For my JMP, the paper provides: (1) the reduced-form framework ($\eta$, $\zeta$, $g$) that my RURO extension structurally decomposes, (2) the critical observation that $g_0$ (weight on the unemployed) absorbs both normative judgments and empirical facts about opportunity availability, and (3) the insight that the extensive elasticity is institutional (depends on labour market features) rather than a pure preference parameter -- exactly the motivation for modelling the opportunity density $g(h, w)$.
