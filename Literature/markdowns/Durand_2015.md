# Full citation

Durand, M. (2015). The OECD Better Life Initiative: How's Life? and the measurement of well-being. *Review of Income and Wealth*, 61(1), 4--17.

# One-sentence contribution

Presents the OECD's multidimensional framework for measuring well-being, organised around 11 dimensions of current well-being (material conditions + quality of life) and sustainability via four capital stocks, explaining the indicator-selection and dissemination strategy behind *How's Life?* and the Better Life Index.

# Why this paper matters

This paper represents a major institutional framework for going beyond GDP. For my JMP, its value is contextual: it shows how a policy-oriented institution translates broad welfare concepts into operational dashboards while remaining attentive to distribution, subjective well-being, and sustainability. It motivates the equivalent income approach as a theoretically grounded alternative to indicator dashboards.

# Core research question

How should well-being be defined, measured, and communicated in a way that goes beyond GDP, captures both current and future well-being, and supports cross-country comparison?

# Model / theoretical framework

Conceptual measurement framework, not a structural model. Current well-being: 11 dimensions (income/wealth, jobs/earnings, housing, health, work-life balance, education/skills, social connections, civic engagement, environmental quality, personal security, subjective well-being). Future well-being: four capital stocks (economic, natural, human, social). Outcome-oriented, people-focused, distribution-sensitive. Rooted broadly in Sen's capabilities approach, though in practice measures achieved outcomes as proxies for capabilities.

# Key objects

- 11 current well-being dimensions + 4 capital stocks for sustainability.
- Dashboard of headline indicators with traffic-light country benchmarking.
- Better Life Index: interactive tool allowing users to choose dimension weights.
- User-chosen weights (Fig. 3): life satisfaction, health, and education ranked highest.

# Data

OECD-assembled internationally comparable indicators from official and non-official sources for *How's Life?* reporting.

# Treatment of preferences

Not modelled structurally. The Better Life Index allows user-defined dimension weights, giving a participatory but not formally identified role to preferences.

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

No modelling of opportunities as feasible sets, latent offers, or demand-side constraints. The framework invokes capabilities language but mostly measures achieved outcomes.

# Welfare / normative object

Multidimensional well-being as a vector of outcomes across 11 dimensions + sustainability. No single welfare ordering; dashboard approach preserves dimensional detail but yields no aggregate ranking. Not derived from fairness axioms.

# Main findings

1. Countries with similar overall well-being often achieve it through different dimensional profiles.
2. Dashboard preferred over composite indices to avoid controversial weighting and normalisation.
3. Progress since 2011 in statistical measurement across dimensions, but many gaps remain.

# Main limitations

- Non-behavioural: no structural model of choices, constraints, or opportunity sets.
- Indicator-based and partly conventional; not derived from formal axioms.
- No single welfare ordering; no decomposition into preferences, opportunities, or responsibility.

# Challenge to this paper

The framework is normatively broad but analytically thin. It stops short of providing a formal theory of individual welfare, interpersonal comparison, or opportunity-sensitive justice. The equivalent income approach provides exactly what the dashboard lacks: a theoretically grounded way to aggregate dimensions through individual preferences while respecting fairness axioms.
