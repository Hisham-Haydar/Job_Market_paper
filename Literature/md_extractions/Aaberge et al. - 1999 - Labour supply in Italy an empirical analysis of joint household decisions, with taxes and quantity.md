The vast majority of labour supply studies report mean-sample elasticities. These elasticities do not capture the heterogeneity of the population and can lead to a misleading interpretation of the aggregate responses in the economy. As an alternative this study provides exact aggregate labour supply elasticities which account for observed and unobserved heterogeneity in preferences across the population. Table IV reports aggregate uncompensated and compensated elasticities. The uncompensated elasticities are derived by predicting (by stochastic simulation) labour supply for each household (wife and husband) under the current regime and when the wage rates are increased by 1%. The aggregate uncompensated elasticity of, for example, female labour supply is obtained by calculating the relatiative change in the mean female labour supply (over all females in the sample) that results from a 1% wage increase. The'estimates' in Table IV are based on 10 sets of simulations. Therefore, we have a distribution of 10'estimates'. What we report in Table IV is the mean and standard deviation for each elasticity. The aggregate compensated elasticities are 'estimated' by the same procedure, but under the restriction that utility levels should remain unchanged. In all these simulations the opportunity and hours densities along with preferences are kept fixed. Note that the elasticities reported in Tables IV and V are computed on the basis of the demographic and educational structure, the tax system and the institutional constraints that the agents faced in 1987. Thus, the elasticities may change when the demographic and educational structure, the tax system or the constraints change.
