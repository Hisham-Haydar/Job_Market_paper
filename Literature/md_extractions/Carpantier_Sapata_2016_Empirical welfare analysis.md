## ORIGINAL PAPER

Empirical welfare analysis: when preferences matter

Jean-François Carpantier 1 · Christelle Sapata 2 Received: 16 May 2013 / Accepted: 3 October 2015 / Published online: 19 October 2015 Abstract The conditional equality and egalitarian equivalence criteria were proposed by Fleurbaey (Fairness, responsibility, and welfare, Oxford University Press, Oxford, 2008) to provide better foundations to interpersonal comparisons in the context of heterogeneous preferences and multidimensional welfare. The first implementations of the egalitarian equivalence criterion follow an approach where the preferences are captured at the group level (based on socio-demographic variables) rather than at the individual level. Our contribution is to extend these models by using information on individual preferences, derived from the potential discrepancy between the group level optimal choice and the revealed choice of the individuals. We implement and compare the conditional equality and egalitarian equivalence criteria on a 2004 US microeconomic dataset and find that these criteria are relatively consistent in the identification of the worst-off. We also show that up to 18% of the worst-off are no longer categorized as worst-off when the empirical approach accounts for individual preferences.

Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona, Barcelona, Spain Measuring the welfare of heterogeneous agents and considering the multi-dimensional character of welfare remains a highly difficult exercise. Obtaining a social ordering that fully respects heterogeneous preferences in an ordinal setting requires some restrictions. One contribution of the theory of fair social orderings (developed in Fleurbaey 2008; Fleurbaey and Maniquet 2005, 2006, 2011; Bossert and Fleurbaey 1996) has been to define restrictive fairness criteria allowing such interpersonal comparisons. Designing a fair redistribution policy with heterogeneous agents is also a difficult task. It requires to draw a line between fair and unfair inequalities. The latter arises from factors beyond the control of individuals such as innate inabilities or parental education (more generally called circumstances) and the former from factors within the control of individuals, and based on their effort or preferences. From this typology between responsibility and non-responsibility factors, we can derive two principles (detailed in Fleurbaey 2008). The first one is the compensation principle, which states that the effect of circumstances (social background, learning abilities, skills) should be neutralized in order to level the playing field or, in other words, give to all the same opportunity set: “equal well-being for equal responsibility”. The second principle is the responsibility principle, usually captured by the liberal reward or neutrality principle, which states that there should be no differentiated intervention when two individuals have the same circumstances, that interventions should not affect/distort responsibility factors: “equal treatment for equal skills”. These two compensation and neutrality principles cannot be both fully satisfied in general. As Schokkaert et al. (2004) put it: “problems arise if we want to hold individuals fully accountable for differences in outcomes that result from differences in (pure) preferences (e.g., for work effort), while we want to compensate them fully for skill (or ability) differences.” This incompatibility has been proven by Fleurbaey (1995) and Bossert (1995). A solution proposed by Fleurbaey (2008) consists in setting constraints such that one principle is preferred to the other. The conditional equality and egalitarian equivalence criteria give pre-eminence respectively to the neutrality and compensation principles. The conditional equality criterion completely fulfills the neutrality principle and partially achieves the compensation for circumstances. The identification of the worstoff is achieved by determining what level of well-being an individual would achieve with some reference responsibility characteristics and his current circumstances. The conditional equality thus remains fully neutral toward individual responsibility factors. We will implement the conditional equality criterion in our empirical illustration with three successive reference responsibility characteristics (from low to high preference for leisure). The egalitarian equivalence criterion on the contrary completely fulfills the compensation principle, and partially the neutrality principle. The identification of the worst-off is achieved by determining what amount of resources an individual would need, with a reference circumstance and his current responsibility characteristics in order to maintain his current level of well-being. We apply and compare these criteria on a 2004 US database in view to identify the worst-off, defined as the 10% individuals most legitimate to benefit from a redistributive transfer. We consider income and leisure as the two sources of well-being, resulting in the traditional consumption-leisure trade-off in labor supply modelling. Accounting for the heterogeneity of individuals requires identifying preferences. The first empirical papers to implement responsibility-sensitive theories of justice are Almas et al. (2011) and Devooght (2005), who used the definition of conditional equality and egalitarian equivalence criteria to measure unfair inequalities for income. We follow an alternative approach and estimate preferences by means of a static structural discrete choice model of labor supply, similar to Aaberge et al. (1995) and van Soest (1995) and follow the implementation of the egalitarian equivalence criterion detailed in Decoster and Haan (2015) and Bargain et al. (2013). One assumption in their approach is that individual preferences are better captured at the group level (based on socio-demographic variables) rather than at the individual level. This is mainly justified by the practical difficulty to identify individual preferences but also by the idea that individual choices are noisy (not well-informed, errors of judgement) and need to be averaged out. What we propose is a way to partially identify the individual preferences, but also an approach where individual choices are better respected. Our contribution is to develop a new empirical approach by using information on individual preferences, derived from the potential discrepancy between the group level optimal choice and the revealed choice of the individuals. We show in our sample that 57% of the individuals made another choice than the one predicted by the model based on socio-demographic group variables. We use these discrepancies to refine our identification of the worst-off according to the egalitarian equivalence criterion. We compare conditional equality and egalitarian equivalence criteria (with and without identification of the individual preferences) and show the differences and similarities. To illustrate the importance of considering individual preferences, we also show that up to 18% of the worst-off drop from the worst-off category when our method to identify individual preferences is implemented. More generally, our approach allows to refine the evaluation of redistributive policies by measuring to which extent socio-demographic criteria are a good guide to identify the worstoff. The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 presents the responsibilitysensitive egalitarian principles and the fairness criteria, Sect. 3 explains the empirical approach and Sect. 4 the extension designed to better reflect individual preferences. We finally present the data and results in Sect. 5 and conclude in Sect. 6.

## 5.3 Who are the worst-off?

The objective of this section is to identify the 91 individuals (10% of the sample) to be considered as worst-off and to see how the reference values (for preferences in the conditional equality criterion and for the budget sets in the egalitarian equivalence criterion) affect this identification. We first report our results for the conditional equality criterion, then for the egalitarian equivalence criterion by emphasizing how accounting for individual preferences affects the results and finally make a comparison of the criteria.

The conditional equality criterion is implemented by fixing different reference values for β l. We first use the sample first decile, average and last decile of β l. We find that the identification of the worst-off based on these values is remarkably stable, with only minor changes (only 2 individuals out of 91 are affected). This is an interesting result for policy makers since the identification (in our sample) is relatively neutral to the choice of the reference value, at least if we take reasonable values for β l.

6 Conclusions This paper presents an empirical exercise on the conditional equality and egalitarian equivalence criteria. We propose an extension of the empirical methodology developed

Empirical welfare analysis: when preferences matter by Bargain et al. (2013) and Decoster and Haan (2015) to implement the egalitarian equivalence criterion of Fleurbaey (2008). The limitation of their approach was to consider only socio-demographic group preferences and not individual ones. We show that part of the individual preferences can be retrieved and incorporated into the analysis. Information on the individual preferences is derived from the potential discrepancy between the group level optimal choice and the revealed individuals’ choice. We also implement the conditional equivalence criterion and compare our results. We apply our model to a sample of singles from the 2004 US CNEF dataset and detail the profiles of the worst-off. We show that the identification of the worst-off by conditional equality and egalitarian equivalence criteria is quite consistent (with the relative exceptions of the zero-wage egalitarian equivalence criterion and the conditional equivalence criterion with (very) large preference for leisure). We more specifically show for the egalitarian equivalence criterion that going beyond socio-demographic group preferences and better estimating individual preferences affects the identification of the worst-off. As an illustration, we find that 18.7% of the worst-off identified by the zero-wage egalitarian equivalence drop from the worst-off category when we account for individual-specific preferences.
