## Abstract

Generous social assistance has been held responsible for inactivity traps and social exclusion in several European countries, hence the recent trend of promoting employment through in-work transfers.

Yet, the relative consensus on the need for dmaking work payT policies is muddied by a number of concerns relative to the design of the reforms and the treatment of the family dimension. Relying on EUROMOD, a EU-15 integrated tax-benefit microsimulation software, we simulate two types of in-work benefits. The first one is means-tested on family income, in the fashion of the British Working Family Tax Credit, while the second is a purely individualized policy. Both reforms are built on the same cost basis (after behavioral responses) and simulated in three European countries suspected to experience large poverty traps, namely Finland, France and Germany. The potential labor supply responses to the reforms and the subsequent redistributive impacts are assessed for each country using a structural discrete-choice model. We compare how both reforms achieve poverty reduction and social inclusion (measured as the number of transitions into activity). All three countries present different initial conditions, including existing tax-benefit systems and distribution of incomes and wages. These sources of heterogeneity are exploited together with different labor supply elasticities to explain the cross-country differences in the impact of the reforms.

Introduction Poverty has been reduced in many industrialized countries by the development of large-scale welfare systems which include generous social assistance schemes for the poorest. However, there is a well-known risk that the instruments used for this purpose generate social exclusion by making work financially unattractive, especially to less productive workers. Consequently, benefit schemes must be designed in a way which preserves sufficient income while dmaking work payT. In this respect, pioneering in-work transfers have been introduced for many years in the US and the UK, with the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) and the Working Family Tax Credit (WFTC) respectively. Several European countries have recently experienced the implementation of similar reforms or actively debated about doing so. Yet, the relative consensus on the need for this type of policy is muddied by concerns about efficient design, given the framework conditions and the general objectives pursued. In particular, the treatment of the family dimension is a crucial issue which deserves specific attention. EITC/WFTC-type of policies, means-tested on household income, are suspected to increase work incentives for single workers but to discourage married women with working partner. It is typically argued that this efficiency loss is outweighted by the equity gains from targeting families in need. In contrast, individualized schemes appear to combine clearer incentive effects with less efficient targeting, since resources may be transferred to workers in well-off families. Overall, whether redistributive and efficiency objectives can be reconciled in a single policy measure remains an open question. This paper provides the first comprehensive comparison of these two alternative policies and their implications on labor force participation. To do so, we simulate the labor supply effects of a purely individualized in-work transfer and of an extended version of the WFTC. We conduct these simulations for three European countries which experience inactivity traps, namely Finland, France and Germany. Despite numerous debates (esp. in France) on the appropriateness of in-work transfers, none of these countries has implemented policies as generous as the EITC/ WFTC. More generally, we tackle the issue of the design of in-work policies by exploring for each country the role of initial conditions, e.g. the institutional framework, the distribution of income, etc. These conditions are key to the success of a reform, as stressed in previous studies (e.g. Bertola, 2000), but have not received systematic attention in large-scale analyses. To analyse the potential effects of each reform on incentives and redistribution, we estimate a structural labor supply model for single and married women, using micro data for the three countries and a tax-benefit microsimulation program. To perform meaningful comparisons, the reform scenarios are calibrated to reach the same budgetary cost after potential behavioral responses. To clarify policy analysis, we compare the reforms in the light of two clear-cut policy goals, namely poverty reduction and social inclusion (measured as the number of transitions into work). Specifically, we question which of the suggested in-work benefits succeeds best on each account and whether the incentive effect of in-work transfers is significant in poverty reduction. Firstly, results show large efficiency gains from moving from family to individually based policies. While the impact on poverty is quite similar between the two policies, the individual wage subsidy increases women’s employment. At the same time, the negative employment effect of the WFTC on married women offset by far the positive effect on single women. This result comes in contrast to those in the US and the UK. Secondly, it turns out that the WFTC can be particularly effective when priority is assigned to lone mothers, a group showing the highest risk of poverty and social exclusion. We also discuss what can be achieved given each country’s social policy agenda.

In-work policies in Europe 2.1. Social assistance and in-work transfers In-work policies have been in place for a long time in the US, with the EITC, and in the UK with the Family Credit and its successors. Canada, Ireland and New Zealand have also had a relatively long experience with such schemes. The official objective set forth by policy makers is twofold: (i) to expand employment by increasing work incentives, (ii) to increase income of disadvantaged groups (see Pearson, 2002). There are also country-specific objectives such as the reduction of child poverty in the UK. Such instruments are often seen as more politically acceptable than a rise in social assistance and more desirable given the fear of increasing work disincentives; they are also seen as more efficient than an increase in the minimum wage, which might push up wage rates above the market equilibrium. 1 In recent years, the idea of in-work transfers has been spreading in continental Europe too, especially in those countries characterized by large levels of social transfers which are suspected to discourage employment. There, dmake work payT (MWP) policies have been suggested to recreate an attractive financial gap between welfare and paid work. In this respect, we focus in this study on Finland, France and Germany, three countries in which suspected disincentive effects are particularly marked due to generous welfare systems. In particular, the treatment of the family dimension and the choice of the unit of assessment may strongly condition the results. Two broad groups of possible schemes are usually encountered, although hybrid measures also exist. On the one hand, some countries have introduced family-based measures, i.e. in-work transfers which depend on household size and are means-tested on family income. These reforms, whose best examples are the EITC and the WFTC, are known to be well-targeted at poor working families, 4 to unequivocally encourages the participation of single individuals but also to discourage secondary earners in couples, bringing about a gender bias against the participation of women (see Eissa and Hoynes, 2004; Blundell et al., 2000, among others). The generosity of the reform also implies a high taper rate in the phase-out range, hence large increases in EMTRs and potential disincentives at the intensive margin. On the other hand some countries like Belgium have shortly experienced purely individualized measures, conditioned on individual earnings only. Given a similar budgetary cost, this type of measure would imply smaller benefits and a larger number of recipients. Indeed, low-paid individuals in well-off families may well receive some transfers. This policy is thought of as having greater incentive effects than the family-based alternative as it has no discouraging effects on second earners in couples; less generous amounts may also imply smaller increases in EMTRs in the phase-out region. It seems that policy makers may have to choose one instrument or the other, depending on how they balance redistributive and incentive objectives. Things may be more complicated. For instance, job-enhancing policies can well be viewed as a way to reduce poverty through increased labor income and an individualized MWP policy could then be considered in this respect. All in all, what can be achieved by family-and individual-based policies is not so clear and requires thorough investigation based on behavioral modeling. In the following, we compare both types of policy measures, choosing the British WFTC as a benchmark for family-based policies. 2.3. Recent trends in the UK and the three countries under study The WFTC was introduced in the UK in October 1999. It is a transfer to households with children where at least one of the adults is in paid work (employment or self-employment) for at least 16 hours per week; a 20% premium is given if one of the working adult is above 30 hours per week; maximum entitlement also increases by 49% per dependent child. Eligibility is based on jointly assessed income in the household; once income reaches a threshold level, the maximum amount is tapered away at a rate of 55% on net income. The WFTC is a more generous variant of the previous Family Credit (FC); for instance, the maximum yearly entitlement for a lone parent with one child was o3,180 in 1998 (FC) compared to o4,160 in 2001 (WFTC). Recent studies rely mostly on ex-ante evaluations based on tax-benefit calculators and structural models of labor supply. 5 Blundell et al. (2000) find a relatively satisfying distributional effect of the reform while they predict a mitigate effect on employment. Participation of single women would increase by 34,000 whereas 20,000 married women with employed partners would stop working. The UK experience clearly illustrates the difficulty of attaining multiple objectives through a single instrument. Let us now turn to the recent reform agenda in Belgium and in the three countries under study. The 2001 Belgian reform provides an interesting example of an individual refundable tax credit on earned income (Crédit d’impôt sur les bas revenus de l’activité professionnelle). In France, a refundable tax credit has also been implemented in 2001 (Prime pour l’Emploi, PPE) but is means-tested at both personal and household levels. In both countries, the measures target workers working full time at the minimum wage. However, maximum entitlements (around 500 EUR per year in both countries) are too modest to have significant effects on work incentives (see Orsini, 2004; Bargain, 2004). The Belgian tax credit has recently been replaced by a reduction of employees’ social security contributions levied on low earnings. In comparison, neither Finland nor Germany have introduced in work transfers stricto sensu. Both countries have extended the level of earned income allowance on income taxation. This concerns the flatrate local taxation in Finland (see Laine, 2002) and the progressive taxation in Germany (see Haan and Steiner, 2004). As opposed to tax credits, tax allowances consist of a fixed deduction from the taxable income; in particular, they are not refundable and the net gain for tax payer is somewhat limited, i.e. around 700 EUR in Finland and 1,115 EUR (in the first tax bracket) in Germany in 2005. Exemptions on social security contributions have also been extended in 2003 in Germany and now apply to gross earnings below 400 EUR, according to the so-called Minijobs reform. Overall, the budget allocated to these recent reforms in Finland, Germany and France is well below the level of transfers implied by the British scheme. An additional motivation for the present study is to question what could have happened, had these countries dedicated the same budgetary expenses as the UK to MWP transfers. 3. Microsimulation, data and sample selection The simulation of the actual tax-benefit systems and the reforms are conducted using EUROMOD, an integrated tax and benefit calculator for the EU-15 countries. For each country and for the year 1998, the program covers the computation of all social contributions, direct taxes and transfers (including child benefit, housing benefit and social assistance) to yield household disposable income. EUROMOD is based on micro-data which have been rendered homogeneous across countries, including similar variables definitions (see Sutherland, 2001, for a comprehensive description). Finnish data are selected from the 1998. Income Distribution Survey which contains socio-economic information on 9,345 representative households. German data come from the 1998 wave of the German Socio-Economic Panel (GSOEP) which contains information on 7,677 representative households. The data used for France are taken from the 1994 Household Budget Survey which contains information on 11,220 representative households; monetary variables have been inflated to year 1998; over the 1994–1998 period, demographics are assumed constant and no structural change occurres in the tax-benefit system. The reforms are simulated for each country on the complete representative sample while labor supply is estimated on selected subgroups of couples and single women, as explained below. A difficult issue concerns the treatment of unemployed workers as opposed to individuals receiving social assistance. Most often, in the absence of modeling of the demand-side, the former are treated as other inactive workers, which amounts to arguing that they voluntarily remain unemployed. A structural model of labor supply We use a discrete-choice model of labor supply that is uniformly applied to the three countries under study. Similarly to Blundell et al. (2000) for the evaluation of the WFTC, we make use of an explicit parameterization of consumption-leisure preferences. The problem is reduced to utility-maximization among a discrete set of hours, which allows to handle non-linear and nonconvex budget sets—due to taxes and means-tested benefits—in a straightforward way. As male labor supply displays less variability than female working hours, it is often reduced to the modeling of participation decisions only. Yet, male participation rates are extremely high in the selected samples, especially for France and Germany (see Table 2); attempts to estimate a joint labor supply model with binary choices for men did not lead to robust results. Consequently, we simply treat male labor supply as fixed at observed values. 15

Potential effects on hours and participation A crucial issue concerns the way a reform interacts with the existing system. This aspect of particular importance in the case of the WTC has been studied briefly in the analysis of budget constraints. The latter cannot be generalized, however, and a comprehensive picture of the impact of the reforms is obtained by looking at the change in the distribution of EMTRs and financial gains to work. Variations in EMTRs imply potential effects at the intensive margin and, in our discrete framework, translate into shifts between part-time and full-time. A detailed analysis is conducted in Bargain and Orsini (in press) and we provide here a short summary of the results. With the introduction of the WTC, EMTRs increase substantially for deciles 3 to 5, given the large phasing-out of the transfer. Despite a uniform taper rate on net income, the rise in EMTRs is effectively more important in France where taxation is relatively lower. Moves from full-time to part-time are to be expected. In contrast, the LWS reform shifts EMTRs downwards along the whole income distribution, while strongest reductions occur between deciles 2 to 4. Variations in financial gains to work indicate potential changes in participation. The gains are simply computed as the relative increase in household disposable income when the woman switches from inactivity to full-or part-time work. Larger amounts of transfer through a well targeted WTC naturally lead to a larger increase in financial gains to work for single women. This is particularly striking with regard to the gain of working part-time, which rises from 47% to 79% in France, from 67% to 91% in Germany and from 56% to 81% in Finland. The increase is much smaller with the LWS (only 2 or 3 percentage points). The average gain of working full-time increases by 7 percentage points in Finland and by more than 10 points in Germany and France with the WTC; it increases only by half of this when the LWS is introduced. Similarly, the proportion of very low gains (less than 40%) decreases substantially in all three countries when the WTC is introduced. However, for married women the picture is completely different. Firstly, it is expected that in the pre-reform situation relative gains are much smaller for married than for single women; this is especially the case in France and Germany where the earnings of the secondary earner are taxed away at the marginal tax rate of the first earner, a consequence of the joint income taxation system. As a matter of fact, the same effect applies with the WTC which is conditional on family income. As a result, additional earnings by wives may lead to a loss in WTC entitlement for their working partners, as seen in the budget curve analysis. The gain of working full-time thus shifts from 58% to 50% in France, from 58% to 53% in Germany and from 65% to 61% in Finland. In addition, the proportion of small gains associated to working full-time (less than 30%) increases drastically in France (from 9.5% to 20% of the selected women) and more moderately in Germany and Finland (from 17% to 23% and from 7.7% to 10.8% respectively). The LWS slightly improves average gains of working (especially full-time) and reduces the proportion of small gains, especially in France and Germany. 5.3. Final discussion In this paper, we present an extended analysis of dmaking work payT policies in Finland, France and Germany. These countries are characterized by very small incentives to work for low-wage households, the result of generous means-tested social transfers. Specifically, we simulate two in-work transfers in these countries. The first one is a working tax credit in the fashion of the British WFTC and the second is a simple wage subsidy. These two O. Bargain, K. Orsini / Labour Economics 13 (2006) 667–697 reforms illustrate the typical opposition between family-and individual-based instruments in the current debate on dmaking work payT policies. Female labor supply estimations are carried out using homogeneous datasets and similar specifications across countries; the labor supply model is then combined with the integrated micro simulation EUROMOD in order to predict potential behavioral responses to the reforms. Differences in dframework conditionsT across countries are carefully analyzed, notably the differences in income and wage rate distributions, to explain the differences in the effects of each reform across countries. We find that the overall female employment decreases after the introduction of the working tax credit. The participation of married women with children declines in all three countries and especially in France, where labor supply is slightly more elastic and where the net gain to work decreases substantially for secondary earners. This effect is only partially offset by a positive impact of the reform on lone mothers’ labor supply (and on a minority of childless women in couples in Germany). In contrast, in France and Germany, there are important efficiency gains to move toward the individual wage subsidy which, for the same fiscal cost, has an unambiguous positive effect on female employment. Results are more modest in Finland due to smaller labor supply elasticities of married women. As far as poverty reduction is concerned, results are surprisingly not so different between the reforms. In fact, both reforms are work-conditional while the poorest households are to be found in the inactive population. This way, even if the WTC is better targeted at lower deciles (2,3,4) than the LWS, households of the first decile do not benefit much from either transfer. Overall, then, the individual policy measure seems more attractive insofar as it contributes both to social inclusion and poverty reduction. Note, however, that we have focused here on social inclusion as a whole, without distinguishing across household types. Yet, results show that the poorest group—single mothers—would be substantially induced to work thanks to the WTC in Germany. In this country, a generous transfer of the WTC-type could reach a double dividend by taking poor singles out of poverty and back to work at the same time. It seems reasonable to think that incentive objectives should focus more on the inclusion of single parents than that of a potential second-earner in couples. Ultimately, policy design could incorporate these aspects to generate beneficial effects for lone mothers while avoiding discouragement of secondary earners in couples. Some improvements should be made to the present analysis. Firstly, a broader range of policy indicators could be used to differentiate the two instruments. Ultimately, the equity-efficiency trade-off that is central to the design of in-work programs should be addressed using a social welfare function. Further work is required in this direction and in particular a proper definition of the money metric equivalent to be used in coherence with the utility function. Also, the dsocial inclusionT objective adopted here seems to have a social value per se in political debates— related to the fact that increased labor market participation generates positive social externalities as described by Phelps (2000)—which could not easily be incorporated into a social welfare function of the usual type. 26 Theoretical work is required to extend Mirrlees’ framework in several directions to eventually cover a broader range of policy concerns. We have focused here 26 bBringing marginalized groups, including those who work in the underground economy, into mainstream economic activities may generate beneficial outcomes for society as a whole, for example through the amelioration of problems like crime, social destitution, drug etc.Q O. Bargain, K. Orsini / Labour Economics 13 (2006) 667–697 on pragmatic policy criteria used by decision-makers and grounding the debates about reforms of European welfare systems.

Secondly, in the present work, the difference in tax-benefit environments has explained much of the differences in the effect of reforms across countries, in particular the difference in the generosity of the social assistance systems. It would be interesting to disentangle further explanatory factors when assessing the differences across countries, especially how much is due to different preferences versus different tax-benefit environments. A complementary exercise, left for future research, then consists of assuming identical elasticities across countries in order to capture what relates specifically to institutional factors.

Note finally that recent trends seem to somehow abandon the idea of expensive in-work transfers in favor of the dwork firstT principle (claimants would be obliged to take job offers or risk a reduction of the benefit otherwise). However, it is not sure that the most stringent aspects of the recent Hartz IV reform in Germany be followed in practice and the new Minimum Income of Activity (Revenu Minimum d’Activité) is a non-compulsory alternative to social assistance in
