## INTRODUCTION

John Rawls's work (1971) has greatly contributed to rehabilitating equality as a basic social value, after decades of utilitarian hegemony, particularly in normative economics, but Rawls also emphasized that full equality of welfare is not an adequate goal either. This thesis was echoed in Dworkin's famous twin papers on equality (Dworkin 1981a,b), and it is now widely accepted that egalitarianism must be selective. The bulk of the debate on'Equality of What?' thus deals with what variables ought to be submitted for selection and how this selection ought to be carried out. Two different kinds of selection appear in this field. First, the relevant individual outcomes have to be determined. A long-standing tradition in normative economics has taken subjective well-being to be the relevant individual outcome, but there are now many objections to this viewpoint (Rawls 1982, Dworkin 1981a, Sen 1985, Cohen 1989). This first selection belongs to all individualistic theories of justice and is not specific to selective egalitarianism. What characterizes selective egalitarianism is an additional selection. I propose calling factor-selective egalitarian any approach that adopts the above method and the above goal. In short, factor-selective egalitarianism identifies factors r, t and w, and seeks to compensate t differentials but not w differentials. The identification of r does not pose much of a problem, but there are many ways in which t and iv can be distinguished, and there are also many devices for compensating t differentials. Consider the method of compensation. Again two main approaches can be distinguished. The first one, exemplified by Rawls's and Dworkin's theories, but also by van Parijs (1990), directly tries to equalize the extended bundles (r, t) across individuals. There is no obvious way in which this can be done, except when t is nullified, as in Rawls's approximation. An important proposal consists in equalizing incomes on an extended market (with virtual trade of talents), and another one sets up a virtual insurance market for talents, again on an equal income basis (see Dworkin 1981b, Roemer 1985). The second approach to compensation focuses not on bundles (r, t), but on the choice sets of all the outcome levels that the individual can reach if she chooses her will w in a given set W,. The objective is then to make all these sets equal across individuals, that is, to equalize opportunities. This second approach is taken up by Arneson and Cohen, Roemer and Sen. It is natural that the authors diverge similarly on the two questions (where to locate the cut t/w; how to compensate), because the choice set method of compensation almost automatically leads to sorting out factors, between t and w, depending on the degree of control the individual has over them. However, it would be possible to base the cut on control and responsibility while choosing the equal (r, t) approach rather than the choice set approach. But no author seems to have chosen this combination. It would also be possible, in fact, to choose something formally identical to the choice set approach without referring to control and responsibility in the t/w cut, and an example will be given in the next section. We are then, in fact, left with only two main schools of factor-selective egalitarianism, the'equal resources' school, which equalizes extended resources (r, t) and identifies w with the content of personal goals, and the 'equal opportunity' school, which equalizes choice sets and identifies w with the sphere of personal control and responsibility. Of course, significant differences exist between authors within each school, but this taxonomy is helpful for spotting the main issues. In this paper I will argue that although the general selective egalitarian project is basically right, the factor-selective approach and especially its equal opportunity formulation are flawed. The equal opportunity approach is generally empty, inefficient, unfeasible, and it relies on a shaky sociological and philosophical basis. Many of these criticisms carry over to factor-selective egalitarianism in general. But I will try to show that, contrary to what is usually contended in the literature, the dismissal of the factor-selective egalitarian project does not lead necessarily to either a restrictive resource-egalitarianism or to a comprehensive outcome-egalitarianism. Another way is indeed practicable, but with a completely different approach that is outcome-selective rather than factor-selective, and relies upon an alternative conception of responsibility.

Section 2 below provides a more precise definition of the equal opportunity approach. Section 3 identifies a separability condition which is presupposed by the factor-selective egalitarian project but which, in practice, is rarely satisfied. Section 4 shows that a basic requirement of factor-selective egalitarianism directly contradicts the Pareto principle. In Section 5, mutual interactions between individuals are shown to raise a difficulty for the choice set approach. In Section 6, miscellaneous criticisms of the sociological and philosophical implications of the equal opportunity project are put forward, and an alternative concept of responsibility is proposed. Section 7 makes use of this alternative approach to propose adopting outcome-selective egalitarianism instead of factor-selective egalitarianism. Section 8 presents a few final remarks. In this paper, the first target of criticism will be the equal opportunity principle, but indications will be given on how far this also applies to the general factor-selective egalitarian project. It is convenient to deal with a particular approach first, because there is no full statement of factorselective egalitarianism as such in the literature. And I focus on equal opportunity rather than equal resources because the former approach has been very clearly defined recently by Arneson and Cohen, and has apparently revived factor-selective egalitarianism, while the latter has no abstract definition (only specific mechanisms have been proposed) and its difficulties have already been extensively studied by Roemer (1985) and van Parijs (1990).

Notice that the formal definition of the equal opportunity principle does not rigorously imply any mention of responsibility or choice. As a matter of fact, its purpose is only to neutralize the effect of certain variables on the individual outcomes, in order to make these outcomes reflect other variables only. The specificity of Arneson's and Cohen's theses is that they advocate the equal opportunity principle on the grounds of responsibility, and locate the relevant cut between 'neutralized' and'revealed' variables at the boundary between nonresponsibility and responsibility.

Do these incompatibilities lead us to a criticism of ERET, and through it of factor-selective egalitarianism, or to a criticism of the Pareto principle itself? The Pareto principle has been directly or indirectly the target of many sensible anti-welfarist criticisms (and also of a few welfarist ones), and it is now widely admitted that it must be used with some caution. The proposal which will be made at the end of this paper will also violate the Pareto principle in some circumstances, and it would thus be unfair to argue against ERET on the grounds of such a violation. But the problem here is that the violations described in this section seem highly questionable.

Factor-selective egalitarianism, insofar as it implies ERET, leads to the following dilemma. On the one hand, it seems natural to hold the individuals responsible for the most volatile and fanciful traits of their personality, and this means that the allocation of resources should not take those parameters into account. On the other hand, for the sake of efficiency it is appealing to adapt the allocation of resources to these particular parameters when this does not raise any crucial social problem.

Most authors, following Rawls and Dworkin, implicitly or explicitly assume a market economy, and seem to think of a requirement of'equal income for equal talent', instead of'equal resource for equal talent'. When equal opportunity is enforced, should not we allow the individuals to trade their endowments in a competitive market? According to a wellknown theorem of economics, a competitive market leads to Paretooptimality. However, it would be naive to believe that this provides an easy solution to the above dilemma. First, what matters ethically in this setting is the final physical allocation, and the use of market trading does not remove the contradiction that the allocation should, and at the same time should not, depend on parameters for which the individuals are responsible. Second, not all cases offer the possibility of market devices as the school example demonstrates.

Besides, in terms of physical allocation, the principle,'equal income for equal talent', displays a very specific pattern of preference rewarding. For given talents, it rewards the individuals who have'cheap' preferences, that is, depending on the context, either those who have eccentric preferences or, on the contrary, those who have conformist preferences (see Fleurbaey 1992). It is not obvious at all that this particular reward pattern is desirable in all cases. One might instead want to reward those who, for instance, have specific preferences (for example, those who are industrious). A market allocation with'equal income for equal talent' is therefore certainly not a non-questionable solution, although it is defensible.

At any rate, the market solution creates mutual influences between people's choice of preferences, since an agent's choice of preferences influences the equilibrium prices, and then alters the extent to which the preferences of another agent can be satisfied. Now, this kind of cross influence between several agents' wills and outcomes raises a serious difficulty for the choice set approach, and this is the topic of the next section.

The'value of choice' approach is helpful in clarifying some important details of the equal social outcome principle. First, it supports the above remark that it may be desirable that individuals' initiative make a substantial contribution to the social outcomes they achieve. The social institutions must deprive individuals of as little autonomy as possible, and only take care of inequalities in final outcomes. Second, even if a global composite outcome is equalized, it may be crucial that individual choice should prevail in determining in detail how the components of the global outcome are filled for everybody (the global outcome is'social' while it consists of a set of private outcomes). Third, in all domains, including private choices, it is the responsibility of social institutions to fight against undesirable influences and to provide the individuals with good conditions for choice. The purpose is not to make sure that choices are genuine and that agents are morally responsible for them (first sense), but instead to enhance choice as a factor of quality of life for all individuals.

A second reason for the selection of private outcomes is that a private sphere must be preserved from any public scrutiny and social intervention. In particular, the basic liberties of thought, speech and movement imply that the outcomes directly concerned with thought, speech and movement should not elicit any intervention. One can probably go further, although maybe not as far as J. S. Mill, who qualified as private all the acts concerning only the self (Mill 1910; Riley 1989). On the other hand, Mill's proviso concerning a possible harm to others should probably be retained. If some private outcomes may be harmful to other persons, then some measures of social prevention should be adopted. For instance, the way I dress is my responsibility, but some eccentric options might be harmful in some ways to other persons in society, and it may then be reasonable to put some limits on the extent of my authority in this respect. A defense of privacy on slightly different grounds is put forth by Rawls (1982). A complete equalization of an overall outcome such as welfare or advantage would rely on an ordering of individual outcomes which would not fully respect the independent and incommensurable values of the different goals and life plans of the individuals. This idea is related to the well-known'bare persons' objection, but we need not go as far as discussing the monist eudemonistic feature of equal welfare, for instance, as Rawls does. In order to defend the principle of a private sphere, all that is necessary is that a comprehensive assessment of a person's success in life should not be made from the central viewpoint of social institutions. Only a partial and basic assessment should be performed at this level, and the rest belongs to individual goals and particularities. This is a third reason for purring some outcomes into the private sphere.

A fourth reason is also proposed by Rawls. The means available to individuals, and their achievements, should not be held up by the unreasonable demands of others. In particular,'strong feelings and zealous aspirations for certain goals do not, as such, give people a claim upon social resources, or a claim to design public institutions so as to achieve these goals. Desires and wants, however intense, are not by themselves reasons in matters of justice' (1982, p. 171). A natural way to protect the individuals from such unreasonable demands is to give all of them responsibility over their own goals and degree of satisfaction. The distinction between private and social outcome might help understand an apparent contradiction that Cohen finds in Rawls's theory. On the one hand, Rawls defends the idea of primary goods on the grounds that people are partially the masters of their preferences: "The use of primary goods ... relies on a capacity to assume responsibility for our ends' (Rawls, 1982, p. 169) On the other hand, Rawls objects to rewarding effort and desert because'the effort a person is willing to make is influenced by his natural abilities and skills and the alternatives open to him. The better endowed are more likely, other things being equal, to strive conscientiously, and there seems to be no way to discount for their greater good fortune' (Rawls, 1971, p. 312). Cohen notices that this suggests a double standard in the treatment of responsibility. A partial responsibility in tastes, according to Rawls, entails no compensation (as if people had a one hundred per cent responsibility), whereas a partial responsibility in effort would lead to a total compensation (as if people had a zero per cent responsibility). Maybe in Rawls's mind, the idea was mainly to make the allocation of primary goods independent of both effort and tastes because in both cases we are unable to determine the degree of genuine personal choice. But in view of the above arguments, another interpretation is possible. There is no reason to subsidize particular tastes insofar as this remains a private matter, that is, they typically belong to the domain of individual responsibility. In contrast, some of the rewards that a person might receive due to her effort are likely to fall into the cluster of social outcomes. And the principle of making social outcomes depend on genuine personal choice (as opposed to circumstances) is much more problematic than the principle of making private outcomes depend on personal choice. A society which does not directly intervene to counter the deterministic factors influencing private outcomes is simply a society which respects privacy. Whereas a society which sets out to distinguish circumstances and choice in order to allot social rewards will run into all the troubles described in the previous section.

In addition to the'value of choice' and the privacy arguments, there might be other reasons for putting some personal outcomes into the private cluster. For instance, height is usually considered a private

However, this is an outcome that individuals can hardly modify, so that choice (genuine choice or Scanlon-type choice) cannot justify this fact.

And individuals are seldom indifferent about their size; it affects their welfare and advantage (some jobs even require a particular size).

Nonetheless, I doubt that this situation should be deemed socially unfair, and it is not clear that we would like equality in this outcome (a uniform size for all individuals in the population) or compensation for this by other means. The same could probably be said about beauty, intelligence, etc. In conclusion, there may exist outcomes that are relatively important for the welfare of individuals even though their unequal distribution is not in itself a matter of social justice. 22 A possible reason for this fact might be a concern to respect the person. A direct social valuation of the individual's physical features or characteristics would undermine her self-esteem, and a principle for a just society might be that such differences between individuals do not matter in themselves. From the standpoint of justice, they matter only insofar as they impair the performances of individuals in their participation in social life.

We must then take issue with the claim that if hard determinism is adopted, full equality of welfare or advantage must ensue. None of the five above arguments is affected by hard determinism. The existence of a private sphere is desirable independently of the free will issue. Outcomeselective egalitarian institutions provide no compensation for any brute bad luck affecting an individual's private outcome, but this is not as harsh as it may seem. Either we simply do not care about inequalities in such outcomes, or we consider it more important not to interfere with individual autonomy in this field.

In view of the reasons for the existence of a private sphere, a few suggestions can be made about outcomes that can reasonably be put into the private cluster. First, due to the first four arguments, the life-style and consumption of individuals should be a private matter, although, of course, Mill's proviso should also apply to circumscribe the effects of externalities. Second, apart from considerations of mental health and education level, I would suggest putting all subjective and intellectual outcomes into the private sphere. This implies respect for basic liberties, but it also concerns the formation and revision of preferences and goals, and their degree of satisfaction. Third, as developed above, respect for the person should preclude any public scrutiny over natural physical characteristics of persons. Another suggestion can be made, now concerning social outcomes. A very important individual outcome indeed seems to be respect for the private sphere, as has been argued above, and it would fall under the responsibility of social institutions to guarantee such respect. Notice that the exact content of this particular'social outcome' would depend on the definition of the private sphere, which can vary to some extent in different societies.

Equal social outcome is then a principle which potentially gets rid of most of the informational problems implied by equal opportunity. The reason is that outcomes are much more easily observed than causal factors. Moreover, all of the relevant social outcomes could probably be chosen to be easily observable, especially if the most subjective outcomes (satisfaction, happiness) are put into the private sphere. No serious ethical limitation seems to be entailed by such an observability condition. Some problems of interpersonal comparison might however arise: how can we compare the career achievements of a plumber and of a lawyer? But I would claim that such problems are less tricky and more sociologically relevant than the factor analysis and welfare comparisons required by factor-selective egalitarianism.

The equal social outcome view is, in brief, a combination of Rawls's and Sen's theories. With Sen and against Rawls, it focuses on functionings and not on goods, because ability differentials are an important source of unjust inequalities amongst individuals. But with Rawls and against Sen, it argues that social institutions should not take care of the fate of individuals in a comprehensive way. 24 Only the distribution of social outcomes, which might also be named'primary functionings' in this perspective, matters from the standpoint of social justice. Another essential difference with Sen is that functionings are the relevant variables, instead of capabilities, which imply an idea of moral responsibility. As an illustration, an application of the equal social outcome approach which would seem plausible to me for western societies, would select only six individual outcomes: respect for the private sphere (denoted s, for each individual /, hereafter), health (hi), education and information (e,), wealth (we,), collective decision-making power (p,), social integration (avoiding loneliness, si,-). Applying the maximin criterion to an index of these six individual outcomes, that is, would be a tractable framework for egalitarian policies. This is just an example, and of course many details have to be worked out concerning the measurement of these six variables, and the shape of the index tp.

I will conclude with two general remarks. First, the definition of the variable s,, respect for the private sphere, can follow two very different approaches. The first one, adopted for instance by J. S. Mill, considers individual autonomy as isolation and independence of the individual from social influences and interventions. This approach is relevant as far as specific institutions (such as the state) are concerned, and the usual definition of basic liberties can rely on this view without any problem. But in a broader sense of individual autonomy, this approach is quite naive, as pointed out by Schoeman (1992), because individual life cannot be lived in isolation from a social context, and for an individual, increasing one's degree of isolation is more often impoverishing than liberating. An alternative view of individual autonomy can be built on the fact that belonging to various independent groups, in various spheres of life, is a basic guarantee against excessive dependency on a particular social group. Enhancing respect for the private sphere may then consist in encouraging multiple social integration rather than in hindering collective 24 In Sen's theory, subjective satisfaction is for instance an important functioning to be taken into account.

institutions. In addition to these considerations, Scanlon's'quality of choice' and individual self-expression can also be observed and fostered in many ways by various social institutions.

The last point is that, as can be seen in the above example with six social outcomes, it may be impossible to implement a precise distribution of some social outcomes (such as education, wealth, power) without seriously reducing individual freedom of choice (of life-style, for instance). One might think that this problem requires the introduction of choice sets or capabilities, instead of functionings. But then we are back into the morass of free choice and of the metaphysics of moral responsibility. I do not, however, believe that this is necessary. Indeed, if there is a trade-off between the distribution of some social outcomes and the respect of the private sphere, such a trade-off can be directly reflected in the index tp or any similar tool, and the implementation of a distribution of individual values of <p is simply a matter of economic analysis, similar to what is done in the theory of optimal taxation. To admit such a trade-off seems more sensible than to consider that differences of individual levels in some basic functionings do not matter provided they reflect choices. I would rather say that such differences always matter, but that they may be tolerated when respect for the private sphere is at stake.
