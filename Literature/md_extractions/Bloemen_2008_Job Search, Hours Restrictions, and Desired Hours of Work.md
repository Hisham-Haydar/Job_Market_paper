# Job Search, Hours Restrictions, and

Desired Hours of Work

Hans G. Bloemen,

Free University Amsterdam A structural empirical job search model is presented that incorporates the labor supply decision of individuals. The arrival of a job offer is modeled as a random draw from a wage-hours offer distribution. Subjective information is used on desired working hours to identify optimal hours from offered hours. Policy simulations are performed to address several policy questions: Does a decrease in unemployment benefits lead to the acceptance of jobs with less preferred working hours? How does a decrease in the length of the standard working week affect the job acceptance behavior of the unemployed?

## I. Introduction

In this article an empirical structural job search model is specified in which I explicitly incorporate working hours as a job characteristic that influences the job acceptance decision of individuals. Thus this approach brings together elements from the labor supply literature and the job search literature.

Early empirical studies in the field of labor supply (e.g., Heckman 1974;

and Hausman 1980) typically focused on the neoclassical labor supply model. In these studies the labor supply function, defined by the tangency point of the indifference curve and the budget constraint, is parameterized.

## III. The Data

We use data from the Dutch Socio-Economic Panel (SEP), which is a household panel survey collected by Statistics Netherlands. The households were interviewed twice a year, in April and October. We use data from the October 1985 wave up to and including the April 1989 wave. 22 Thus, the total observation period is three and a half years. In each survey wave individuals are asked to report their occupational status for every month in the past 6 months. ## C. Residual Analysis

To analyze how the model fits the data on unemployment duration we plotted the residuals. Note that the three model variants are all stationary structural duration models, which do not account for possible duration dependence of the hazard. The models, though, do incorporate various sources of unobserved heterogeneity. The residuals of the models are plotted in figure 3. The dashed-dotted line in the figure is the survivor function according to an exponential distribution with parameter 1. If the models are correctly specified, the residuals should be distributed according to this distribution. Although it is clear that the models would not survive a formal test, the results are not unreasonable for a stationary structural duration model. The models do reasonably well for shorter durations but fail to explain some of the larger durations. The residuals reveal evidence of neglected negative duration dependence of the hazard. The fit of the base model is comparable to the model variants estimated with desired hours, even though the use of additional data on desired hours imposes additional restrictions on the parameters of the utility function.

164

Bloemen The negative duration dependence, indicated by the residual analysis, is consistent with many reduced-form empirical studies, most of which find negative duration dependence (see, e.g., Lancaster 1979; Lynch 1985; Blau and Robins 1986). Any evidence of duration dependence in reducedform models for unemployment duration is generally interpreted as evidence against stationarity. 29 Blau and Robins (1986) also show that negative duration dependence of the hazard rate can be the net result of arrival rates and reservation wages that both decline with unemployment duration. 30 Bloemen (2005), in a structural stationary model of job search with endogenous search intensity, also finds evidence of neglected stationarity and negative duration dependence. He shows with a separate residual analysis for forward and backward recurrence times that the exponential model (implied by the stationarity assumption) is notably unable to explain the longer unemployment durations.

## V. Conclusions

The literature of empirical job search models usually ignores other characteristics than the wage in the job acceptance decision. In this article I present a structural job search model in which jobs are characterized by a combination that exists of a wage rate and a number of working hours that arrive randomly from a joint offer distribution. The model has a reservation utility property. The acceptance decision can also be characterized by the reservation wage rate, which is a function of working hours. The model provides a link between static labor supply models with job offer restrictions and job search models. The distribution of observed working hours is the result of an interaction of hours restrictions originating from the demand side of the labor market and individual preferences. We use subjective data on desired working hours to disentangle the hours offer distribution and the distribution of optimal working hours, and specific care is taken to model the relationship between the observed desired hours and optimal hours. The results of three model variants are presented: a base variant that does not exploit information on desired working hours at all, the “optimal desired hours model” that follows the common practice in the literature by assuming that desired hours are equal to optimal hours, up to a measurement error, and the “hours satisfaction model” that explicitly exploits the routing and the phrasing of the questionnaire by which information on desired hours is obtained and allows for a difference between “optimality” and “satisfaction.” The eventual empirical model is a structural job search model for unemployment duration, accepted wages and hours, and desired hours, which is estimated with data from the Dutch Socio-Economic Panel. The model allows for unobserved heterogeneity in preferences and in the job

Desired Hours of Work 173 offer arrival rate. We estimate the parameters of the utility function, and thereby optimal working hours, job offer arrival rates, layoff rates, and the wage and hours offer distribution. All of the three before-mentioned specifications fit the data on unemployment duration about equally well according to residual analysis. The residual analysis also reveals neglected negative duration dependence for the models. Especially the longer spells of unemployment cannot be explained by a stationary model, even if unobserved heterogeneity is accounted for. The distribution of observed working hours is fitted best by the base model, but this is due to the overparameterization of the base model. The main difference in the three specifications is the way in which they succeed in disentangling hours offer probabilities from acceptance probabilities. There is a clear distinction between the base model, on the one hand, and the two specifications that allow for the use of desired hours, on the other hand. The base specification suggests that preferences are relatively flat: this is shown by the shape of the distribution of optimal hours generated by the model as well as by the shape of the reservation wage rate as a function of hours, which does not have a pronounced minimum. In other words, it is hard to identify preferences for working hours without the use of information on desired working hours. The base model predicts that the optimal hours are on average higher than observed working hours. This is in contrast with the responses of the survey respondents, which indicate that the majority of the workers that are not satisfied with working hours report being overemployed. The optimal desired hours variant shows a concentration of optimal hours in the range of 32–44 hours a week and a pronounced minimum of the reservation wage rate at 36 hours a week. The hours satisfaction model also generates a clear relationship between reservation wage rates and working hours, even though the link between desired hours and optimal hours is much weaker in the hours satisfaction model. The hours satisfaction model differs from the optimal desired hours model as the hours satisfaction model shows smaller reservation wage rates at low working hours. This shows a larger willingness to accept jobs with part-time working hours. This is also reflected in the elasticities of the expected unemployment duration with respect to the benefit level that we computed. The hours satisfaction model shows a somewhat larger sensitivity of the hazard with respect to changes in the benefit level. Since individuals are more willing to accept jobs with working hours outside the full-time range their unemployment duration is decreasing more in response to a decrease in benefits. If we assume that (observed) desired hours are equal to observed hours for individuals who are satisfied with their working hours we find that for the hours satisfaction model the fit of the desired hours is much better than for the optimal desired hours model. The hours satisfaction model 174

Bloemen explains a large part of the peak that we observe in desired hours. The hours satisfaction also gives a better prediction of the overemployment that is observed in the survey responses. The rich nature of the model is exploited to address various policy issues. Does a decrease in unemployment benefits lead to the acceptance of jobs with unfavorable hours? A model simulation in which benefits were decreased by 5% showed that the impact on accepted working hours was not large. There was a small shift in the distribution of accepted working hours toward part-time jobs. Apparently, a lower income during unemployment makes jobs with part-time hours, which have a lower income per period, more acceptable. Does a decrease in the length of the standard working week affect unemployment duration, and does it affect the distribution of accepted hours? A decrease of the standard working week was simulated by shifting the peak in the hours offer distribution down by one category, from 38–41 hours to 34–37 hours a week. Recall that a large percentage of the sample respondents report the desire to work less. The results of the simulation show that the average hazard out of unemployment is increased. There is a decrease in the percentage of workers with part-time (less than 34 hours) jobs. Apparently a job with 36 hours is more acceptable to a lot of workers with preferences for parttime jobs, and consequently they accept a new full-time job (with lower working hours) more easily. There is also a small increase in the percentage of accepted jobs with more than 40 hours a week, as for workers who want to work many hours a 36-hour working week is not acceptable anymore, whereas a 40-hour working week was. As there are more individuals who want to work less than 40 hours a week, on balance the hazard rate increases. Finally, we simulated the impact on the hazard and the accepted hours if the hours offer distribution is equal to the empirical distribution of desired hours. We also find an increase in the hazard but not as large as the increase obtained by a decrease in the length of the standard working week. If someone unemployed has a higher probability of getting a job offer with working hours equal to his desired level the value search increases, which may lead to an increase in rejections of jobs with other levels than the desired level.
