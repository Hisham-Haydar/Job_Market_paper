# Full citation

Fleurbaey, Marc, and François Maniquet. 2019. “Well-being measurement with non-classical goods.” *Economic Theory* 68: 765–786. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper extends the fairness-based theory of individual well-being measurement beyond the classical divisible-goods setting, showing that the earlier ray-utility and money-metric families do not generalize unchanged once goods are ordinal or not always desirable, and deriving a short list of admissible families for these richer environments. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it directly addresses a weakness of the earlier Fleurbaey-Maniquet framework: many practically important dimensions of well-being are not classical goods. Housing quality, health, labor status, leisure, distance to city centers, and other attributes are often ordinal, bounded, discrete, or subject to satiation. The paper asks how fairness-based well-being measurement changes once those features are admitted. 

For your project, this is highly relevant because jobs-and-wellbeing settings are full of non-classical dimensions. Hours of work, job quality, occupation, and other job attributes are typically not well represented by the simple classical-goods model. This paper does not solve your (W(z,R,A;y)) problem, but it materially enlarges the normative measurement toolkit on the (z)-side. 

# Core research question

How should individual well-being be measured, on the basis of fairness principles and heterogeneous preferences, when the goods involved are not all classical—specifically, when some goods are only ordinal or when some goods are not always desirable, may be bounded, or may come in discrete quantities? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is fully theoretical and continues the program developed in Fleurbaey and Maniquet (2011, 2017, 2018). It starts from the earlier result that, under classical divisible desirable goods, two main families of well-being measures emerge, containing ray utility and money-metric utility. It then studies what survives once classical assumptions are relaxed. 

The paper is explicitly motivated by applications in poverty and inequality measurement where many observed dimensions are not classical goods. The introduction states that the theoretical inequality and poverty literature is largely classical, whereas applications often involve goods of the kinds studied here. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The framework is axiomatic and normative throughout. The core object is again an individual well-being measure (W) built from a consumed bundle and a preference relation, under the requirement that well-being comparisons be grounded in fairness principles bearing on consumed quantities and preferences. 

The paper studies three environments. First, the classical benchmark with consumption set (X=\mathbb{R}*+^K) and continuous, convex, monotonic preferences (R^{cl}). Second, an ordinal-goods model, still with (X=\mathbb{R}*+^K), but where convex combinations of quantities are no longer meaningful, so the relevant domain is the larger set of monotonic continuous preferences (R^{od}). Third, a satiation / non-classical model with consumption set (X=\mathbb{R}_+\times A), where the first good is classical and (A) is a compact set of attributes; preferences are continuous and monotonic only in the first good. Pages 768–777 are the core model-development pages. 

The paper then shows how to combine the resulting measures in a general case with classical goods and non-classical attributes together. Section 5 provides four combined families: a ray-plus-fixed-attribute family, a ray-plus-preferred-attribute family, a money-metric-plus-fixed-attribute family, and a money-metric-plus-preferred-attribute family. This is the paper’s final synthesis. 

The framework is not positive and not empirical. Agents do not choose jobs or hours in a structural model. The paper evaluates realized bundles and attributes under given preferences. Feasible opportunity sets are not modeled. 

# Key objects

The central object is the well-being measure (W(x,R)), or (W((m,a),R)) in the non-classical model with one classical good (m) and attribute vector (a). The lower, upper, and indifference contours remain the basic geometric objects behind the axioms. 

In the classical benchmark, the key candidate measures remain ray utility (W^\ell) and money-metric utility (W^p), inherited from the earlier work and recalled in Section 2. Figure 2 on page 771 illustrates both on the same graph. 

In the satiation model, two new objects are central. One is the fixed-attribute measure (W^{\tilde a}), defined relative to a common reference attribute (\tilde a). The other is the preferred-attribute measure (W^{a^{\max}}), defined relative to the agent’s preferred attribute at the relevant income level. Figures 4–6 on pages 776–778 illustrate these new constructions. 

In the general case, the four combined measures are (W^{\ell \tilde a}), (W^{\ell a^{\max}}), (W^{p\tilde a}), and (W^{pa^{\max}}). These are the final list of candidate families in Section 5.

# Identification logic

This is not an econometric identification paper. The relevant logic is axiomatic characterization. The paper starts from two fairness axioms inherited from earlier work—Supremum Nested Contour and Infimum Nested Contour or Infimum Nested Contour*—and asks what they imply under different assumptions about the nature of goods. 

The key result is that the classical dual-family structure is not stable under generalization. With ordinal goods, the two basic axioms become compatible and together characterize monotone-consumption-path well-being measures. With satiation / non-classical goods, the same axioms characterize fixed-attribute measures, but there is also another ethically attractive family characterized by Equal Well-Being at Preferred Attribute. These are logical, not statistical, characterization results. 

So the paper’s “identification” contribution is to classify admissible well-being measures by domain. It tells us which families survive once goods are no longer classical.

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are heterogeneous and always central. In all environments, the well-being measure must respect individual preferences, meaning that within each preference ordering the measure preserves the preference ranking of bundles. This remains a foundational requirement throughout. 

The paper’s main novelty is not on the preference side, but on the goods side. Still, preference domains change with the model. In the classical case the domain is continuous, convex, monotonic preferences. In the ordinal-goods case the domain expands to all monotonic continuous preferences because convex-combination structure is no longer meaningful. In the satiation case preferences need only be continuous and monotonic in the first, classical good. 

This is important for your project because it shows that once one changes the ontology of the goods, one must also reconsider which preference restrictions remain meaningful.

# Welfare / normative object

The welfare object is the individual well-being measure itself. The paper continues the Fleurbaey-Maniquet project of grounding interpersonal comparisons in fairness principles applied to bundles and preferences, rather than in raw utility comparability or subjective well-being data. 

Normatively, the major contribution is domain sensitivity. The paper shows that the right family of well-being measures depends on the type of goods under consideration. In the classical model, the familiar ray-utility and money-metric families remain relevant. In the ordinal-goods case, monotone consumption path measures are characterized. In the satiation case, fixed-attribute measures and preferred-attribute measures emerge. 

The paper is not about responsibility for opportunities, compensation for unequal feasible sets, or source decomposition of inequality. It is about how to define the individual welfare metric when dimensions of consumption are non-classical. 

# Main findings

The first main finding is that the earlier classical results are recovered and summarized. In the classical divisible desirable-goods setting, Supremum Nested Contour leads to the family containing ray utility, while Infimum Nested Contour leads to the family containing money-metric utility. Section 2 recalls these results and notes that the relevant measures in the earlier 2018 paper are strictly concave transforms of (W^\ell) and (W^p). 

The second main finding is that, with ordinal desirable goods, the two basic axioms become compatible and jointly characterize a **monotone consumption path** family. Theorem 2 states that a well-being measure satisfies Supremum Nested Contour and Infimum Nested Contour* if and only if there exists a monotone consumption path (P) and a strictly increasing function (w:P\to\mathbb{R}_+) such that well-being is determined by the unique point (p\in P) indifferent to the bundle. This is a substantive departure from the classical dual-family case. 

The third main finding is that, in the satiation / compact-attribute model, Supremum Nested Contour and Infimum Nested Contour* characterize the **fixed-attribute** family (W^{\tilde a}). Theorem 3 states that any such measure is a strictly increasing transform of the income amount that, combined with the common reference attribute (\tilde a), is indifferent to the actual bundle. Figure 4 on page 776 illustrates this clearly. 

The fourth main finding is that there is another ethically appealing family in the satiation model, characterized by **Equal Well-Being at Preferred Attribute**. Theorem 4 shows that this yields measures ordinally equivalent to (W^{a^{\max}}), where well-being is measured by the level of the first good that, combined with the agent’s preferred attribute at that level, is indifferent to the actual bundle. Figures 5 and 6 on pages 777–778 visualize this. 

The fifth main finding is the final synthesis. In the general mixed case, the various results can be combined to define four families of well-being measures: (W^{\ell\tilde a}), (W^{\ell a^{\max}}), (W^{p\tilde a}), and (W^{pa^{\max}}). Section 5 explicitly states these four constructions. 

# Main limitations

The main limitation for your project is that the paper still has no explicit opportunity-set object. Even though it studies richer types of goods, it remains a theory of well-being from realized bundles and preferences, not from realized bundles plus feasible opportunities. 

A second limitation is that the theory remains at a very abstract level. The paper tells you which families are admissible in different domains, but not how to estimate them empirically from labor-market data or how to choose among them in a concrete application absent further ethical commitments. 

A third limitation is that the paper does not study aggregation. It emphasizes that the resulting well-being measures can then be aggregated with many different social aggregators, but does not analyze which aggregators match which fairness requirements in the richer mixed-goods setting.

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing because it shows that the welfare-measurement problem changes when the goods of interest are not all classical. That is directly relevant for any project in which jobs, hours, health, or qualitative attributes are part of the evaluated situation. 

## possible use for model design

High relevance on the normative design side. The paper suggests that once you move beyond pure divisible goods, you should not automatically keep the same well-being measure. Instead, the type of goods in (z) matters. This is an important design lesson for a jobs-and-wellbeing measure. 

## possible use for identification

Its relevance for identification is axiomatic rather than empirical. It identifies admissible families by domain assumptions. That is useful for choosing a defensible welfare metric before asking how to estimate or operationalize it. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

This is the strongest relevance margin. The paper extends the fairness-based measurement program into richer commodity spaces and produces a shortlist of candidate families. For your project, it is especially helpful if the realized bundle includes qualitative or bounded job-related dimensions. 

## possible use for decomposition

Direct relevance is limited. The paper does not decompose inequality into opportunities, preferences, and pay schedules. Indirectly, it is relevant because decomposition requires a prior choice of individual well-being metric, and the paper shows how that metric depends on the nature of the goods. # Challenge to this paper

The main challenge is that the paper expands the measurement domain without expanding the opportunity domain. It convincingly shows that the type of goods matters for well-being measurement, but it still treats well-being as a function of realized consumption and preferences only. For projects where inequality in feasible opportunities is central, that remains an important omission.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper remains primarily on the realized-bundle-and-preferences side. It enriches the treatment of goods, not the treatment of opportunities. Preferences continue to matter centrally, but now the paper recognizes that different dimensions of consumption may require different evaluative structures.

This is a very important extension paper for the normative measurement side of your corpus.
