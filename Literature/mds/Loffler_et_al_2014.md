# Full citation

Löffler, Max, Andreas Peichl, and Sebastian Siegloch. 2014. *Structural labor supply models and wage exogeneity*. SOEPpapers on Multidisciplinary Panel Data Research No. 675. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper shows, by estimating thousands of structural discrete-choice labor supply models, that estimated labor supply elasticities are not very sensitive to most common utility-side modeling choices but are highly sensitive to how wages are treated, especially to the usual exogeneity assumption between wages and preferences; relaxing that assumption yields much higher elasticities. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is not just another labor-supply estimation exercise. Its main contribution is methodological and diagnostic: it asks why structural micro labor-supply elasticities are often low, and it identifies wage treatment—not mainly utility functional form—as the central driver. 

For your project, this is highly relevant on the positive side. Any framework that uses structural labor-supply estimation to recover preferences, simulate policy, or later build welfare measures depends critically on whether estimated preference parameters are contaminated by restrictive wage assumptions. # Core research question

Why do state-of-the-art structural micro labor supply models often deliver relatively low labor supply elasticities, and to what extent is this due to specific modeling assumptions—especially the common assumption that wages are exogenous to preferences and labor-supply choices? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is set in the literature on structural labor-supply estimation, especially the discrete-choice random-utility tradition associated with Aaberge, Dagsvik, van Soest, and related work. It is motivated by the broader puzzle that macro labor-supply elasticities are typically larger than micro ones, and by the importance of elasticities for welfare analysis and optimal taxation. 

Empirically, the main dataset is the German SOEP. The paper uses the 2008 wave, which reports labor-supply and income information for 2007, and restricts attention to the working-age population. As a robustness check, the authors also use US data from the March CPS 2007. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a structural discrete-choice labor-supply model formulated as a random-utility model. Individuals or households choose among a finite set of hours alternatives or job types. Utility depends on consumption, leisure, household/job characteristics, preferences, and labor-market conditions. Consumption is disposable income generated by wages, hours, non-labor income, and the tax-benefit system. Equations (1)–(4) lay out the general structure. 

The framework is positive, not normative. It is designed to estimate labor-supply behavior and elasticities for use in policy evaluation. The paper is explicit that structural micro models are needed for ex ante policy analysis and out-of-sample predictions, but it does not define a welfare metric or fairness criterion. 

On the choice side, agents choose over a discrete set of labor-supply states. For singles, the paper uses seven alternatives, from non-participation to 60 weekly hours. For couples with two flexible partners, the joint choice set contains 72 alternatives. This is a standard discrete-hours setting rather than a continuous-hours model. 

On feasible sets, the paper does not model explicit opportunity sets in your strong (A)-sense. However, it does allow labor-market conditions (g(j)) or (\gamma_n) to capture the availability of job types, fixed costs, part-time restrictions, or related constraints. This is an implicit or reduced-form treatment of opportunities, not a latent-job or observed menu-of-jobs framework. 

# Key objects

The central behavioral object is the conditional or mixed logit choice probability over hours/job alternatives. The key decomposition is between the systematic utility part (v(C_{nj},L_j)) and the availability or frequency term (g(j)), which represents labor-market conditions or constraints attached to job types. Equation (4) is central here. 

A second key object is the wage process. The paper’s main analytical target is the standard assumption
[
\mathrm{Corr}(\beta_n,w_{nj}\mid x_{nj})=0,\qquad \mathrm{Corr}(\gamma_n,w_{nj}\mid x_{nj})=0,
]
namely independence between wages and both preferences and labor-market conditions conditional on observables. Equation (5) formalizes this. 

A third key object is the large model-comparison design. The authors estimate 3,456 distinct structural labor-supply specifications per labor-supply group, corresponding to plausible combinations of utility functions, heterogeneity assumptions, welfare stigma, hours restrictions/fixed costs, wage-imputation choices, and wage-prediction-error handling. Table 3 is the central summary. 

A fourth key object is the authors’ preferred joint wage-preference estimation model. In this setup, wages depend on hours of work, and the wage-equation residual is allowed to correlate with preference parameters for consumption and leisure. Equations (8)–(14) in Appendix A describe this structure. 

# Data

The main data source is the German Socio-Economic Panel. The analysis uses the 2008 wave, which contains information for calendar year 2007. The sample excludes those younger than 17 or older than 65 and omits households where all decision makers are self-employed, civil servants, or in military service. The sample is then split into five demographic/labor-supply groups: single men, single women, couples with only the man flexible, couples with only the woman flexible, and couples with both partners flexible. 

For Germany, the paper simulates disposable income using IZAΨMOD, a detailed representation of the German tax-benefit system. To reduce computational burden, it approximates the tax-benefit system with a flexible second-degree polynomial mapping gross earnings and household characteristics into disposable income, achieving very high fit. 

For robustness, the paper also uses data from IPUMS-CPS for the US, specifically the March CPS 2007, and computes taxes with TAXSIM. 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural and heavily model-based. Preferences are identified from variation in wages, hours, non-labor income, household characteristics, and nonlinear tax-benefit schedules. The paper emphasizes that this is the standard identification logic of discrete-choice labor-supply models rather than quasi-experimental identification. 

The paper’s main identification contribution is diagnostic. It argues that standard models usually impose exogeneity between wages and preferences/labor-market conditions mainly for convenience, not because the assumption is well justified. This exogeneity assumption is shown to be empirically consequential. 

The joint estimation strategy identifies hours-dependent wages and correlations between wage residuals and preference parameters by estimating wages and labor supply simultaneously. Workers with observed wages contribute actual wage residuals; non-workers are integrated over possible wage residuals. This gives the model enough structure to test whether the common zero-correlation restriction is rejected. Appendix A explains this identification logic. 

Identification of opportunities remains weak. Constraints such as fixed costs, hours restrictions, or job availability enter only through reduced-form terms such as (g(j)), not through an explicit feasible-set object. So the paper is much better at identifying wage-preference interaction than at cleanly separating preferences from opportunities. [reasonable inference for my project] supported by 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy has two layers. First, the authors conduct a very large controlled sensitivity analysis. They estimate 3,456 structural models for each labor-supply group and analyze how fit and elasticities vary with assumptions about the utility function, observed and unobserved heterogeneity, welfare stigma, fixed costs/hours restrictions, wage imputation, and treatment of wage-prediction error. In total they run 17,280 maximum-likelihood estimations, retaining about 16,730 converged results. This is one of the paper’s main innovations. 

Second, the authors propose a flexible joint estimation strategy for wages and labor supply. This model allows hourly wages to vary with hours of work and allows wage residuals to correlate with preference heterogeneity. Estimation is by maximum simulated likelihood using Halton draws. The preferred specification is estimated for single women because of computational constraints. 

The paper uses the Akaike Information Criterion as the main fit metric and simulates uncompensated labor-supply elasticities by increasing own wages, typically by 10%. Table 4 reports the marginal effect of modeling assumptions for the SOEP, Figure 1 shows the distribution of converged-model elasticities, and Table 5 presents the joint-estimation results for single women. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled explicitly through the direct-utility function over consumption and leisure. The paper considers multiple common utility specifications—translog, quadratic, Box-Cox, and related variants—and allows for both observed and unobserved heterogeneity. This is standard structural labor-supply practice. 

But the key point is that the paper questions whether estimated preference heterogeneity is really interpretable when wages are treated too restrictively. Once the model allows wages to depend on hours and allows correlation between wages and preference parameters, estimated elasticities change markedly. This implies that part of what standard models attribute to stable preferences may in fact be an artifact of wage treatment. [reasonable inference for my project] supported by

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

The paper does not model explicit opportunity sets or latent job menus in the strong RURO sense. There is no observed or estimated set (A_i) of feasible jobs. Instead, constraints are incorporated in reduced-form ways: fixed costs of work, part-time restrictions, welfare stigma, or job-type availability terms (g(j)). 

This means the paper treats opportunities only implicitly. It clearly recognizes that labor-market conditions matter, and it allows them to shift the probability of choosing certain job types, but it does not build a full opportunity-set model. That places it between a pure unconstrained labor-supply model and a full latent-jobs framework. 

The paper is stronger on wage endogeneity than on opportunity heterogeneity. It helps distinguish preferences from wage processes more than it distinguishes preferences from feasible opportunities. For your project, this is important but partial. [reasonable inference for my project] supported by 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is positive, with policy relevance. It does not construct a welfare measure, equivalent income, or social welfare function. Its normative relevance is indirect: labor-supply elasticities are key inputs for welfare analysis and optimal tax design, and the paper explicitly highlights this motivation. 

The closest normative object is the use of labor-supply elasticities in optimal-tax formulas. The paper illustrates that changing the elasticity from 0.25 to 0.6 can materially lower the optimal top marginal tax rate in Diamond-Saez-style calculations. This is a policy-design implication, not a welfare metric. 

The paper is not about responsibility for opportunities, compensation, reference opportunity sets, or decomposition of welfare inequality. Its contribution is upstream: it asks whether the behavioral parameters fed into those exercises are being estimated under overly restrictive assumptions. 

# Main findings

The first main finding is that most commonly discussed modeling assumptions do not systematically alter estimated labor-supply elasticities very much. In particular, the paper finds that utility functional form, observed heterogeneity, and several other specification details matter less than often assumed. Table 4 and the discussion in Section 4 make this explicit. 

The second main finding is that wage treatment matters a lot. Predicting wages for the full sample instead of only non-workers can roughly double estimated elasticities if wage-prediction error is not handled properly. More generally, the paper concludes that the wage imputation procedure and treatment of wage-prediction error explain much more variation in estimated elasticities than the specification of utility functions. 

The third main finding is that the exogeneity assumption between wages and preferences is empirically rejected in the flexible joint-estimation model. For single women, wages are significantly lower in part-time jobs than in full-time jobs, and higher wages are associated with stronger preferences for leisure and weaker relative preferences for consumption. Table 5 is the key source. 

The fourth main finding is quantitative and important: in the preferred joint model, estimated labor-supply elasticities are more than twice as high as in conventional exogeneity-based models. The paper reports a move from about 0.25 in restrictive models to around 0.6 in the more flexible model. Table 5 and the discussion in Section 5 emphasize this result. 

A fifth main finding is policy-relevant. Using the higher elasticity estimate in a Diamond-Saez-style optimal-tax calculation reduces the implied optimal top marginal tax rate substantially, from about 72.7% under elasticity 0.25 to about 52.6% under elasticity 0.6. The conclusion uses this as an illustration of why specification matters for policy design. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that the strongest flexible joint-estimation exercise is implemented only for single women, largely because of computational constraints. This weakens the generality of the joint-estimation results, even though the broader sensitivity exercise covers all groups. 

A second limitation is that the model remains within the standard discrete-hours labor-supply paradigm. Even with flexible wage treatment, it still does not model explicit job sets or richer opportunity structures. So the paper improves the wage-preference side of the problem much more than the opportunity-set side. 

A third limitation is that the identification remains structural and assumption-driven. The paper does not use quasi-experimental identification, and it does not claim to have identified the “true” elasticity. The authors explicitly say such a true single value may not even exist. 

A fourth limitation is that wage-hours dependence and wage-preference correlation may capture several mechanisms at once, including unobserved productivity, frictions, and job characteristics. The paper shows these restrictions matter, but it does not fully decompose why they matter. [reasonable inference for my project] supported by 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is highly useful for framing because it gives a disciplined methodological reason not to take structurally estimated preferences or elasticities at face value when wage treatment is restrictive.

## possible use for model design

It is highly useful for model design on the empirical side. If your model estimates preferences from labor supply, you should think carefully about whether wages are treated as fixed, endogenous to hours, or correlated with latent preferences.

## possible use for identification

This is one of its strongest uses. The paper is essentially a warning that an important identifying restriction in standard labor-supply models—wage exogeneity—can be false and quantitatively consequential.

## possible use for welfare measurement

Directly limited, since the paper does not construct a welfare metric. Indirectly important, because any welfare metric built on structurally estimated preferences may inherit biases from the wage model.

## possible use for decomposition

It is moderately useful. It does not offer an (R/A/y) decomposition, but it does separate the role of wage treatment from other specification choices and thereby clarifies one major source of behavioral-estimation variation.

## possible use for comparative application

Moderately useful. The main application is Germany, with US robustness checks, so the comparative dimension is limited but still informative.

# Research questions this paper inspires

How much of the estimated preference heterogeneity in standard structural labor-supply models disappears once one models wages, hours, and job availability jointly?

Can the paper’s flexible wage-preference approach be embedded in a RURO-type model where feasible job sets (A_i) are explicit rather than absorbed into reduced-form constraints?

If one constructs a welfare measure (W(z,R,A;y)) using estimated (R), how sensitive is that welfare ranking to the wage exogeneity assumption in the first-stage labor-supply model?

Can one extend the joint estimation of wages and preferences to couples, occupations, or job-characteristics models without losing tractability?

How would the paper’s conclusions change if hours-dependent wages were interpreted as evidence about opportunity sets or employer-side job structure rather than only about wage specification?

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is that the paper identifies a major misspecification problem without fully replacing the standard labor-supply model with a richer opportunity model. It convincingly shows that wage exogeneity is too restrictive and that elasticities rise once it is relaxed. But it still leaves open whether the remaining structure adequately captures the job side of the labor market. For your project, that means the paper is a major methodological advance, but not yet a full preferences-versus-opportunities resolution.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is more useful for wages and preferences than for opportunities. Its key message is that estimated labor-supply preferences and elasticities are highly sensitive to how wages are modeled, and that ignoring wage-preference interaction can make labor supply appear much less responsive than it is. 

But it is not yet a full opportunities paper. Hours restrictions and fixed costs are included, yet opportunities are not represented as explicit feasible job sets. So for your project, it is best read as a methodological correction to the (R)- and (y)-side of structural estimation, not as a direct model of (A).
