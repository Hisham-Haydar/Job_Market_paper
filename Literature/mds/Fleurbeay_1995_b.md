# Full citation

Fleurbaey, Marc. 1995. “Three Solutions for the Compensation Problem.” *Journal of Economic Theory* 65: 505–521. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper studies fair division when unequal claims arise from non-transferable talents or handicaps rather than direct monetary claims, proves that full and selective compensation are generally incompatible, and characterizes three allocation rules—conditional equality, egalitarian-equivalence, and average compensation—under axioms centered on consistency and compensation.

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it gives a clean axiomatic treatment of compensation when interpersonal differences are not themselves transferable resources. That is a central conceptual move for later work on fairness, responsibility, and well-being measurement. Instead of treating inequality as a problem of redistributing direct claims expressed in the same unit as the resource, Fleurbaey studies differences in talents or handicaps that change what agents can derive from a common resource. 

For your project, the paper is especially important as a precursor to later Fleurbaey-Maniquet work. It does not offer a labour-supply model, an opportunity-set model, or a (W(z,R,A;y)) framework. But it does formulate, in an early and rigorous way, the central normative tension between compensating for exogenous heterogeneity and respecting preference-related differences.

# Core research question

How should a one-dimensional transferable resource be divided among agents who differ in non-transferable talents or handicaps, when fairness requires some form of compensation for these differences but full equalization principles may be mutually incompatible?

# Economic setting and context

The setting is a pure exchange / fair-division problem with one transferable good and finitely many agents. Each agent has a non-transferable personal parameter (y_i), interpreted as a talent or handicap, and preferences over bundles ((x_i,y_i)), where (x_i) is the amount of external resource received. The motivating examples are not labour-supply problems but general fair-division situations, such as cost sharing for a common facility where agents differ in ways that affect the benefit they derive from the service. The elevator-repair example in the introduction is used precisely to motivate compensation for some personal differences but not necessarily for all preference-related differences. 

The paper is purely normative and axiomatic. It does not estimate anything, use data, or study policy incidence empirically. Its objective is to characterize fair solutions under alternative compensation principles and to understand how the consistency axiom disciplines the admissible rules in a one-good setting.

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is an axiomatic fair-division model with one transferable resource. Total resources (w>0) must be allocated among (n) agents. Each agent (i) is characterized by a personal parameter (y_i) and by preferences (R_i) over ((x_i,y_i)\in \mathbb{R}*+\times Y). Preferences are strictly monotone in the transferable resource and representable by a utility function continuous in (x_i). An economy is (\mathcal{E}=(y;R;w)). A solution (\varphi) maps an admissible economy into a feasible allocation (x\in\mathbb{R}*+^n) with (\sum_i x_i=w). 

The feasible set is simply the simplex of non-negative allocations summing to (w). There is no job-choice structure, no budget frontier in the labour-supply sense, and no explicit opportunity set beyond this aggregate resource constraint. Opportunities / constraints are therefore modeled only as aggregate feasibility plus non-transferable personal heterogeneity, not as individual feasible sets of alternatives. 

The framework is normative throughout. The central objects are axioms on fairness and coherence: anonymity, continuity, consistency, expansion invariance, resource monotonicity, population monotonicity, and a family of compensation requirements such as equal resource for equal handicap and equal welfare for equal preference.

# Key objects

The key economic objects are the transferable resource (w), the individual allocation (x_i), the non-transferable parameter (y_i), and the preference relation (R_i) over ((x_i,y_i)). These define the compensation problem itself. 

The main fairness axioms are EREH (“equal resource for equal handicap”) and EWEP (“equal welfare for equal preference”), together with their weakened forms EREH* and EWEP*. These express, respectively, selective compensation for handicaps and full compensation across agents with identical preferences. Proposition 1 shows that the strong forms EREH and EWEP are incompatible.

The main solution concepts are: (R)-conditional equality, which equalizes welfare according to a benchmark preference ordering (R); (y)-egalitarian-equivalent, which equalizes agents relative to a benchmark handicap (y); and average compensation, which averages across the allocations each agent would propose when leximinizing welfare according to her own preferences. These are the paper’s three central constructive objects.

# Identification logic

This is not an identification paper in the econometric sense. The relevant logic is axiomatic characterization. The paper asks which solution rules are selected by combinations of ethical and consistency requirements, rather than how to recover parameters from observed behaviour.

The crucial non-empirical identifying device is consistency. Fleurbaey shows that, in a one-good model, consistency has remarkably strong implications: combined with anonymity and mild regularity, it yields expansion invariance, resource monotonicity, and population monotonicity, and it strengthens weakened compensation axioms into their full versions. This is the main structural force in the paper.

Thus, the paper’s “identification” is really uniqueness under axioms. Conditional equality and egalitarian-equivalence are singled out by dual axiom systems involving consistency plus weakened selective/full-compensation properties. Average compensation is then identified by anonymity, EWEP*, and a “sensitiveness” axiom, but notably without consistency.

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are central and heterogeneous. Each agent has her own ordering (R_i) over allocations combined with handicaps. The paper is careful not to collapse all heterogeneity into preferences alone: there is an explicit non-transferable parameter (y_i) separate from (R_i). This separation is one of the paper’s conceptual strengths. 

At the same time, preferences are used normatively in different ways across the three solutions. Conditional equality uses a benchmark preference ordering (R) and applies a leximin / maximin criterion relative to that benchmark. Egalitarian-equivalence instead uses each agent’s own preferences to compare her actual bundle to a common benchmark bundle with the reference handicap. Average compensation uses every agent’s own preferences as an evaluative input, then aggregates the implied proposals.

So the paper is not preference-invariant. Rather, it studies different ethically structured ways of using preferences in compensation. That is highly relevant to any later attempt to distinguish “respect for preferences” from “compensation for exogenous disadvantage.”

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is crucial for your project, and the paper is both relevant and limited. It does not model opportunities as feasible job sets, actual menus of alternatives, or stochastic opportunities. There is no analogue of an individual opportunity set (A_i) in the later jobs-and-wellbeing sense. The only formal constraint is aggregate feasibility of the one-dimensional resource. 

However, the non-transferable parameter (y_i) plays a role somewhat analogous to a circumstance, talent, or handicap that affects what a given resource bundle means for the person. In that sense, the paper is about compensation for non-transferable heterogeneity rather than for direct resource inequality alone. This is not an opportunity-set model, but it is a model of ethically relevant exogenous heterogeneity.

The framework therefore helps distinguish preference heterogeneity and non-transferable personal heterogeneity, but not opportunity heterogeneity in the empirical labour-market sense. It is much closer to compensation for circumstances or handicaps than to a model of feasible job sets. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative. Its object is a fair allocation rule for the division of a common resource in the presence of non-transferable heterogeneity. Welfare is not summarized by a social welfare function in the standard income-tax sense, nor by a money-metric utility. Instead, welfare enters through ordinal preference comparisons over ((x_i,y_i)) and through axioms such as EWEP.

The paper is directly about compensation. That is its main normative theme. It asks how much compensation should be granted for handicap differentials and what axioms characterize plausible compensation rules. It is not a paper about decomposition of inequality into measured components, nor about welfare evaluation of reforms, nor about social welfare aggregation over empirical distributions.

It is useful for thinking about responsibility versus compensation only at an early foundational level. Handicaps/talents are treated as ethically relevant differences to be compensated in some way, while the paper is cautious about allowing all preference-generated valuation differences to dictate claims. That tension is very close to later responsibility-sensitive welfare economics, even though the explicit vocabulary of responsibility is not yet central here. # Main findings

The first main result is an impossibility theorem: no solution can satisfy both EREH and EWEP. In other words, one cannot simultaneously require equal resources for equal handicaps and full equalization of welfare across agents with identical preferences in any sufficiently rich domain. Proposition 1 establishes this basic tension. 

The second main result is that consistency is extremely powerful in one-good models. Under consistency, weakened versions of compensation principles become much stronger. In particular, any consistent solution satisfying EREH* actually satisfies EREH, and any consistent solution satisfying EWEP* actually satisfies EWEP. Together with the impossibility result, this yields Corollary 1: there is no consistent solution satisfying both EREH* and EWEP*. 

The third main result is the dual characterization of two consistent solutions. (R)-conditional equality is the unique consistent solution satisfying EREH* and (R)-EWEP*. (y)-egalitarian-equivalent is the unique consistent solution satisfying (y)-EREH* and EWEP. Proposition 9 is the core theorem here, and Propositions 7 and 8 show that both solutions are non-empty on suitable domains and satisfy anonymity, continuity, consistency, expansion invariance, population monotonicity, and resource monotonicity.

The fourth main result is the construction of average compensation, which satisfies EREH and EWEP* and gives all agents’ valuations of handicaps some role in the allocation rule. But this rule does not satisfy consistency, expansion invariance, or population monotonicity. Proposition 10 states these properties, and Proposition 11 shows that under anonymity, sensitiveness, and EWEP*, average compensation is uniquely selected when there are at least three distinct handicaps. 

# Main limitations

The main limitation is the one-good structure. The author is explicit that some peculiar features of the characterization results come from the fact that only a one-dimensional resource is distributed. This makes consistency unusually strong and makes some solutions independent of the population’s full preference profile in ways that may be less compelling in richer environments. 

A second limitation is the absence of explicit opportunity sets, labour-market institutions, or behavioural choice. The model is normatively sharp but economically sparse relative to structural labour-supply or jobs-and-wellbeing frameworks. It cannot speak directly to latent jobs, hours restrictions, wage schedules, or decomposition of realized well-being into opportunities and preferences. # Research questions this paper inspires

Can a jobs-and-wellbeing measure (W(z,R,A;y)) reproduce, in a richer setting with actual opportunity sets, the duality between conditional equality and egalitarian-equivalence that Fleurbaey characterizes here?

When non-transferable disadvantage is represented by differences in feasible job sets (A_i) rather than by a scalar handicap (y_i), what is the analogue of EREH and EWEP?

Is there an opportunity-set analogue of average compensation in which all agents’ valuations of different feasible sets enter the social evaluation of compensation?

How does the power of consistency change once the one-good model is replaced by multidimensional bundles or by discrete job alternatives?

Can one characterize a responsibility-sensitive rule that preserves the compensation insight of Fleurbaey (1995) while explicitly separating exogenous opportunities from preference responsibility?

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is that the paper’s elegance depends heavily on a very stripped-down environment. The one-good setting makes consistency extraordinarily powerful and produces sharp uniqueness results, but it also distances the analysis from the actual structure of labour-market or multidimensional well-being problems. The normative intuition is strong; the direct applicability is limited.

# Useful quotations / formulas

A central impossibility statement is Proposition 1: there is no solution satisfying both EREH and EWEP. This is the paper’s clearest formal expression of the compensation dilemma. 

The key constructive definitions are the two benchmark-based rules. (R)-conditional equality chooses (x) so that all agents are indifferent, according to the benchmark preference (R), between their own ((x_i,y_i)) and each other’s positions. (y)-egalitarian-equivalent chooses (x) so that each agent is indifferent between ((x_i,y_i)) and a common bundle ((x',y)) with the benchmark handicap. These two rules are explicitly presented as dual.

The average compensation rule is
[
x=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x^i,
]
where each (x^i) is the leximin proposal based on agent (i)’s own valuation of the handicap profile. This compact formula captures the rule’s main intuition.

# My quick takeaway

This is a foundational compensation paper rather than a labour-supply or welfare-measurement paper in the later applied sense. Its central value for your corpus is that it rigorously formalizes the impossibility of simultaneously satisfying strong selective and full compensation principles, and then shows how benchmark-based solutions emerge once consistency is imposed. For your JMP, it is not a direct model of (W(z,R,A;y)), but it is an important early building block for the normative logic behind compensation, reference-based evaluation, and the treatment of exogenous heterogeneity.
