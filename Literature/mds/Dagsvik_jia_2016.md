# Full citation

Dagsvik, John K., and Zhiyang Jia. 2016. “Labor Supply as a Choice Among Latent Jobs: Unobserved Heterogeneity and Identification.” *Journal of Applied Econometrics* 31(3): 487–506. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper develops and studies identification of a latent-job labor-supply model in which workers choose among worker-specific sets of job offers characterized by wages, hours, and non-pecuniary attributes, and then applies the framework to married/cohabiting couples in Norway.

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters a great deal for your project because it is one of the clearest formal labor-supply papers in which opportunities are not collapsed into a universal budget set. Instead, labor supply is explicitly generated by choice among latent job opportunities, with worker-specific choice constraints and non-pecuniary job characteristics. That is much closer to your (A)-type object than standard discrete-hours labor-supply models. 

It is also especially valuable because the paper does not merely propose the framework; it focuses on the identification problem created by having both preferences and latent choice constraints in the same model. This is exactly the type of issue that matters if one wants to separate preferences from opportunities empirically rather than only conceptually.

# Core research question

Under what conditions can a labor-supply model based on latent job choice sets be identified from conventional cross-section microdata, especially when both preferences and opportunity constraints matter and when offered wage distributions may include unobserved heterogeneity?

# Economic setting and context

The paper is motivated by dissatisfaction with traditional labor-supply models that treat the problem as a choice over disposable income and leisure alone. The authors argue that real labor markets involve preferences over job characteristics and restrictions on feasible jobs and hours, so a more realistic framework should model labor supply as job choice.

The empirical application is to Norwegian married/cohabiting couples using 1997 microdata. The authors also discuss institutional restrictions on hours of work, including the prevalence of part-time work among married women and the importance of institutional hours patterns in sectors such as public health care. This matters because the model is used not only to fit observed labor supply, but also to simulate reforms in the opportunity distribution of jobs.

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a latent job-choice model. Labor supply is not treated as a direct choice over hours and consumption; instead, the worker chooses among latent job “packages” or market opportunities, each characterized by offered wage (W(z)), offered hours (H(z)), and non-pecuniary job attributes, alongside non-market opportunities indexed separately. Utility is written as (U(C,h,z)), where (z) indexes opportunities, and under Assumption 1 the utility has multiplicative structure (U(C,h,z)=v(C,h)\varepsilon(z)). The budget side is (C=f(hw,I)), where (I) is non-labor income and (f) is the net-of-tax function.

This framework is positive rather than normative. The agent chooses the preferred available job from an unobserved worker-specific set. The feasible set is therefore not universal. It is represented by a stochastic opportunity structure: job taste shifters are generated by Poisson processes, and available wage-hours offers are distributed according to an opportunity density (\theta g_1(h)g_2(w\mid h)), where (\theta) measures job availability, (g_1(h)) is the distribution of offered hours, and (g_2(w\mid h)) is the distribution of offered wages conditional on hours.

A central contribution is the derivation of the observed choice density. Theorem 1 shows that the joint density of chosen hours and wage is proportional to the attractiveness term (v(f(hw),I,h)) multiplied by the opportunity density (\theta g_1(h)g_2(w\mid h)), normalized over all feasible hours and wages. This makes explicit that observed labor supply reflects both preferences and opportunity structure.

The paper also studies an extended model with unobserved heterogeneity in offered wage distributions via a random effect (\eta). In one version, wage variation is across jobs for a given worker; in another, the worker faces one person-specific offered wage with inter-individual heterogeneity. This distinction is important both theoretically and empirically.

# Key objects

The main economic objects are the deterministic preference component (v(C,h)), the random taste shifters (\varepsilon(z)), the net-of-tax function (f(hw,I)), the job-availability parameter (\theta), the opportunity density components (g_1(h)) and (g_2(w\mid h)), and the observed choice density (\phi(h,w\mid I)). These objects together generate the observed labor-supply distribution.

The most important conceptual object for your purposes is the “opportunity measure,” (\theta g_1(h)g_2(w\mid h)). This is the paper’s closest analogue to a feasible opportunity-set representation. It is not a set-valued (A_i) observed for each individual, but it is an explicit structural representation of latent opportunities, including restrictions on hours and wages.

In the extended model, the random effect (\eta) and, in some formulations, job-specific wage heterogeneity (\xi(z)), are also key objects because they determine how much unobserved wage variation is treated as across jobs versus across individuals. 

# Data

The empirical application uses microdata from the Norwegian Labor Survey 1997 for married/cohabiting couples. The summary statistics table reports separate descriptive moments for both-working couples, only-husband-working couples, and only-wife-working couples. The sample contains 2254 households where both work, 256 where only the husband works, and 5 where only the wife works. 

The data are not ideal for the authors’ purpose because hours are measured with nontrivial error: weekly hours are observed, but usual weeks worked per year and overtime are not. The paper explicitly states that this induces the familiar division-bias problem, creating a spurious negative correlation between observed wage rates and hours. This is an important practical limitation for estimation. 

# Identification logic

Identification is the central contribution of the paper. The core problem is that observed labor-supply behavior is jointly determined by preferences and latent job constraints, so standard multinomial-logit or mixed-logit identification results do not apply. The paper shows that from the observable ratio (\phi(h,w\mid I)/\phi(0,0\mid I)), one can identify the product of preference and opportunity terms, but not generally separate (v(C,h)) from (\theta g_1(h)g_2(w\mid h)) nonparametrically. 

Theorem 2 shows partial nonparametric identification under an exclusion restriction using non-labor income: (v(C,h)) can be expressed as a partially identified object up to an unknown constant and unknown function of hours. Thus the model is not nonparametrically point-identified in general. This is a very important negative result. 

Theorem 3 adds independence between offered wage rates and offered hours and obtains identification of the distribution of offered hours (g_1(h)), while preferences are still only identified up to a multiplicative term depending on hours. Theorem 4 imposes a generalized Box–Cox functional form for (v(C,h)) and additional regularity restrictions, yielding full identification for the simpler model. Theorem 5 extends identification to the case with a random effect in offered wage rates, using an exogenous variable (X) that shifts only the opportunity density. 

This is therefore not a design-based identification paper. Identification is structural, but it is highly explicit about what is and is not identified under various assumptions. # Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy estimates joint labor-supply behavior for couples under two “extreme” wage-heterogeneity specifications. Model 1 allows wage variation across jobs for a given individual but not inter-individual random effects in wages; Model 2 allows inter-individual wage heterogeneity with one person-specific wage per agent and no within-person across-job wage heterogeneity. The paper compares their empirical performance. 

The utility function is specified as generalized Box–Cox. Feasible annual hours are discretized into eight alternatives: 0, 208, 624, 1040, 1456, 1950, 2340, and 2600. The offered-hours distribution is specified with a mostly uniform mass plus peaks at part-time and full-time hours, interpreted as institutional features of job availability. Wage equations are estimated in a three-stage procedure designed to mitigate division bias when offered wages and offered hours are independent. 

Model fit is assessed using observed versus predicted marginal and joint hours distributions, McFadden pseudo-(R^2), and Andrews-type chi-square specification tests. The paper reports that Model 2 fits slightly better visually and substantially better according to the Andrews test. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are represented explicitly over consumption, leisure or hours, and non-pecuniary job attributes. This is a major step beyond conventional labor-supply models, which usually abstract from job characteristics and treat labor supply as a choice over hours and income only. The paper is explicit that agents may care about job tasks, location, and the social and physical environment. 

At the same time, the paper’s core identification message is that preferences are hard to separate from opportunities. Without additional assumptions or extra information such as desired hours, the deterministic utility component cannot generally be identified separately from the opportunity measure. This is precisely the kind of empirical entanglement your project is concerned with. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is the paper’s main value for your project. Opportunities are modeled explicitly. The worker does not face a universal deterministic choice set. Instead, each worker faces an unobserved set of latent market and non-market opportunities, represented through the opportunity measure (\theta g_1(h)g_2(w\mid h)). This object captures restrictions due to qualifications, competition, institutional regulation of hours, and stochastic availability of job types.

The model therefore goes well beyond treating availability constraints implicitly. It explicitly allows for hours restrictions, unequal access to job types, and differences in the composition of feasible offers. This is one of the strongest papers in your corpus for thinking about how a labor-supply model can incorporate opportunity heterogeneity structurally.

However, the opportunity object is latent and probabilistic, not an observed set (A_i). The paper does not recover person-specific feasible sets directly; it estimates structural features of the distribution of latent opportunities. Thus it helps distinguish both preference heterogeneity and opportunity heterogeneity, but only within a probabilistic latent-choice-set framework.

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is almost entirely positive rather than explicitly normative. It does not define a welfare measure, does not compute equivalent income or social welfare, and does not discuss fairness, responsibility, or compensation principles. Its concern is behavioural modelling, identification, and simulation of policy-relevant changes in opportunities and taxes.

That said, the paper is highly relevant for normative work indirectly because it provides an explicit model of opportunity constraints that could serve as a positive foundation for later welfare analysis. In particular, it is useful for any project that wants to speak normatively about opportunities while avoiding the empirical mistake of treating all observed labor-supply variation as preference-driven. # Main findings

The main theoretical finding is that the latent-job framework is not generally identified nonparametrically from cross-section data because observed labor supply reflects both preferences and opportunity constraints. Identification can be obtained only under additional restrictions such as independence of offered hours and wages, functional-form assumptions on preferences, or exogenous variables that shift only opportunity distributions. 

Empirically, both estimated model versions fit the Norwegian data reasonably well, but the model with solely inter-individual wage heterogeneity fits better than the model with wage variation only across jobs. The conclusion section states this explicitly, and the Andrews goodness-of-fit test strongly favours Model 2. 

The paper also finds moderate own-wage responsiveness for married women and very small wage responsiveness for married men. In Model 2, the own-wage elasticity of married women’s probability of working is 0.33, so a 5 percent increase in women’s wages raises participation by roughly 1.5 percent; the cross-wage elasticity is negative and smaller in magnitude. Table II on page 14 reports these elasticities.

A further important finding is nonlinear labour-supply response. *Figure 2 on page 15* shows expected annual hours of work for married women as a function of their wage under two husband-wage scenarios. The labor-supply curves are steeper at low wage levels, especially in Model 2, indicating stronger wage sensitivity at the lower end.

Finally, the paper shows that the model can simulate reforms to the opportunity distribution itself. In the example, removing the part-time peak in offered hours and shifting mass toward full-time jobs substantially reduces part-time work and raises full-time work for married women, while leaving men’s labour supply nearly unchanged. *Figure 3 on page 16* displays this pre-reform and post-reform hours distribution.

# Main limitations

The first limitation is data quality. The authors explicitly state that the Norwegian Labor Survey is not ideal because of measurement error in hours. Since annual weeks and overtime are unobserved, the data generate division bias and complicate identification of the relationship between wages and hours. 

A second limitation is that the opportunity set remains latent and distributional. The model captures opportunity heterogeneity far better than conventional approaches, but it still does not deliver observed person-specific feasible sets. For a project like yours, this means it is a strong empirical foundation but not yet a direct empirical (A_i)-dataset. [reasonable inference for my project] supported by

A third limitation is that the empirical comparison of wage heterogeneity is conducted only between two extreme models. The paper itself notes that a more general framework allowing both across-job and across-person wage heterogeneity is theoretically available, but difficult to estimate credibly with these data. 

A fourth limitation is that the framework is not an equilibrium model. The policy simulations regarding hours restrictions are pure supply-side simulations conditional on opportunity distributions, not full labor-market equilibrium analyses. This matters for interpretation of policy counterfactuals.

# Challenge to this paper

The main challenge is that, although the paper moves decisively toward modeling opportunities, the opportunity object remains latent, distributional, and only partially identified without substantial structure. For a project that wants to make normative claims about actual feasible sets, one may still worry that the estimated opportunity measure is too reduced-form to bear heavy ethical interpretation on its own. # Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is one of the strongest in your corpus on opportunities versus preferences. It explicitly states that traditional labor-supply models neglect the fact that workers face restrictions on job opportunities and may have preferences over job types, and then builds a framework in which both are present. 

Its main contribution is precisely to show that one cannot in general separate the two from standard cross-section data without additional structure. That makes it directly relevant to your project’s central concern about distinguishing preferences from opportunities, both conceptually and empirically. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The core choice-density formula is Theorem 1:
[
\phi(h,w\mid I)=
\frac{v(f(hw),I,h),\theta g_1(h)g_2(w\mid h)}
{v(f(0),I,0)+\theta\sum_{r\in D}\int_0^\infty v(f(ry),I,r)g_1(r)g_2(y\mid r),dy}.
]
This is central because it makes explicit that observed choice is relative attractiveness weighted by availability.

A second crucial expression is the observable ratio
[
\phi(h,w\mid I)/\phi(0,0\mid I),
]
which the paper uses to show what is identified and why preferences and opportunity measures are confounded without additional assumptions. The identification discussion around equation (7) on page 7 is fundamental. 

The empirical results in *Table II on page 14* and *Figure 2 on page 15* are especially useful if you want quantitative evidence on nonlinear female labor-supply response in the maintained model. The policy-simulation result in *Figure 3 on page 16* is useful as a demonstration that the framework can study reforms to opportunity distributions, not only wage-tax reforms.
