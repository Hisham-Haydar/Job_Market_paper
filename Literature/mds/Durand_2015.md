# Full citation

Durand, Martine. 2015. “The OECD Better Life Initiative: How’s Life? and the Measurement of Well-Being.” *Review of Income and Wealth* 61(1): 4–17. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper presents the OECD’s multidimensional framework for measuring well-being, organized around 11 dimensions of current well-being and a sustainability perspective based on four types of capital, and explains the indicator-selection and dissemination strategy behind *How’s Life?* and the Better Life Index. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is a compact and influential statement of a major institutional framework for measuring well-being beyond GDP. For your project, its main value is conceptual rather than econometric: it shows how a policy-oriented institution translates a broad welfare concept into an operational dashboard while remaining attentive to distribution, subjective well-being, and sustainability. 

It is also useful because it explicitly situates the OECD framework relative to the capabilities approach and to the Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi agenda. However, it does not address structural labour supply, discrete choice, feasible job sets, or opportunity-sensitive fairness in your specific sense. Its relevance is therefore mainly on the general welfare-measurement side, not on the (W(z,R,A;y)) modelling side. 

# Core research question

How should well-being be defined, measured, monitored, and communicated in a way that goes beyond GDP, captures both current and future well-being, and remains useful for cross-country comparison and policy discussion? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is not a single-country empirical study. It is a methodological and institutional presentation of the OECD Better Life Initiative, launched in 2011, and of the framework used in the OECD’s *How’s Life?* reporting. Its context is international statistical practice and policy-oriented social measurement. 

The intended scope is cross-country comparison, mainly within the OECD area, though the paper explicitly notes that the framework can be adapted for countries at different stages of development and is not meant to be fixed permanently. The framework’s central visual summary is the diagram in Figure 1 on page 5, which separates current well-being from the sustainability of well-being over time. 

# Model / theoretical framework

This is not a structural behavioural model. It is a conceptual measurement framework. Current well-being is divided into two broad domains: material living conditions and quality of life. These are further decomposed into 11 dimensions: income and wealth; jobs and earnings; housing; health; work–life balance; education and skills; social connections; civic engagement and governance; environmental quality; personal security; and subjective well-being. Future well-being is treated through the sustainability of well-being over time, measured via economic, natural, human, and social capital. Figure 1 on page 5 is the core formal representation. 

The framework is explicitly outcome-oriented. It focuses on people rather than the economy, emphasizes well-being outcomes rather than inputs, considers both objective and subjective indicators, and gives importance to distributions across groups alongside averages. These four features are presented as distinctive properties of the OECD approach. 

The framework is normative and descriptive rather than positive-structural. It does not model agents choosing among feasible alternatives. There is no formal individual optimization problem, no explicit feasible set, and no econometric identification strategy in the usual sense. Instead, the paper proposes a multidimensional evaluative architecture for statistical monitoring. 

The paper states that the framework may be regarded as rooted in Sen’s capabilities approach. But it also concedes that, in practice, the OECD mainly measures achieved outcomes and uses these as imperfect operational proxies for capabilities because direct measures of freedom or constraint are generally unavailable. This point is made most clearly on pages 8–9. 

# Key objects

The main objects are the 11 current well-being dimensions, the four capital stocks for future well-being, the dashboard of headline indicators, the traffic-light presentation of country performance, and the Better Life Index as an interactive dissemination tool. 

The current well-being dimensions are the most important economic objects in the paper. They are not latent variables in an econometric model but normative-statistical categories used for monitoring outcomes. For future well-being, the relevant objects are the stocks of economic, natural, human, and social capital that sustain well-being over time. 

Another key object is the distinction between dashboard and composite approaches. The paper argues that composite indexes can obscure information and require controversial normalization and weighting, whereas dashboards retain dimensional detail, though at the cost of simplicity. The Better Life Index partially addresses weighting by letting users choose their own weights, but the paper remains cautious about aggregation. 

# Data

The paper is not built around one micro-dataset or one estimation sample. The data base is the set of internationally comparable well-being indicators assembled by the OECD for *How’s Life?*, drawing on official and sometimes non-official sources. Indicator selection is discussed in detail on pages 9–11. 

The paper lists the original 2011 headline indicators for each dimension and notes additional headline indicators introduced in the 2013 edition, such as housing costs, education expectancy, satisfaction with water quality, short job tenure, and adult competencies. These are cross-national monitoring indicators, not variables in a structural model. 

Where official data are unavailable or insufficiently comparable, the OECD uses non-official sources as placeholders, while explicitly warning that these results should be treated more cautiously. This is an important practical feature of the framework. 

# Identification logic

This is not an identification paper in the econometric sense. There is no causal or structural identification argument linking observed behaviour to parameters. Instead, the relevant logic is conceptual-statistical: define dimensions of well-being, specify criteria for indicator selection, and then populate the framework with the best currently available internationally comparable measures. 

The most important “selection logic” is that indicators should measure individual or household outcomes, reflect outcomes rather than means, allow disaggregation across groups, have face validity, be widely accepted, be policy-relevant, be comparable across countries, and, as far as possible, come from official sources. This is the paper’s closest analogue to an identification strategy. 

Because the framework is a measurement architecture rather than a behavioural model, its inferential limits are substantial from your perspective. It does not identify preferences, opportunity sets, trade-offs, or welfare weights from observed choices. Those are largely treated outside the model, either through normative judgement or through user-defined weighting in the Better Life Index. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper has no formal estimation stage. Its empirical strategy is indicator construction, selection, benchmarking, and dissemination. The OECD populates the framework with headline indicators, presents them in dashboard form, summarizes country performance using traffic lights, and offers an interactive Better Life Index through which users can apply their own weights to the 11 well-being dimensions. 

The traffic-light approach classifies countries into top 20 percent, middle 60 percent, and bottom 20 percent for each indicator set. The Better Life Index, shown in Figure 2 on page 13, is a dissemination and engagement tool rather than an estimation method. Figure 3 on page 14 reports the weights chosen by users of the Better Life Index as of May 2013. 

The paper is therefore best understood as a framework-and-indicator paper, not as an econometric application. Its contribution is operationalization, not estimation. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are not modeled structurally. The paper does not estimate utility functions, choice probabilities, or welfare weights from individual choices. In that sense, preference heterogeneity is largely outside the formal structure. 

There are two limited roles for preferences. First, the paper places itself broadly within the capabilities tradition rather than a purely welfarist tradition, which already implies some distancing from utility-only approaches. Second, the Better Life Index allows users to assign their own dimension weights, which gives a participatory but not formally identified role to preferences or priorities. Figure 3 on page 14 shows that Better Life Index users tended to rank life satisfaction, health, and education most highly. 

For your project, this means the paper is not useful for modelling (R) in a rigorous behavioural sense. It is closer to a pluralistic welfare dashboard than to any structural or axiomatic treatment of heterogeneous preferences. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is crucial for your project, and the paper is quite limited here. It does not model opportunities or constraints as feasible choice sets. There is no analogue of an individual job opportunity set (A), no labour-market rationing, no latent offer distribution, and no formal distinction between realized outcomes and opportunity sets. 

The paper does discuss capabilities, freedom, and political voice, and it notes that some outcomes such as health, education, and civic freedom may expand people’s room for choice. But it explicitly acknowledges that the OECD framework mostly measures achieved outcomes rather than the underlying sets of options available to individuals. This is a broad affinity to opportunity-sensitive reasoning, not an explicit treatment of opportunities. 

So the paper does not help distinguish preference heterogeneity from opportunity heterogeneity. It also does not study responsibility versus compensation for opportunities. At most, it provides a general multidimensional setting in which such questions could later be asked. 

# Welfare / normative object

The normative object is multidimensional well-being, understood as a vector of outcomes across 11 dimensions plus the sustainability conditions that support future well-being. The paper rejects GDP as a sufficient proxy for welfare and rejects a purely income-based or purely utility-based assessment. 

This is therefore an explicitly normative measurement framework, though not an axiomatic one in your sense. The paper does not derive welfare measures from fairness axioms or formal social choice conditions. Instead, it justifies its framework through policy relevance, empirical feasibility, and broad normative appeal, especially via the capabilities approach. 

The paper is also distribution-sensitive. It emphasizes that well-being measurement should consider disparities across gender, age, and socio-economic groups alongside averages. But it does not decompose inequality into preferences, opportunities, effort, or other responsibility-sensitive sources. 

# Main findings

The first main finding is institutional and conceptual: the OECD framework measures current well-being through 11 dimensions and future well-being through four forms of capital. Figure 1 on page 5 is the key summary of this architecture. 

The second main finding is methodological: the OECD prefers a dashboard of indicators to a fully aggregated composite index because composite measures involve controversial normalization and weighting choices and may conceal important information. The paper nonetheless uses traffic lights and the Better Life Index as communication tools. 

A third substantive finding is that countries with similar overall well-being performance often achieve it through different dimensional profiles. The paper explicitly notes that countries differ in strengths and weaknesses across dimensions, so equal overall performance need not mean similar well-being structures. 

A fourth empirical point, drawn from the Better Life Index, is that among users who shared their indexes, life satisfaction, health, and education were the most highly rated dimensions on average. Figure 3 on page 14 reports these weights. The paper rightly notes that these responses are not representative of national populations. 

A fifth important result is forward-looking: the paper documents progress since 2011 in statistical work on wealth, decent work, health functioning, adult competencies, time use, environmental-economic accounting, subjective well-being, and sustainability, but also stresses that many measurement gaps remain. Section 6 is essentially a measurement agenda rather than a substantive welfare conclusion. 

# Main limitations

The most important limitation for your project is that the framework is non-behavioural. It does not model choices, constraints, opportunity sets, or welfare trade-offs structurally. Hence it cannot support the type of decomposition you want between realized bundle (z), preferences (R), feasible set (A), and pay schedule (y). 

A second limitation is that the framework is indicator-based and partly conventional. The choice of dimensions and indicators is reasoned and consultative, but not derived from a formal theorem or identified from behavior. This is unavoidable in many policy dashboards, but it matters for analytical rigor. 

A third limitation is aggregation. The paper itself acknowledges the difficulty of producing composite well-being indices and the loss of information involved in doing so. The dashboard solution preserves dimensional detail, but at the cost of yielding no single welfare ordering. 

A fourth limitation is measurement quality heterogeneity across dimensions. Income, jobs, housing, health, and education are statistically better measured than social connections, civic engagement, or some environmental and subjective dimensions. The paper is transparent on this point. 

A fifth limitation is that, although the framework is distribution-sensitive, it does not provide a formal decomposition of inequality into opportunities, preferences, circumstances, or responsibility-sensitive components. It is therefore not directly usable for your decomposition agenda. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is useful for broad framing if you want to motivate why well-being should not be reduced to GDP or current income alone. It provides a policy-recognized language for multidimensional well-being, subjective well-being, and sustainability. 

## possible use for model design

Direct relevance is limited. The paper does not offer a structural model of labour supply, discrete choice, or opportunity sets. Its main design lesson is conceptual: if your framework aspires to measure well-being rather than only behavior, you must be explicit about dimensions, aggregation, and sustainability. 

## possible use for identification

Very limited. The paper does not provide an identification strategy for preferences, opportunities, or welfare weights. At most, it clarifies the criteria by which indicators should be selected and compared internationally. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

Moderate relevance. It is useful as a broad multidimensional benchmark and as an example of how institutions operationalize well-being beyond income. It is not useful for deriving a formal individual welfare measure from choices or opportunity sets. 

## possible use for decomposition

Low relevance for your purposes. The paper discusses distributions across groups and avoids composite over-aggregation, but it does not decompose welfare inequality into opportunities, preferences, or pay schedules. 

## possible use for comparative application

High relevance. The framework is explicitly designed for international comparisons and benchmarking. If part of your longer-run agenda involves comparing welfare across countries or regions, this paper is useful as a high-level comparative reference. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

Can a multidimensional OECD-style well-being dashboard be reconciled with an individual-level structural welfare measure (W(z,R,A;y)) rather than remaining at the indicator level?

How would a capabilities-inspired multidimensional framework change if one measured actual feasible job sets (A_i) rather than achieved outcomes only?

Can subjective well-being and multidimensional dashboard indicators be linked formally to money-metric or opportunity-sensitive welfare measures in labour-supply settings?

What is lost, and what is gained, when moving from a multidimensional institutional dashboard to a single theoretically structured welfare measure?

Can cross-country comparative well-being frameworks be enriched with responsibility-sensitive decompositions that distinguish opportunities, preferences, and policy environments?

# Challenge to this paper

The main challenge is that the framework is normatively broad but analytically thin. It is persuasive as a public-statistics architecture, yet it stops short of providing a formal theory of individual welfare, of interpersonal comparison, or of opportunity-sensitive justice. For projects like yours, that makes it useful as a background reference but insufficient as an analytical foundation.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is largely orthogonal to the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction that matters for your JMP. It neither estimates preferences nor models opportunities as feasible sets. Instead, it measures achieved outcomes and supplements them with subjective assessments and a sustainability perspective. 

The one partial overlap is that the paper invokes capabilities and freedom, which signals concern for opportunities in a broad ethical sense. But this remains conceptual and indicator-based, not formalized as actual opportunity-set measurement. So the paper should not be read as a contribution to the empirical separation of preferences and opportunities.

# My quick takeaway

This is a useful background paper on multidimensional well-being measurement, especially if you want a recognized policy framework for going beyond GDP and current income. But it is not a core paper for the analytical heart of your project. It does not model labour supply, discrete choice, feasible job sets, or responsibility-sensitive welfare. Its main value for your corpus is as a high-level institutional benchmark on what broad well-being measurement looks like once one leaves the narrow domain of income-based metrics.
