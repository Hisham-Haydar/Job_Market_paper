# Full citation

Saez, Emmanuel. 2002. “Optimal Income Transfer Programs: Intensive versus Extensive Labor Supply Responses.” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 117(3): 1039–1073. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper shows that the optimal shape of low-income transfer programs depends critically on whether labor-supply responses are concentrated on the intensive margin or the extensive margin: the former favors a classical Negative Income Tax, while the latter can justify an Earned Income Tax Credit–type schedule with negative marginal tax rates at the bottom. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is one of the clearest formal statements of the idea that the *margin* of behavioral response is normatively decisive for transfer design. It does not merely say that elasticities matter. It says that *which elasticity* matters—participation versus hours/intensity—changes the sign and shape of optimal marginal tax rates at low earnings. That is a major result for any project concerned with tax-benefit design or welfare evaluation under labor-supply heterogeneity. 

For your project, the main value is not in explicit opportunity-set modeling, but in the sharp separation it draws between distinct behavioral channels and the policy schedules they rationalize. It is also useful because it introduces social marginal welfare weights (g_i) in a tractable way and explicitly discusses how views about whether the unemployed are “deserving” affect optimal policy. That makes it relevant for responsibility-versus-compensation questions, even though it does not develop a full axiomatic fairness theory. 

# Core research question

How should optimal income-transfer programs be designed when low-income labor-supply responses occur partly through labor-force participation and partly through hours or effort on the job, and what does that imply for the NIT-versus-EITC debate? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is situated in optimal income taxation and income-support design for low earners. It is motivated by the contrast between traditional Negative Income Tax schemes, common in Europe and in many categorical U.S. welfare programs, and the U.S. Earned Income Tax Credit, which subsidizes earnings at the bottom instead of giving the largest transfer to zero earners. Figure 1 on page 1040 presents this contrast graphically. 

The policy background is explicitly U.S.-relevant. The paper discusses the evidence that EITC expansions raised participation among low-income single mothers, while hours-of-work elasticities conditional on working are generally small. It then asks what type of transfer schedule is optimal once those empirical margins are built into a formal optimal-tax framework. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a welfarist optimal income-transfer model with discrete occupational choice and heterogeneous tastes for work. Individuals face a finite set of earnings outcomes (w_0=0,w_1,\dots,w_I), where (w_0) corresponds to unemployment and the higher (w_i) correspond to higher-skill jobs. The government observes realized earnings only and sets net taxes/transfers (T_i), so after-tax income is (c_i=w_i-T_i). The planner maximizes a weighted sum of individual utilities subject to a government budget constraint. 

The paper studies three related environments. In the pure extensive model, people choose only whether to work or not, so the relevant behavioral response is participation. In the pure intensive model, workers move between adjacent occupations as relative net rewards change, so the relevant behavioral response is effort or earnings intensity. In the mixed model, both channels are present. Section II develops all three cases formally. 

The framework is positive and normative at once. Positive behavior is summarized by labor-supply functions and elasticities. Normative evaluation is summarized by social marginal welfare weights (g_i), which represent the social value of an extra dollar to each earnings group. The paper emphasizes that the government redistributes only on realized earnings and ignores other observable characteristics for tractability, though it notes that actual U.S. policy often conditions on family status and related tags. 

# Key objects

The central objects are the after-tax incomes (c_i=w_i-T_i), the occupation shares (h_i), the government revenue requirement (H), and the marginal social welfare weights (g_i). The weights summarize redistributive tastes: the lower the earnings level, the higher the social value of one extra dollar, unless the government deliberately places a low weight on the unemployed. 

The key behavioral objects are the participation elasticity (\eta_i) in the extensive model and the mobility/intensive elasticity (\zeta_i), together with its mapping into the standard earnings elasticity (E_i). The entire analytical contrast between EITC-type and NIT-type programs runs through these elasticities. 

# Data

The paper is not an estimation paper, but it does perform numerical calibration. The income distribution is calibrated using annual individual earnings from the March 1997 CPS for nonstudent adults aged 18–60, with earnings defined as wage income plus self-employment income in 1996 dollars. Table III on page 1071 reports the discrete earnings grid and the associated density weights. 

The paper also uses elasticity ranges drawn from the empirical literature. For low incomes, the participation elasticity is allowed to vary across 0, 0.5, and 1, while the intensive elasticity for low incomes is allowed to vary across 0, 0.25, and 0.5. For middle and high incomes, the intensive elasticity is taken as 0.25 or 0.5. These calibration choices are described in Section III.2. 

# Identification logic

There is no identification strategy in the causal or quasi-experimental sense inside the paper itself. The theoretical results are analytical, and the quantitative results are calibrations based on externally chosen elasticities and an empirical earnings distribution. The paper relies on the empirical literature for plausible ranges of participation and intensive elasticities, especially evidence from NIT experiments and EITC expansions. 

More concretely, the calibration is disciplined by two inputs: the observed earnings distribution and a set of elasticities intended to be consistent with prior evidence. The paper explicitly presents this exercise as illustrative rather than definitive, precisely because the literature does not pin down the relevant elasticities with great precision for the full population. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy is calibrated simulation, not estimation. The paper first derives optimal-tax formulas for the pure extensive, pure intensive, and mixed models. It then numerically solves for optimal schedules under different combinations of participation elasticities, intensive elasticities, and redistributive tastes. Figures and tables summarize the resulting schedules. 

The key quantitative outputs are the guaranteed income, the average marginal tax rate over low-income and middle-income ranges, the break-even point, the top-end marginal rate, and the induced unemployment rate. Figure IV and Tables I–II on pages 1059–1061 are the central presentation devices. 

# Treatment of preferences

The paper does not estimate preference heterogeneity directly. Individual heterogeneity is present in the background through tastes for work and occupational choice, but the operational objects in the analysis are the aggregate labor-supply functions (h_i) and the elasticities derived from them. In that sense, preferences are present only through reduced-form behavioral responses. 

Normatively, the paper uses marginal social welfare weights (g_i), not preference-respecting fairness axioms. A particularly important point is that the weight on the unemployed, (g_0), is allowed to be higher or lower than the weight on low-wage workers depending on whether the government regards the unemployed as more needy or less deserving. This is not a revealed-preference treatment; it is an explicit normative input. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is important for your project, and the paper is useful but limited. It does not model opportunities as feasible job sets (A), latent job offers, or demand-side job availability. Opportunities appear only in a very stylized way through the discrete set of occupational earnings levels and the possibility of unemployment. In the pure extensive model, each individual may either work in the occupation corresponding to their skill or remain unemployed. In the pure intensive model, individuals move between adjacent occupations. In the mixed model, both channels operate. 

So the paper does model constraints and margins of choice, but not in a RURO or opportunity-set sense. There is no individual-specific feasible set of jobs observed or estimated, no latent job-offer distribution, and no explicit demand-side rationing. The government is assumed to observe only earnings and to condition taxes only on earnings, even though the paper notes that actual systems often use richer observable information. 

The paper therefore helps distinguish *behavioral margins* more than it distinguishes *preference heterogeneity* from *opportunity heterogeneity*. Its contribution is strongest on participation versus intensity, not on preferences versus opportunities. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly welfarist. The planner maximizes a weighted sum of individual utilities subject to a revenue constraint, and the redistribution motive is summarized by the marginal social welfare weights (g_i). The framework is therefore positive with explicit welfare optimization, not purely positive and not explicitly axiomatic in the social-choice sense. 

For your purposes, the most relevant normative feature is the treatment of the unemployed. The paper emphasizes that whether the unemployed are viewed as unable to work or as choosing not to work matters for their welfare weight (g_0). A conservative government might set (g_0<g_1), while a more liberal government might set (g_0>g_1). This is directly a responsibility-versus-compensation issue, although the paper does not formalize it as such. 

The paper does not provide a decomposition of inequality into opportunities, preferences, and related components. Nor does it build a well-being measure over bundles and feasible sets. Its welfare object is optimal transfer design, not inequality decomposition. 

# Main findings

The main theoretical finding is stark. In the pure extensive model, negative marginal tax rates at the bottom can be optimal, because subsidizing low earnings encourages labor-force entry. In the pure intensive model, optimal marginal tax rates are nonnegative, so redistribution takes the form of a classic NIT with a guaranteed income and positive phasing-out rates. The mixed model shows that the sign and size of low-income marginal tax rates depend on the relative size of participation and intensive elasticities. 

The paper’s benchmark simulations imply that when participation responses are substantial, the optimal transfer program combines a moderate guaranteed income with very low, zero, or even negative marginal tax rates at the bottom, and then substantial phasing-out rates further up the earnings distribution. In the paper’s own summary, for realistic elasticities the optimal program yields a moderate guaranteed income, a zero marginal tax rate at the bottom, and substantial phase-out rates farther up. 

The numerical exercises also show that stronger redistributive tastes raise the guaranteed income and can eliminate the EITC bubble. Table II shows this clearly: with high redistributive taste (v=4), guaranteed income remains high even when the participation elasticity is large, whereas with low redistributive taste (v=0.25), guaranteed income becomes very small and the EITC component is correspondingly more important. 

Finally, the paper argues that the combined EITC and U.S. welfare system for single mothers comes close to the benchmark optimal schedules if participation elasticities are indeed substantial, as the empirical literature suggests. This is one of the paper’s most policy-relevant conclusions. 

# Main limitations

The first limitation is that opportunities are extremely stylized. The occupational menu is discrete and fixed, and there is no explicit model of job availability, employer constraints, search frictions, or latent opportunity sets. For your (W(z,R,A;y))-type framework, this means the paper is much closer to a reduced behavioral model than to a model of opportunity sets (A). 

A second limitation is that the quantitative results are calibrated, not estimated from the paper’s own identifying design. The paper is explicit that the simulations are illustrative because there is no strong consensus on the relevant elasticities for the full population. 

A third limitation is the deliberate restriction to individual taxation based on earnings alone. The paper brackets secondary-earner decisions, joint household taxation, and richer tagging. It notes that these omissions matter empirically and theoretically, especially for family-based transfer design. 

A fourth limitation is that the welfarist objective is imposed rather than derived from a fairness theory. The socially controversial question of who deserves support is pushed into the choice of the marginal welfare weights (g_i), especially (g_0), but the paper does not provide an axiomatic resolution of that issue. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing if your JMP wants to argue that labor-supply behavior cannot be summarized by a single elasticity or a single distortion channel. It provides a crisp and influential argument that participation and intensive responses imply qualitatively different optimal policies. 

## possible use for model design

It is useful for model design on the behavioral side, especially if your project involves discrete labor-supply or occupational choices. The mixed model is not a full opportunity-set model, but it is a tractable template for embedding multiple labor-supply margins into policy design. 

## possible use for identification

Directly, the paper is limited here. It relies on external elasticity estimates rather than identifying them internally. Indirectly, it is useful because it clarifies exactly which elasticities matter for policy shape and therefore what an empirical project should prioritize estimating. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

Useful mainly as a transfer-design paper rather than a welfare-measurement paper. It shows how social marginal welfare weights can be combined with labor-supply elasticities to derive optimal schedules, but it does not construct a broader well-being index. 

## possible use for decomposition

Limited. The paper does not decompose welfare or inequality into preferences, opportunities, or policy components. It decomposes the *policy problem* into behavioral margins, which is conceptually useful but not a decomposition of inequality in your sense. 

## possible use for comparative application

Potentially useful for comparing tax-transfer design across institutional settings with different participation elasticities or labor-market structures. The paper itself hints that institutional features of labor markets affect the relative importance of extensive and intensive margins.

# Challenge to this paper

The sharpest challenge is that the extensive-versus-intensive distinction is behaviorally illuminating but ethically under-specified. The paper can generate very different optimal schedules depending on the welfare weight placed on the unemployed and on the magnitude of participation elasticities, yet both objects are treated as primitives. For a project interested in fairness and opportunity, that leaves open the deeper question of *why* unemployment should be treated as chosen, constrained, or compensated.

The paper is highly relevant to the distinction between different *behavioral* sources of response, but only weakly relevant to the distinction between opportunities and preferences as you formulate it. # Useful quotations / formulas

The key extensive-margin formula is
[
\frac{T_i-T_0}{c_i-c_0}=\frac{1}{\eta_i}(1-g_i).
]
This makes the EITC logic transparent: when the welfare weight on low-wage workers is above one, negative marginal tax rates at the bottom can be optimal. 

The key intensive-margin formula is
[
\frac{T_i-T_{i-1}}{c_i-c_{i-1}}
===============================

\frac{1}{\zeta_i h_i}
\Big[(1-g_i)h_i+\cdots+(1-g_I)h_I\Big].
]
This is the formula behind the result that marginal tax rates are nonnegative in the pure intensive model when social weights are decreasing with income. 

The mixed model formula is
[
\frac{T_i-T_{i-1}}{c_i-c_{i-1}}
===============================

\frac{1}{\zeta_i h_i}
\sum_{j=i}^I h_j\left[1-g_j-\eta_j\frac{T_j-T_0}{c_j-c_0}\right].
]
This is the most useful formula for your corpus because it shows exactly how participation responses enter the optimal-tax condition.

# My quick takeaway

This is a core paper for the behavioral side of low-income transfer design. Its decisive insight is that the optimal program depends on whether labor-supply adjustment occurs mainly through participation or through effort/intensity. For your project, it is very valuable as a bridge between structural labor-supply reasoning and normative policy design. Its weakness, relative to your framework, is that opportunities are not modeled as feasible sets and preference-opportunity separation remains largely unresolved.
