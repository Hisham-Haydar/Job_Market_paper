# Full citation

Fleurbaey, Marc. 1995. “Equal Opportunity or Equal Social Outcome?” *Economics and Philosophy* 11: 25–55. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper argues that the standard equal-opportunity / factor-selective egalitarian approach is theoretically unstable, often empty or inefficient, and too dependent on a problematic notion of responsibility, and it proposes instead an outcome-selective egalitarian framework centered on equality in “social outcomes” while preserving a private sphere. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is not merely another statement in favor of compensation or responsibility. It is a deep critique of the dominant “factor-selective” strategy itself. Instead of asking only which factors should be compensated and which should be left to responsibility, Fleurbaey asks whether this whole architecture is coherent. 

For your project, the paper is especially important because it directly interrogates equal opportunity, responsibility, compensation, separability, and the distinction between what society should equalize and what should remain private. It is therefore highly relevant to your axiomatic work, even though its opportunity concept is not yet the explicit feasible-job-set object (A) that you use. 

# Core research question

Should egalitarian justice be understood as equalizing opportunities by compensating for talents/circumstances while leaving will/choice to individual responsibility, or is a better approach to equalize only selected “social outcomes” and to treat the rest as belonging to a protected private sphere? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is entirely theoretical. It is situated in the “equality of what?” debate after Rawls, Dworkin, Sen, Arneson, Cohen, Roemer, and van Parijs. Its target is what Fleurbaey calls “factor-selective egalitarianism”: choose the relevant individual outcome (O), decompose its determinants into resources (r), talents (t), and will (w), then compensate for (t) but not for (w). 

The paper distinguishes two main schools within this general project. The “equal resources” school equalizes extended bundles ((r,t)) and typically locates (w) in personal goals and ambitions. The “equal opportunity” school equalizes opportunity sets and defines (w) by personal control and responsibility. Fleurbaey’s main critical focus is the equal-opportunity version, though many objections extend to factor-selective egalitarianism more broadly. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is axiomatic and normative. A comprehensive individual outcome is written (O(r,t,w)), where (r) denotes socially controllable resources, (t) denotes talents or circumstances to be compensated, and (w) denotes will or the dimension for which the individual is held responsible. Equal opportunity is defined as equality of the choice sets of attainable outcomes as the individual varies (w) over the admissible set (W). 

More formally, equal opportunity requires the sets ({(O_i,w_i)\mid O_i=O(r_i,t_i,w_i),,w_i\in W}) to be the same across individuals. Fleurbaey emphasizes that this is not a trivial requirement. It presupposes strong structural conditions on (O), especially a separability condition that effectively aggregates external resources and talents into a common “extended resource” bundle. 

The framework is entirely normative, not positive. There is no behavioral estimation, no empirical identification, and no observed feasible set. The paper evaluates rival principles of justice and responsibility by logical coherence, feasibility, efficiency, and moral plausibility. 

# Key objects

The central object is the comprehensive outcome function (O(r,t,w)). A first key distinction is between factor-selective egalitarianism and outcome-selective egalitarianism. The former sorts causal factors; the latter sorts outcomes themselves into socially relevant and private domains. 

A second key object is the separability condition
[
O(r,t,w)=\mu(v(r,t),w),
]
which is shown to be effectively required if equal opportunity is to be nonempty in general. This condition says that resources and talents must first be collapsed into a single extended-resource bundle (v(r,t)), after which outcome depends on (v) and (w). 

A third key set of objects are the distributional requirements RT, OW, and ERET. RT requires “more resource for less talent.” OW requires “better outcome for better will.” ERET requires “equal resource for equal talent.” These are used to expose the internal tensions of factor-selective egalitarianism, especially its conflict with efficiency and Pareto principles. 

A fourth key object is the proposed replacement architecture: split the vector of outcomes into
[
O=(O_1,O_2),
]
where (O_1) are “social outcomes” for which society bears responsibility and should seek equality, and (O_2) are “private outcomes” left to individuals and their close circles. In the concluding illustration, Fleurbaey suggests examples such as respect for the private sphere, health, education/information, wealth, collective decision-making power, and social integration as candidate social outcomes. 

# Data

There are no empirical data. This is a purely theoretical paper. The examples are stylized and illustrative, such as school performance, race models, resource allocation under heterogeneous talents, and the “Bert” motorbike case used to test the moral plausibility of equal opportunity. 

# Identification logic

There is no econometric identification. The relevant analogue is logical characterization and critique. The paper identifies structural conditions under which equal opportunity can be meaningfully defined, and it proves or argues that these conditions are highly restrictive. 

The crucial logical claim is that equal opportunity is generally nonempty only if the outcome function is separable in the specific way above. This means that the opportunity-equalization project does not really solve the problem of aggregating external and internal resources; it presupposes that this aggregation has already been done. 

A second key logical result is that even weakened factor-selective requirements such as ERET can conflict with strong or even weak Pareto optimality once resources are multidimensional or once outcome functions display richer dependence on resources, talents, and will. Identification here is therefore theorem-like rather than empirical. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

None. The strategy is conceptual decomposition, formal argument, counterexample construction, and normative diagnosis. The paper first defines the equal-opportunity approach, then derives the separability requisite, then studies efficiency/Pareto conflicts, then analyzes externalities and mutual interactions, then critiques the responsibility concept, and finally proposes outcome-selective egalitarianism as an alternative. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are not modeled explicitly as (R_i) in the way later Fleurbaey-Maniquet work does, but the paper is deeply concerned with the status of tastes, ambitions, goals, and will. In the Rawls–Dworkin line, (w) often corresponds to personal goals and ambitions. In the Arneson–Cohen line, (w) corresponds instead to what the individual genuinely controls and can be held morally responsible for. 

Fleurbaey is highly critical of the latter move. He argues that grounding distributive justice on genuine free choice is metaphysically fragile, practically unworkable, and morally crude. The free-will problem, the burden-of-proof problem, the evolution of personality through time, and the difficulty of assessing degrees of responsibility all undermine the equal-opportunity project. The Bert example is the paper’s main moral test case. 

In the alternative outcome-selective view, individual tastes and many subjective outcomes can remain in the private sphere, but not because they are fully controlled in the metaphysical sense. Rather, they remain private because of autonomy, privacy, plurality of life plans, and the value of preserving individual choice. This is a fundamentally different use of “responsibility.” 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is central. The paper is explicitly about equal opportunity, but its opportunity concept is not a job-set or menu-of-feasible-bundles concept in your sense. Opportunity is defined as the set of attainable outcome levels generated by variations in (w), holding the allocated resources and the individual’s talents/circumstances fixed. 

The paper therefore models opportunities abstractly as opportunity sets over outcomes, not as explicit feasible sets of jobs or occupations. It does not distinguish actual job availability, infeasible alternatives, wage schedules by job, or latent offers. That is a major difference from your (W(z,R,A;y)) architecture. 

Still, the paper is highly relevant because it critiques the whole equal-opportunity project at the normative level. It argues that equalizing opportunities across individuals usually requires unrealistic separability, fails under mutual interactions and externalities, and depends on an unstable concept of responsibility. Thus it is useful not for empirical modeling of (A), but for understanding the philosophical limits of opportunity-equalization as a justice principle. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative. Its object is distributive justice. It contrasts two broad approaches. The criticized approach is factor-selective egalitarianism, especially equal opportunity. The proposed alternative is outcome-selective egalitarianism: society should equalize only certain “social outcomes,” while leaving “private outcomes” outside the scope of egalitarian correction. 

This is directly relevant to responsibility versus compensation. The paper rejects the idea that society should sort the causes of disadvantage into morally compensable and noncompensable factors using a deep notion of free choice. Instead, it proposes that society decide which outcomes are of public concern and equalize those, while protecting a sphere of individual responsibility in the second, institutional sense. 

The paper therefore helps with responsibility for opportunities and compensation for outcomes, but not via reference opportunity sets or money-metric welfare measures. It is a structural normative critique and redesign of egalitarian reasoning. 

# Main findings

The first main finding is that equal opportunity is generally nonempty only under a separability condition of the form
[
O(r,t,w)=\mu(v(r,t),w).
]
This means that the equal-opportunity approach presupposes that resources and talents can already be aggregated into a single extended-resource bundle. It therefore does not truly solve that aggregation problem. 

The second main finding is that factor-selective egalitarian requirements such as RT, OW, and even the weaker ERET can conflict with Pareto optimality. In multidimensional or nonstandard environments, insisting that resource allocation ignore certain aspects of responsible choice can generate inefficiency or implausible allocations. 

The third main finding is that mutual interactions and externalities create a further difficulty for equal opportunity. Once one individual’s will affects another’s outcome, the ex ante definition of individual opportunity sets becomes unstable or even ill-defined. The race-on-separate-tracks picture behind equal opportunity is therefore a poor model of actual social life. 

The fourth main finding is that the responsibility notion used by equal opportunity is normatively and practically defective. The free-will problem, the problem of changing selves over time, the harshness of categorical no-compensation verdicts, and the impossibility of operationalizing degrees of responsibility all undermine the approach. The Bert example is the sharpest moral illustration. 

The fifth main finding is constructive. Fleurbaey proposes outcome-selective egalitarianism. Society should equalize social outcomes, preserve a private sphere, and interpret responsibility primarily in the institutional/decision-making sense rather than in the metaphysical sense of genuine free will. This alternative is presented as combining elements of Rawls and Sen while avoiding the mistakes of equal opportunity. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation, relative to your project, is that the paper’s formal objects are still abstract. It does not provide a fully developed wellbeing measure, and it does not move to explicit feasible-job-set modeling. Its opportunity concept is more philosophical than economic in your sense. 

A second limitation is that the positive alternative remains partly programmatic. Outcome-selective egalitarianism is persuasive conceptually, but the paper does not fully specify a general aggregation rule for multiple social outcomes. Fleurbaey explicitly notes that aggregation may be difficult or undesirable in some cases. 

A third limitation is that while the critique of equal opportunity is very strong, some parts rely on stylized examples and philosophical diagnosis rather than theorem-level characterization in every dimension. The paper is foundational rather than final. 

A fourth limitation is that the boundary between social and private outcomes is left partly to public debate. This is likely correct, but it means the theory does not uniquely determine the full content of justice. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is extremely useful for framing. It provides a sharp way to explain why a simple “compensate opportunities, respect choices” formula is not enough, and why the normative architecture must be more careful.

## possible use for model design

It is highly useful on the normative model-design side. It suggests that instead of sorting causal factors into responsibility and non-responsibility categories, one may want to sort outcome domains into social and private spheres.

## possible use for identification

Directly, it does not help with empirical identification. Indirectly, it is very useful because it warns that any empirical decomposition between choice and circumstance is normatively overburdened if it tries to do all the work of justice.

## possible use for welfare measurement

It is strongly useful conceptually. It does not give a scalar individual welfare measure, but it clarifies what kind of outcome space may legitimately enter a wellbeing or justice metric and what should remain private.

## possible use for decomposition

It is highly useful for normative decomposition. It criticizes factor-based decomposition and proposes an outcome-based decomposition between social and private outcomes.

## possible use for comparative application

Limited directly, because it is abstract. Strong indirectly, because it offers a normative map that can be applied across very different institutional contexts.

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is that the paper’s critique is more developed than its constructive formal replacement. It convincingly destabilizes equal opportunity and factor-selective egalitarianism, but the outcome-selective alternative remains only partially formalized. For your project, that is not a flaw so much as an invitation: the next step is to give this alternative a sharper individual wellbeing representation.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is centrally about opportunities versus outcomes, and indirectly about opportunities versus preferences. It is not a paper on preference heterogeneity in the Bargain et al. sense. Instead, it asks whether justice should compensate circumstances by equalizing opportunities or should focus on the equalization of selected outcomes. 

For your project, it is especially valuable because it cautions against treating “opportunities” as a morally self-sufficient object. Even if opportunities matter, the paper argues, the real normative task may be to decide which outcomes society should equalize and which should remain private.
