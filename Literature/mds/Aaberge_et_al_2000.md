# Full citation

Aaberge, Rolf, Ugo Colombino, and Steinar Strøm. 2000. “Labour Supply Responses and Welfare Effects from Replacing Current Tax Rules by a Flat Tax: Empirical Evidence from Italy, Norway and Sweden.” *Journal of Population Economics* 13: 595–621. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper uses structurally estimated joint labour-supply models for married couples in Italy, Norway, and Sweden to simulate labour-supply and welfare effects of replacing the existing progressive labour-income tax systems by revenue-neutral flat taxes, showing strong cross-country heterogeneity and a sharp decline in labour-supply responsiveness with pre-reform income. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is one of the clearest early cross-country structural exercises linking labour-supply estimation, tax reform simulation, and money-metric welfare analysis. It is not only about elasticities. It explicitly compares how the same type of tax reform—a revenue-neutral flat tax—plays out in three different institutional and behavioural environments. 

For your project, the paper is especially useful because it relies on a labour-supply framework where households choose among job packages and where opportunity sets vary across individuals. That makes it substantially more relevant than standard reduced-form flat-tax comparisons. It also studies the distribution of welfare gains and losses across households, not only mean effects. 

# Core research question

What are the labour-supply, distributional, and welfare consequences of replacing the country-specific current labour-income tax systems in Italy, Norway, and Sweden by revenue-neutral flat taxes, when behaviour is modeled with structural household labour-supply models that allow heterogeneous preferences and constrained job opportunities? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is a cross-country comparison for three European countries with different labour-market institutions and different degrees of tax progressivity. The substantive policy question is whether ongoing movements toward less progressive taxation can be justified on efficiency grounds, especially in a context of European integration and possible tax competition. 

The application is limited to married couples. The authors focus on this group because married women are considered more responsive to tax incentives than many other groups and therefore particularly informative for labour-supply reform analysis. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a structural discrete-choice / random-opportunities labour-supply model in which households choose among discrete “job packages.” A job package is characterized by wage rates, hours of work, and other non-pecuniary job attributes. The household maximizes utility over these job packages subject to the tax-based budget constraint. 

This is not a standard continuous-hours Hausman-style model. The paper stresses four distinguishing features: flexible utility functional forms, exact representation of tax and transfer systems, restrictions on offered hours in the market, and joint decisions of married couples. Quantity constraints are central. Not every hours level is equally available, and opportunity sets differ across households. 

The framework is positive with welfare applications. Positively, it models joint household labour supply under taxes and institutional constraints. Normatively, it evaluates tax reforms using equivalent variation at the household level. It does not construct an explicit fairness theory, social welfare ordering, or axiomatic well-being measure. 

# Key objects

The main economic objects are the household utility function over consumption and spouses’ leisure, the tax function mapping gross to disposable income, the opportunity densities over wage offers and offered hours, and the household-level equivalent variation used to measure welfare change. 

A central modelling object is the opportunity density for job packages. Offered wages and offered hours are not treated as universally available. Instead, the set of feasible opportunities is represented statistically, and this opportunity structure is allowed to differ across households. 

The main welfare object is equivalent variation. It is computed at the household level and interpreted as the amount of money that would have to be added to or subtracted from disposable income under the initial tax regime to make the household indifferent between the initial and alternative tax system. 

# Data

The paper estimates country-specific labour-supply models on household microdata for married couples from Italy, Norway, and Sweden. The data years are 1987 for Italy, 1986 for Norway, and 1981 for Sweden. Households with self-employment income above 20% of total gross income are excluded. For the retained households, self-employment and capital income are added to net household income and treated as exogenous. Spouses are restricted to ages 20–68. 

The simulation exercise itself is performed for the 1992 tax systems. For Norway, 1992 population characteristics are used in the simulations. For the other two countries, the characteristics used in estimation are also used in simulation. This distinction matters for interpretation. 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural and predominantly parametric. The paper does not use quasi-experimental variation. The model is identified through cross-household variation in observed wages, hours, demographics, education, and tax schedules, together with maintained assumptions on the functional form of utility and on the opportunity densities for offered wages and hours. 

The identification of labour-supply responses is therefore inseparable from the model structure. Because both preferences and opportunities are partly unobserved, elasticities and welfare effects are obtained by stochastic simulation rather than by design-based causal variation. # Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper estimates separate structural labour-supply models for each country and then uses those models to simulate behaviour under the actual 1992 tax systems and under revenue-neutral flat taxes. The simulation is stochastic because choice opportunities and some preference components are random from the econometrician’s point of view. 

Labour-supply elasticities are derived by stochastic simulation from a 1% increase in wages. Policy simulations are performed by drawing wage rates and job opportunities from the estimated distributions, solving the household’s utility-maximization problem under alternative tax regimes, and then computing labour supply, income, taxes, and equivalent variation. 

The flat-tax rates are calibrated so that tax revenue equals revenue under the current 1992 systems. The paper reports that the resulting proportional rates are about 24% in Italy, 25% in Norway, and 29% in Sweden. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are explicitly heterogeneous. The structural utility functions differ across countries and allow household behaviour to depend on consumption, leisure of both spouses, age, and other household characteristics. Unobserved heterogeneity is also present, since some components of utility vary randomly from the econometrician’s perspective. 

However, the paper’s treatment of preferences is behavioural rather than ethical. Preferences matter because they shape labour-supply responses and therefore welfare changes under alternative tax rules. The paper does not ask whether preference heterogeneity should be respected, neutralized, or held fixed in normative evaluation. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is one of the paper’s strongest sections for your purposes. Opportunities are not assumed universal. Households face job packages characterized by wage rates, hours, and other attributes, and the opportunity sets differ across individuals. The model explicitly incorporates restrictions on offered hours and random opportunity sets. 

The paper is clear that labour-market rigidity matters. Offered hours are drawn from market distributions with country-specific structures. In particular, Italian labour-market rigidity is treated as stronger, while Norwegian labour markets are modeled as more flexible by European standards. Swedish and Italian hours distributions contain institutional peaks, and these institutional differences help explain the cross-country labour-supply responses. 

Thus, the paper helps distinguish both preference heterogeneity and opportunity heterogeneity on the positive side. It does not assume a universal feasible set. It does use hours restrictions and latent job opportunities. It therefore does not ignore demand-side or institutional constraints.

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is positive with welfare applications. Welfare is measured by household-level equivalent variation rather than by estimated utility levels directly. The authors explicitly note that the model allows the distribution of welfare gains and losses to be studied, and they emphasize the importance of heterogeneity in these welfare effects. 

This is not an explicit theory of responsibility versus compensation. The welfare analysis is money-metric and household-based. The paper does not distinguish morally relevant and irrelevant sources of inequality in the manner of Roemer or Fleurbaey-Maniquet, nor does it define actual versus reference opportunity sets. 

It is also not a decomposition paper in your sense. It studies the distribution of gains and losses from tax reform, but it does not decompose inequality into opportunities, preferences, and pay schedules. It is more useful as an empirical welfare-simulation paper than as a decomposition paper. 

# Main findings

The first major finding is that female labour supply is more responsive than male labour supply in all three countries, but responsiveness declines sharply with pre-reform disposable income. In all three countries, the poorest households have much larger elasticities than the richest households. 

The second main finding is that the cross-country labour-supply effects of a flat tax differ substantially. Labour-supply responses are strongest in Norway, especially for women, and weaker in Italy and Sweden. The paper attributes this to differences in tax systems, labour-market institutions, and opportunity sets, including hours rigidities. 

Third, the efficiency costs of the current systems relative to a flat tax are found to be rather high in Norway and much lower, though still positive, in Italy and Sweden. Mean equivalent variation is positive in all three countries, implying average efficiency gains from moving to the flat tax. 

Fourth, distributional effects are not uniform across countries. The paper finds only minor changes in inequality in Italy, increased disposable-income inequality in Sweden, and a reduction in disposable-income inequality in Norway because the strong labour-supply response among poorer households more than offsets the reduced progressivity of the tax system. 

Fifth, the distribution of welfare gains and losses is highly heterogeneous. Rich households tend to benefit more than poor households in welfare terms in all three countries. A majority of households are predicted to lose from the flat tax in Italy and Sweden, whereas a majority are predicted to win in Norway. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that the analysis is restricted to married couples. Singles are not included, and the welfare conclusions are therefore not population-wide. This matters for external validity and for broader policy interpretation. 

A second limitation is that the policy simulations are partial-equilibrium. Offered wage and hours opportunity densities are treated as exogenous and unaffected by the tax reform, although the total number of jobs is assumed to move with labour supply. The authors explicitly note that the results might differ in a general-equilibrium framework. 

A third limitation concerns welfare interpretation. Equivalent variation is used at the household level, but the paper itself notes that money-metric welfare comparisons require controversial assumptions about cardinalization and interpersonal comparability. It also notes that, under an ordinal framework, one can still identify winners and losers, but not compare magnitudes of welfare changes across households. 

A fourth limitation is that the paper does not provide a direct decomposition of welfare changes into preference, opportunity, and tax-schedule components. # Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is highly useful for framing a structural empirical project on jobs, taxation, and welfare because it shows that cross-country differences in tax effects cannot be understood without modelling both preferences and opportunity structures.

## possible use for model design

It is very useful for model design. It operationalizes job-package choice, random opportunity sets, and hours restrictions in a way that is much closer to RURO-style modelling than standard labour-supply models.

## possible use for identification

It is useful mainly as an example of structural identification and internally consistent counterfactual simulation. It is less useful for causal identification in the modern reduced-form sense.

## possible use for welfare measurement

It is useful because it studies the full distribution of equivalent variation rather than only mean welfare effects. That is an important step toward the kind of individual-level evaluation you care about, even though its welfare concept is still money-metric and household-based.

## possible use for decomposition

Directly, limited. Indirectly, strong. The paper already contains separate preference and opportunity ingredients, so it suggests a route toward decomposition once a more explicit normative framework is imposed.

## possible use for comparative application

It is especially useful comparatively. The same reform is simulated in three countries under a harmonized modelling strategy, which makes it a good benchmark for thinking about how institutions and opportunity structures mediate reform effects.

Can one decompose the equivalent-variation gains from a tax reform into components driven by the tax mapping (y), by preference heterogeneity (R), and by opportunity heterogeneity (A)?

Would the rank ordering of winners and losers change materially if welfare were measured over income and leisure using a common or preference-respecting welfare metric rather than household EV?

# Challenge to this paper

The main challenge is that the paper’s welfare conclusions are stronger than its ethical foundations. It models opportunities and behaviour carefully, but its welfare analysis relies on equivalent variation, which the authors themselves acknowledge is controversial because it presupposes specific cardinal and comparable utility representations. For your project, that means the behavioural side is strong, but the normative side remains under-theorized.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is very useful for the positive side of the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction. It does not collapse all heterogeneity into tastes. Instead, it explicitly models both heterogeneous preferences and heterogeneous opportunity sets. That is a major reason it remains relevant. 

But it does not transform that distinction into a normative theory. It does not ask whether bad opportunities should be compensated while preference differences should be respected or held fixed. Thus it is best seen as a strong empirical precursor to your framework rather than as a finished solution to the opportunities-versus-preferences problem. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

A central modelling formula is the labour-supply probability density in which the choice probability depends on the structural utility term and the opportunity density for feasible job packages. This is the core formal device that makes the paper relevant for latent-opportunity modelling. 

A central welfare object is equivalent variation, defined as the money amount that makes the household indifferent between the initial and alternative tax regimes. This is crucial because the paper’s welfare results are entirely built on this money-metric comparison. 

A central empirical regularity is that labour-supply elasticities decline sharply with pre-reform disposable income, and that the strong response of poor households can partly offset the disequalizing effects of a flatter tax schedule, as in Norway.
