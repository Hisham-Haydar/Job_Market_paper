# Full citation

Aaberge, Rolf, John K. Dagsvik, and Steinar Strøm. 1995. “Labor Supply Responses and Welfare Effects of Tax Reforms.” *The Scandinavian Journal of Economics* 97(4): 635–659. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper develops and estimates for Norway a structural labour-supply model with heterogeneous opportunity sets and non-pecuniary job attributes, and uses it to evaluate the behavioural, income-distribution, and welfare effects of replacing the 1979 progressive income tax by either a revenue-neutral proportional tax or the 1992 tax system. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is an early and foundational contribution in the Aaberge–Dagsvik–Strøm line that explicitly models labour supply as choice among feasible job packages rather than as unconstrained choice over hours on a budget line. For your research direction, this is important because the paper makes opportunity sets part of the positive model rather than treating all heterogeneity as preference heterogeneity. 

It also matters because the paper does not stop at labour-supply elasticities. It moves to welfare evaluation using equivalent variation derived from indirect random utilities. This makes it directly relevant for the bridge between structural labour-supply estimation and individual welfare measurement, even though it does not formulate an explicit (W(z,R,A;y)) framework. 

# Core research question

How large were the labour-supply distortions created by the Norwegian progressive wage-income tax system of 1979, and what are the behavioural, distributional, and welfare consequences of replacing that system by either a revenue-neutral proportional wage tax or the actual 1992 tax system? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper studies Norway, with particular attention to the movement during the 1980s and early 1990s toward less progressive taxation of wage income. The benchmark system is the 1979 Norwegian tax system, when top marginal tax rates were extremely high by later standards. The reforms considered are a hypothetical proportional tax and the actual 1992 tax rules. 

The broader context is the Scandinavian welfare-state debate on the trade-off between redistribution and efficiency. The authors explicitly ask whether lower marginal tax rates reduce distortions enough to justify movement toward proportional taxation, and whether the income-distribution and welfare consequences differ depending on whether one looks at disposable income or welfare. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a structural random-utility labour-supply model in which labour supply is represented as a discrete choice among feasible “job packages.” Each job package is characterized by hours of work, wage rates, and non-pecuniary attributes. The model allows for non-convex budget sets, joint decisions by husbands and wives, and heterogeneous opportunity sets. 

The paper is explicit that this framework differs from conventional labour-supply models in which consumption and leisure are the only choice variables. Here, agents may have preferences over qualitative job characteristics, and quantity constraints in the labour market are recognized through differential availability of jobs across workers. This means the feasible set is not universal and depends on market opportunities and worker qualifications. 

The framework is positive with welfare applications. Positively, it models household labour-supply choices under taxes and labour-market opportunity constraints. Normatively, it evaluates tax reforms using equivalent variation at the household level and studies the distribution of welfare gains and losses. It is not an axiomatic fairness model. 

# Key objects

The main behavioural objects are the household utility function over consumption, male hours, female hours, and job attributes; the tax function (f) mapping gross income into after-tax income; and the opportunity sets (B_i(h,w)) of feasible jobs for each worker. The job-choice probabilities depend on both the structural utility term (\psi(\cdot)) and the opportunity-density terms (g(h,w)), (g_0), and their couple analogues. 

The key welfare object is equivalent variation (EV), defined as the amount of non-taxable income that would have to be added under the initial tax system to make the household indifferent between the initial and alternative tax systems. EV is therefore a household-specific money-metric welfare change. 

The main policy objects are the 1979 tax system, a revenue-neutral proportional tax on wage income, the 1992 tax system, and lump-sum taxes used as a non-distortive benchmark. 

# Data

The model is estimated on Norwegian labour-market and tax-return data from 1979 for married couples. The paper repeatedly states that the empirical application concerns married couples and that the model is estimated on 1979 data for that group. Figures 1–3 compare observed and simulated distributions of female hours, male hours, and household consumption.

# Identification logic

Identification is structural and primarily parametric. The model is estimated from observed labour-supply outcomes, wages, taxes, and household characteristics, under assumptions on the distribution of the random utility components and on the structure of opportunity densities. There is no quasi-experimental identification strategy. 

A central identification issue in the paper is the separation of the systematic utility component from the opportunity-density component. The authors explicitly state that full identification requires additional assumptions about the distribution of offered hours. This is why they specify the opportunity density of hours as uniform except for peaks at part-time and full-time work. That is an important methodological point for your project. 

Thus, identification of opportunities is partial and model-dependent. The paper is very useful because it openly discusses that the structural term and the opportunity-density term cannot in general be separated without additional restrictions. # Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy is maximum-likelihood estimation of the joint labour-supply model for married couples. The deterministic utility function is parameterized flexibly in consumption and spouses’ leisure, with age, children, and a leisure-interaction term. Wage opportunity densities are modeled as lognormal, with means depending on schooling and experience, and hours opportunities are modeled with peaks at full-time for men and at part-time and full-time for women. Tables 1 and 2 report the main parameter estimates. 

Policy simulations are then performed in partial equilibrium. The distribution of feasible market and non-market opportunities is held fixed across tax reforms. The authors simulate the effects of switching from the 1979 rules to proportional taxation, to the 1992 system, and to lump-sum taxation. The proportional rate is chosen to keep tax revenue constant relative to the 1979 regime. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled explicitly and flexibly through the deterministic part of utility, which depends on household consumption, male leisure, female leisure, age, children, and spouse interaction. The model also includes unobserved heterogeneity through random taste shifters over job opportunities. 

However, the paper does not use heterogeneous preferences as the direct welfare object for social evaluation. Instead, it derives household-specific EV from indirect random utilities. Thus, preferences are central for behaviour and for welfare calculation, but the paper does not turn preference heterogeneity into an explicit normative criterion about what should be respected or neutralized. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is one of the strongest papers in your corpus for the positive treatment of opportunities. The paper explicitly models labour supply as choice among feasible job packages. Jobs differ by wage, hours, and non-pecuniary attributes, and different types of jobs are not equally available to each agent. Opportunity sets differ because qualifications differ and jobs require different qualifications. 

The paper therefore does not assume a universal common choice set. Nor does it assume that workers freely choose any hours value on a budget line. Instead, the labour-supply density is a function of both the structural utility term and an aggregate representation of feasible opportunities. This is very close, on the positive side, to an (A)-aware framework. 

The hours-opportunity densities are modeled as mostly uniform with peaks at institutionally salient hour bundles. This means opportunity heterogeneity is treated explicitly but parametrically. The paper therefore helps distinguish both preference heterogeneity and opportunity heterogeneity, but it does not directly observe or normatively evaluate actual set-valued feasible sets. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is positive with explicit welfare applications. Its welfare object is household-level equivalent variation. EV is defined as the amount of non-taxable income that would make the household indifferent between the initial and reformed tax systems. This welfare measure incorporates both consumption and leisure changes caused by behavioural responses. 

The paper is careful to emphasize heterogeneity. Because the model includes observed and unobserved heterogeneity, EV is a random variable across households, and the authors study its distribution rather than only its mean. This is an important methodological advance over representative-agent or homogeneous-welfare approaches. 

The paper is not explicitly normative in the responsibility-versus-compensation sense. It does not ask whether poor opportunities should be compensated or whether some labour-supply differences should be treated as responsibility. Nor does it define reference opportunity sets. On decomposition, it does not decompose inequality into opportunities, preferences, and pay schedules, although it does compare welfare, gross income, and disposable income distributions. 

# Main findings

The headline result is that replacing the 1979 Norwegian wage-income tax system by a revenue-neutral proportional tax generates large behavioural responses, especially among low-income households and especially among married women. Table 4 reports that female participation rises from 74.2% to 84.3%, male participation from 93.7% to 99.5%, female annual hours from 1,043 to 1,642, male annual hours from 2,132 to 2,790, and gross household income from NOK 136,279 to NOK 208,449. 

The mean equivalent variation from this proportional-tax reform is NOK 25,956, equal to 63.8% of 1979 tax revenue. This is very large. The authors interpret this as evidence that proportional taxation would remove a substantial part of the labour-supply distortions created by the 1979 system. 

A striking result is that proportional taxation reduces inequality in disposable household income. Table 6 shows the Gini of disposable income falling from 0.161 under the 1979 rules to 0.152 under proportional taxation. This occurs because low-income households respond more strongly in labour supply than high-income households. 

But the welfare interpretation differs from the income-distribution result. The paper finds that rich households benefit much more in welfare terms than poor households, because the poor obtain more income mainly by greatly increasing work effort, while the rich enjoy large income gains with comparatively small increases in effort. This is one of the paper’s most important substantive findings. 

The 1992 reform yields much more modest welfare gains. Mean EV is only NOK 1,588, while mean tax revenue rises by about NOK 8,186 per household. The 1992 reform also increases inequality in gross and disposable income, and 43% of households lose from the reform while 57% gain. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that the policy simulations are partial equilibrium. The opportunity density is kept fixed, so the model assumes that supply creates its own demand. This is a major modelling restriction, especially if tax reforms would in reality affect the distribution of offered jobs or wages. 

A second limitation is that the opportunity densities are parametrically specified. The identification discussion itself acknowledges that full separation between the structural utility term and the opportunity-density term is not possible without additional assumptions. For your project, this means the paper is very helpful conceptually but does not nonparametrically recover (A). 

A third limitation is welfare interpretation. EV is household-based and money-metric. It provides a strong applied welfare measure, but the paper does not justify it through a deeper ethical theory of responsibility or compensation. # Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is highly useful for framing because it shows that a structural labour-supply model with explicit opportunity constraints can produce very different conclusions depending on whether one looks at disposable income inequality or welfare gains.

## possible use for model design

It is extremely useful for model design. It gives a clear formal template for treating jobs as packages with hours, wages, and non-pecuniary characteristics, and for representing feasible opportunities statistically rather than assuming a universal hours choice set.

## possible use for identification

It is useful because it explicitly discusses the identification problem between structural utility and opportunity density. That is highly relevant to any project that wants to separate preferences from opportunities.

## possible use for welfare measurement

It is very useful. The EV-based welfare analysis shows one way to move from a structural random-utility model to household-level welfare comparisons, while preserving behavioural responses and heterogeneity.

## possible use for decomposition

Directly limited. The paper does not give an (R/A/y) decomposition. Indirectly, strong, because it already separates the preference term from the opportunity-density term and shows that income-distribution and welfare-distribution conclusions can diverge.

## possible use for comparative application

Moderate. The paper is Norway-specific, but it is a foundational case for later Italy/Norway/Sweden comparisons and later optimal-tax applications by the same authors.

# Research questions this paper inspires

1. Can the paper’s opportunity-density representation (g_0 g(h,w)) be translated into an explicit feasible-set object (A_i) suitable for an individual well-being measure (W(z,R,A;y))?

2. How should one normatively evaluate cases in which a reform reduces income inequality but increases welfare inequality because poorer households must work much more to obtain their gains?

3. Can the structural separation between the preference term (\psi(\cdot)) and the opportunity-density term be strengthened enough to support decomposition of welfare inequality into (R)- and (A)-driven components?

4. How sensitive are the paper’s welfare conclusions to the partial-equilibrium assumption that the opportunity density is fixed under reform?

5. Would a reference-opportunity-set welfare measure rank the 1979-to-proportional-tax reform differently from EV?

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is conceptual. The paper successfully shows that income inequality and welfare inequality can move in opposite directions once labour-supply responses are modelled. But it leaves open the ethical question of how that divergence should be interpreted. If poor households gain income only by sacrificing much more leisure, should that be counted as a welfare improvement of the same kind as the gains enjoyed by rich households? The paper measures this through EV, but it does not provide a broader fairness theory of why this is the right evaluative lens.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is one of the strongest early positive contributions in the literature to the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction. It explicitly models opportunities through feasible job packages and opportunity densities rather than absorbing all labour-supply differences into tastes. 

At the same time, it does not convert that distinction into a normative theory of which inequalities should be compensated. So it is best read as a foundational empirical paper that creates the structure you would need before building an opportunity-sensitive well-being measure. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

A central formula is the labour-supply density
[
\varphi(h,w)=\frac{\psi(h,w)g_0g(h,w)}{\psi(0,0)+g_0\sum_{x>0}\sum_{y>0}\psi(x,y)g(x,y)},
]
or its continuous analogue. This is the paper’s clearest formal statement that realized labour supply depends jointly on preferences and opportunities. 

The household-level welfare object is equivalent variation, defined implicitly by
[
V_i(0,f_0)=V_i(EV,f_1),
]
where (f_0) is the initial tax system and (f_1) the reform. This is central because it is the paper’s core welfare metric. 

Table 4 is especially important. It reports that under proportional taxation female participation rises from 74.2 to 84.3 percent, male participation from 93.7 to 99.5 percent, and gross household income from NOK 136,279 to NOK 208,449, while mean taxes remain constant by construction.
