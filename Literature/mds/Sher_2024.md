# Full citation

Sher, Itai. 2024. “Generalized Social Marginal Welfare Weights Imply Inconsistent Comparisons of Tax Policies.” arXiv working paper, version dated June 25, 2024. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper proves that Saez–Stantcheva-style generalized social marginal welfare weights yield consistent comparisons of tax policies only when they have a structurally utilitarian form, and otherwise they imply social preference cycles.

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it targets a central methodological move in contemporary optimal-tax theory: replacing a global social welfare objective with local generalized welfare weights that supposedly encode broader ethical values. Sher’s result is that this move fails in general. That is directly important for any project that wants to incorporate fairness, responsibility, or opportunity-sensitive principles without losing coherence in policy comparison.

For your research program, the paper is especially valuable because it tells you something negative but foundational: one cannot generally represent broad ethical ideas merely by changing local weights on gains and losses. If your eventual (W(z,R,A;y)) framework is to be normatively rigorous, it likely needs an explicit global welfare object or an equally strong alternative, not just local marginal weights. # Core research question

Can generalized social marginal welfare weights provide a coherent way to evaluate tax policies while incorporating non-utilitarian ethical principles without relying on a global social welfare objective?

# Economic setting and context

The setting is a stylized optimal income tax model built on the Saez and Stantcheva framework. There is a continuum of agents (i\in[0,1]), each with consumption (c_i), income (z_i), observable characteristics (x_i), and unobservable characteristics (y_i). Tax policy is a function (T(z,x)), and agent behavior is modeled through utility maximization under the tax schedule. 

The context is not empirical and not country-specific. The paper is a theoretical contribution in normative public economics, directed at the foundations of tax-policy evaluation rather than at estimation or simulation of actual reforms.

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a theoretical optimal-tax model with quasilinear utility for most of the paper:
[
U_i(c_i,z_i)=u(c_i-v_i(z_i)),
]
where (v_i(z_i)=v(z_i,x_i,y_i)) is the cost of earning income (z_i), and tax policy determines consumption through (c_i=z_i-T_i(z_i)). Agents choose income optimally in response to a tax policy, and the paper studies how tax policies are normatively compared. 

The framework is explicitly normative. The positive side is minimal and stylized: agents optimize given taxes. The substantive object of study is the evaluation rule applied to tax reforms, specifically generalized social marginal welfare weights (g(c_i,z_i;x_i,y_i)), which assign a marginal social value to consumption for each agent as a function of outcomes and characteristics.

There is no explicit modeling of opportunities or feasible job sets in the sense relevant to RURO or jobs-and-opportunities models. The feasible set is just the tax-constrained income-consumption choice problem. The paper is therefore about the consistency of welfare evaluation, not about opportunity-set modeling. 

# Key objects

The main objects are tax policies (T), indirect utilities (U_i(T)), revenue (R(T)), and generalized social marginal welfare weights (g(c_i,z_i;x_i,y_i)). The paper also defines local welfare weights (g_i(T)) and develops implied global relations (\prec_g) and (\sim_g) over tax policies from these local comparisons.

The critical conceptual object is **structural utilitarianism**. In the quasilinear case, it requires that if two ((c_i,z_i)) bundles deliver the same utility or equivalently the same (c_i-v_i(z_i)), then they must receive the same welfare weight. Sher shows this property is necessary and sufficient for consistency of welfare weights.

# Data

There is no original dataset. The paper contains no empirical estimation, no country application, and no microsimulation exercise. All results are theorem-based within a stylized tax model.

# Identification logic

There is no econometric identification. The paper’s logic is analytical: it starts from the local-comparison claims of generalized welfare weights, defines the global comparisons those local claims implicitly commit one to, and then proves that non-structurally-utilitarian weights generate inconsistency through social preference cycles.

The crucial assumptions are smoothness of tax-policy families, standard optimization conditions for individual choice, and the adoption of the global improvement and global indifference principles that map local welfare-weight comparisons into global social judgements. If those principles are accepted, inconsistency follows outside the structurally utilitarian case.

# Estimation / empirical strategy

There is no estimation. The empirical strategy is absent because the paper is purely theoretical. Its method is formal theorem-proof analysis, with illustrative constructions involving individualized taxes, libertarian weights, and poverty-alleviation weights to show how inconsistency arises.

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are represented in the standard optimal-tax way through utility and indirect utility. For most of the paper, utility is quasilinear, and this is used to connect welfare weights, utility levels, and marginal utility of consumption. The paper later generalizes the core result beyond quasilinearity. 

Importantly, the paper does not study heterogeneity in tastes for its own sake. Preference structure matters because it determines what a coherent welfare-weight system must look like. The key claim is that if welfare weights vary in ways not anchored to utility structure, local comparisons cease to fit any consistent global ranking.

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative. Its object is not welfare measurement of realized lives in a broad sense, but the coherence of evaluating tax policies through generalized social marginal welfare weights. It asks whether one can dispense with a global social welfare objective and instead rely on local weighted assessments of tax changes.

Sher’s answer is that this works only when welfare weights arise from a generalized utilitarian social welfare function of the form
[
\int F_i(U_i),di,
]
with (F_i) an agent-specific increasing transformation. Otherwise, local weights imply inconsistent global comparisons. This is the paper’s central theorem-level conclusion.

The paper is not about responsibility for opportunities, compensation for opportunities, or decomposition of inequality in the sense central to your project. But it is highly relevant to the normative technology one may try to use for those purposes. In particular, it is a warning against trying to encode broad ethical principles solely through local marginal weights. [reasonable inference for my project] supported by

# Main findings

The first main finding is that local comparisons made by generalized welfare weights implicitly entail global comparisons among tax policies. Sher formalizes this by defining strict and weak relations (\prec_g) and (\sim_g) that summarize some of the global judgements implied by local welfare-weight reasoning.

The second main finding is that **structural utilitarianism** is necessary and sufficient for consistency. If welfare weights are structurally utilitarian, they can be generated by a generalized utilitarian social welfare function; if not, they imply preference cycles over tax policies.

The third main finding is the inconsistency theorem itself: if welfare weights are not structurally utilitarian, there exist equal-revenue tax policies (T_0,T_1,T_2,T_3) such that
[
T_0 \prec_g T_1 \sim_g T_2 \prec_g T_3 \sim_g T_0.
]
This is the formal statement that the generalized-weight approach can imply cyclic and hence inconsistent policy comparisons.

The fourth main finding is that prominent examples advanced as ethically attractive applications of generalized weights, including libertarian weights depending on taxes paid and poverty-alleviation weights depending on consumption or poverty status, fall into the inconsistent class.

# Main limitations

The main limitation for your JMP is that the paper does not model labor-market opportunities, job sets, or structural labor-supply environments beyond a very abstract income-choice setup. It therefore cannot directly help with empirical separation of preferences and opportunities. 

A second limitation is that the result is negative and methodological. It tells you what does **not** work, but it does not by itself deliver a complete alternative welfare framework. The paper points toward generalized utilitarian social welfare functions and more thoroughgoing revisions, but it does not construct a full jobs-and-opportunities welfare measure. 

A third limitation is that the analysis is conducted in a stylized tax model. That is appropriate for the theorem, but it leaves open how the argument interacts with richer structural models involving extensive-margin work, hours, occupations, latent offers, or multidimensional job characteristics. # Useful quotations / formulas

A central formula is the quasilinear utility specification:
[
U_i(c_i,z_i)=u(c_i-v_i(z_i)).
]
This matters because structural utilitarianism and the equivalence between utility, marginal utility, and welfare weights are first developed in this environment. 

The key characterization result is that structurally utilitarian welfare weights are exactly those that can be generated by a generalized utilitarian social welfare function
[
\int F_i(U_i),di.

This is the paper’s central positive theorem.

The main inconsistency result is the cycle

T_0 \prec_g T_1 \sim_g T_2 \prec_g T_3 \sim_g T_0,
]
which shows that non-structurally-utilitarian generalized welfare weights imply inconsistent tax-policy comparisons.

# My quick takeaway

This is a high-value paper for the methodological core of your project. It does not help with latent jobs, feasible sets, or empirical decomposition. But it is extremely useful for disciplining the welfare layer of your analysis. Its main lesson is stark: once one goes beyond standard utilitarian weights, consistency is not automatic, and in fact generally fails unless the weights are generated by a global welfare structure. For a project like yours, that is a strong argument for building the global well-being measure first and deriving local policy comparisons from it, not the reverse.
