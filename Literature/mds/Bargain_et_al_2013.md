# Full citation

Bargain, Olivier, André Decoster, Mathias Dolls, Dirk Neumann, Andreas Peichl, and Sebastian Siegloch. 2013. “Welfare, labor supply and heterogeneous preferences: evidence for Europe and the US.” *Social Choice and Welfare* 41: 789–817. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper estimates harmonized discrete-choice labor supply models for married women in 11 European countries and the US, then uses the estimated consumption-leisure preferences to compare alternative individual welfare metrics that explicitly retain preference heterogeneity rather than assuming identical tastes. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it directly addresses a central problem in your project: how to evaluate well-being when individuals differ in preferences. The paper is not about opportunities in the feasible-set sense, but it is one of the most relevant empirical papers for the welfare-measurement side of your framework because it moves beyond income and beyond representative-agent assumptions. 

It is especially important because it shows empirically that welfare rankings across countries can change substantially depending on the normative treatment of heterogeneous preferences. This is precisely the type of issue that becomes central once one refuses to collapse welfare into income only or into a common reference preference. 

# Core research question

How should one compare individual well-being across countries when welfare depends on both consumption and leisure and when individuals have heterogeneous preferences over that trade-off? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is an international comparative exercise covering Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands, Portugal, Sweden, the United Kingdom, and the United States. It is motivated by the “beyond GDP” agenda after the Stiglitz Commission and by recent social-choice work on welfare comparisons under heterogeneous preferences. 

The empirical focus is narrow but deliberate. The authors estimate labor supply only for married women, arguing that this group exhibits substantial variation in hours and participation and is therefore especially useful for identifying consumption-leisure preferences in a comparable way across countries. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a structural discrete-choice labor supply model with random utility. Households choose among discrete hours alternatives for married women, while husbands’ labor supply is fixed. Utility depends on household net income and female leisure, with observed heterogeneity through taste shifters and unobserved heterogeneity through extreme-value random terms. 

The framework is positive in its estimation stage and normative in its welfare-comparison stage. Positively, it estimates country-specific and household-specific consumption-leisure preferences from labor supply behaviour under national tax-benefit rules. Normatively, it computes alternative individual welfare metrics based on those estimated indifference curves and compares how these metrics rank households across countries. 

The paper does not model opportunities or feasible job sets explicitly. The choice environment is the standard discrete-hours labor supply setting with tax-benefit constraints, not a RURO or latent-job framework. Demand-side rationing, job availability, and non-pecuniary job attributes are not part of the analysis. This is crucial for locating the paper relative to your project. 

# Key objects

The main positive objects are household net income, female labor hours, leisure, gross wages, non-labor income, and the estimated utility parameters of the Box–Cox consumption-leisure utility function. The key behavioural object is the marginal rate of substitution between consumption and labor time, which the authors use to summarize preference heterogeneity across households and countries. Table 2 is central here. 

The key normative objects are the three welfare metrics derived from fair-allocation theory: the “wage” metric, the “rent + reference wage” metric, and the “rent” metric. They all respect preference heterogeneity but differ in how they normatively treat differences in willingness to work. Figure 1 on page 6 graphically illustrates these metrics. 

The empirical ranking object is the percentile position of each household in the global welfare distribution. The paper emphasizes pure orderings of individual well-being rather than any social welfare function. Tables 3 and 4 report the resulting cross-country rankings. 

# Data

The paper uses harmonized microdata for 11 European countries and the US. For EU countries, the underlying data correspond to either 1998 or 2001 depending on the country and are paired with EUROMOD simulations; for the US, the paper uses 2006 IPUMS-CPS data referring to year 2005 and computes taxes and transfers with TAXSIM. Table 1 reports average net income, non-labor income, female wages, female hours, and participation rates by country. 

The sample is restricted to married couples in which husbands work at least 30 hours per week. Women are aged 18–59 and are excluded if disabled, retired, or in education. The full sample contains 42,975 households. 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural. The labor supply model is identified by variation in net income across discrete hours alternatives generated by national tax-benefit systems, together with variation in wages, non-labor income, and demographic taste shifters. The paper explicitly relies on the standard structural identification logic of discrete labor supply models rather than on quasi-experimental variation. 

The crucial identification target is not a treatment effect but the shape of consumption-leisure preferences. Once those are estimated, the paper derives indifference curves and then computes the welfare metrics. So the identifying assumptions that matter most are functional-form assumptions on utility, comparability of the hours discretization across countries, and accurate tax-benefit simulation. # Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy has two steps. First, the authors estimate a common structural discrete-choice labor supply model separately for each country, using a Box–Cox utility function over consumption and leisure. Heterogeneity in preferences is introduced through household-specific taste shifters, while tax-benefit calculators are used to compute net income at each discrete hours point. 

Second, they derive individual indifference curves from the estimated preferences and compute the welfare metrics numerically. They then pool all households internationally and compare their percentile positions under each metric. Figure 2 on page 17 shows rank correlations across metrics, while Figure 3 on page 18 illustrates the cumulative distributions for two selected countries. Tables 3–6 contain the main comparative results and decompositions. 

# Treatment of preferences

This is the central contribution of the paper. Preferences are not treated as nuisance heterogeneity to be eliminated. Instead, the paper estimates them explicitly and then asks how welfare comparisons change when this heterogeneity is retained rather than suppressed. 

The paper contrasts the standard reference-preference approach, where all households are evaluated using the preferences of a fixed reference household, with fair-allocation metrics that fully respect heterogeneous preferences. This distinction is the theoretical core of the paper and is the reason it is so relevant for your welfare-measurement agenda. 

The authors also decompose variation in marginal rates of substitution and conclude that the differences in welfare rankings are driven mainly by genuine cross-country differences in work preferences rather than by demographic composition. This is one of the paper’s key empirical claims. Tables 5 and 6 are central for that result. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

The paper does not model opportunities explicitly in the feasible-set sense. There is no set-valued opportunity object, no latent job distribution, and no direct account of labour-demand restrictions. The budget set is determined by tax-benefit rules and wages, but the paper is not about opportunities as a separate welfare-relevant object. 

As a result, the paper helps distinguish preferences from income-only welfare metrics, but it does not help much with distinguishing preferences from opportunities. # Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative. Its core normative object is individual well-being measured in the consumption-leisure space under alternative interpersonal welfare metrics. It does not aggregate these metrics into a social welfare function; it focuses on pure individual orderings instead. 

The main normative issue is how to treat heterogeneous preferences. The three metrics all respect preference heterogeneity but differ in whether they tend to favour relatively industrious individuals or relatively work-averse individuals. The paper explains this as a tension between compensation and liberal reward. This is one of the most relevant normative discussions in your corpus for thinking about responsibility-sensitive welfare measurement. 

The paper is not about responsibility for opportunities or compensation for opportunity sets. It is about welfare comparison under heterogeneous tastes. Nor is it a decomposition paper in your sense: it does not decompose welfare inequality into opportunities, preferences, and pay schedules. It does, however, decompose differences in welfare rankings into preference parameters versus demographic composition. 

# Main findings

The first main finding is that once heterogeneous preferences are fully respected, the ranking of households across countries depends strongly on the chosen welfare metric. Tables 3 and 4 show substantial reranking, and the paper highlights average changes in percentile positions of at least 15 percentage points for 7 out of 12 countries when moving from one extreme metric to another. 

The second main finding is substantive and comparative. Countries with relatively high female work attachment, such as the US and Nordic countries, rank better under metrics that tend to favour households with a higher willingness to work. Countries with relatively lower female labour supply, such as Austria, Ireland, Germany, and the Netherlands, rank better under metrics more favourable to lower willingness to work. Tables 3 and 4 and Figure 3 illustrate this. 

The third main finding is that these ranking differences are driven primarily by estimated cross-country preference heterogeneity rather than by differences in demographic composition. Table 5 shows that country-specific preference parameters explain much more of the rank-correlation changes than age, education, or children alone, and Table 6 confirms that demographic differences by themselves generate only small shifts in average rank positions. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that the paper does not model opportunities separately from preferences. This is the most important limitation relative to your (W(z,R,A;y)) framework. Since the labor supply model is standard discrete choice, some country differences in actual opportunity conditions could be picked up as taste differences. 

A second limitation is the narrow population focus. The analysis is restricted to married women in couples with husbands working at least 30 hours, so the welfare rankings are not representative of full-country populations. This is a deliberate modelling choice, but it limits the scope of the empirical conclusions. 

A third limitation is dimensional. Welfare depends only on consumption and leisure in the empirical implementation. The paper itself notes omitted fixed costs of work, demand-side constraints, and public services as possible missing elements that could matter for welfare and for estimated preferences. 

A fourth limitation is that the paper does not produce a policy-evaluation object such as optimal taxation or a social welfare aggregate. It gives interpersonal rankings, which is useful, but it leaves open how these rankings should be socially aggregated. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is central for framing the welfare-measurement side of your JMP. It provides a direct empirical example of how welfare comparisons become normatively sensitive once preference heterogeneity is not assumed away.

## possible use for model design

It is moderately useful for model design. The labour-supply estimation framework is standard and useful if your empirical object remains the consumption-leisure trade-off, but it is not a model of explicit opportunity sets or latent jobs.

## possible use for identification

It is useful mainly as a structural identification example for preferences, not for opportunities. The paper shows how harmonized tax-benefit variation can be used to identify comparable labor-supply preference parameters across countries.

## possible use for welfare measurement

This is where the paper is extremely useful. It is probably one of the most relevant empirical papers in your corpus for thinking about reference metrics, interpersonal comparability, and how respecting preference heterogeneity changes welfare rankings.

## possible use for decomposition

It is moderately useful. It does not offer your (R/A/y) decomposition, but it does provide a decomposition of ranking differences into preference parameters versus demographic composition.

## possible use for comparative application

It is highly useful comparatively. The harmonized cross-country structure is one of the paper’s main strengths, and it offers a template for comparative welfare work that is more rigorous than simple income comparisons.

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is that the paper’s normative sophistication on preferences is not matched by equal sophistication on opportunities. It takes heterogeneous tastes very seriously, but it does not take heterogeneous feasible sets very seriously. For your research direction, that means it solves one half of the problem—interpersonal comparison under heterogeneous (R)—while leaving the treatment of (A) largely unresolved.

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

This is the benchmark paper itself. It should serve in your corpus as the main empirical reference on welfare measurement under heterogeneous consumption-leisure preferences. Relative to earlier Bargain work on rationing and making-work-pay policies, its novelty is the normative welfare-comparison layer rather than the treatment of demand-side constraints. Relative to the Aaberge–Colombino line, its main distinctive feature is that it explicitly embraces heterogeneous preferences in the welfare metric instead of replacing them with a common reference preference. 

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is much more useful for preferences than for opportunities. It is one of the clearest empirical demonstrations that the normative treatment of heterogeneous preferences materially changes welfare comparisons. In that sense, it is central for the “preferences” side of your project. 

But it says little about opportunities as feasible sets or job access. So it should not be read as a general solution to the opportunities-versus-preferences problem. It is better read as a benchmark for the case where (R) is taken seriously and (A) is largely abstracted from. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

A central theoretical contribution is the definition of the three welfare metrics. The “wage” metric is
[
\nu_i^W(u,\mu_r=0)=\min_{\tilde w_i}{\tilde w_i \mid v_i(\tilde w_i,\mu_r=0)\ge u},
]
the “rent + reference wage” metric is
[
\nu_i^{RW}(u,w_r)=e_i(u,w_r),
]
and the “rent” metric is
[
\nu_i^R(u,h_r=0)=c_i(u,0).
]
These are the core normative objects of the paper. 

The utility specification used in estimation is
[
u_i(c_{ij},T-h_{ij})=\beta_c\frac{c_{ij}^{\alpha_c}-1}{\alpha_c}+\beta_{li}\frac{(T-h_{ij})^{\alpha_l}-1}{\alpha_l},
]
with household-specific leisure taste shifters in (\beta_{li}). This is central because it is the empirical bridge from labor supply estimation to welfare metrics. 

Figure 1 on page 6 is especially useful. It visually shows how the “rent,” “rent + reference wage,” and “wage” metrics rank two individuals with crossing indifference curves differently. This figure is probably the single best visual summary of the paper’s normative logic.
