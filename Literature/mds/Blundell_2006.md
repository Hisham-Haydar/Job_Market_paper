# Full citation

Blundell, Richard. 2006. “Earned income tax credit policies: Impact and optimality.” *Labour Economics* 13(4): 423–443. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper evaluates the labour-supply impact of UK earned income tax credit reforms for lone parents and asks whether the resulting in-work transfer design is close to optimal once extensive and intensive labour-supply responses, tax-benefit interactions, and social welfare weights are taken into account. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it links three layers that are often kept separate: quasi-experimental impact evaluation, structural labour-supply modelling, and optimal-tax design. That combination makes it more useful than a purely descriptive policy paper or a purely theoretical optimal-tax paper. 

For your project, it is especially relevant as a bridge paper between structural labour-supply estimation and normative policy design. It does not model opportunities as feasible job sets, but it does show how detailed budget constraints, take-up, fixed costs, childcare costs, and extensive-margin responses can be embedded in a structural model and then used to assess “optimality.” 

# Core research question

What was the labour-supply impact of the UK Working Families’ Tax Credit for lone parents, why did its observed impact differ from the apparently more modest US EITC design, and can an earned income tax credit of this kind be justified as an optimal income-transfer policy for low-income families with children? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is set in the UK welfare-to-work reforms of the late 1990s and early 2000s, with particular emphasis on the Working Families’ Tax Credit (WFTC) introduced in 1999 as an expansion of Family Credit. The lecture places UK reforms in comparison with the US Earned Income Tax Credit and asks why the UK policy, which appeared more generous in gross terms, generated a smaller observed labour-supply response. 

The motivating social problem is low labour-market attachment and high poverty among parents with children, especially lone parents. The lecture also highlights the rise in workless couples with children in the UK and the policy importance of balancing child-poverty reduction against work incentives. Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 on pages 5–6 illustrate the broader labour-market background: falling employment among single mothers and a high share of workless couples. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The paper combines two frameworks. For impact evaluation, it uses both a structural labour-supply model and a quasi-experimental difference-in-differences approach. For normative analysis, it embeds the structurally estimated labour-supply responses inside an optimal-tax problem in which the government chooses tax schedule parameters to maximize a social welfare function subject to a budget constraint. 

The structural model is a stochastic discrete-choice labour-supply model with programme participation. Individuals choose among discrete hours ranges rather than a continuous hours distribution. The paper states that the model includes tax-benefit interactions, take-up or stigma costs, childcare costs, fixed work-related costs, and unobserved heterogeneity in preferences and programme participation. The hours categories are (0), (1\text{–}15), (16\text{–}22), (23\text{–}29), (30\text{–}36), and (37+). 

The feasible set is therefore a set of discrete labour-supply alternatives generated by the tax-benefit system and work-related costs. This is a budget-set framework, not an explicit opportunity-set framework. The model is positive when used to estimate labour-supply responses and normative when used to derive optimal tax schedules. 

# Key objects

The main positive objects are the household or individual budget constraints under Family Credit, WFTC, and the broader package of contemporaneous reforms; extensive and intensive labour-supply elasticities; take-up or stigma costs; childcare costs; and transition probabilities across hours states under reform. 

The key normative objects are the social welfare transformation function, the induced welfare weights, and the optimal tax schedule over earnings ranges. The paper specifies the transformation as a function (G(U)) indexed by a parameter (\theta), where more negative values of (\theta) correspond to greater concern for equality in utilities. The government chooses a schedule consisting of out-of-work income and marginal tax rates over several earnings regions. 

The central policy objects are the WFTC and EITC schedules themselves. Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 show the UK WFTC expansion and the WFTC–EITC comparison, while Fig. 5 and Fig. 8 show how interactions with other benefits flatten effective incentives for a typical lone parent. 

# Data

The difference-in-differences evaluation uses UK Family Resources Survey (FRS) data and Labour Force Survey (LFS) data spanning Spring 1996 to Spring 2003, with Summer 1999 to Spring 2000 excluded because that was the transition period around WFTC implementation. Individuals above age 45 are also dropped in the difference-in-differences exercise. 

The structural analysis relies on the FRS and on detailed tax-benefit microsimulation. The lecture states that the FRS is the key source because it measures income, hours, family composition, and other variables accurately enough to construct potential budget constraints. It contrasts this with the LFS, which is larger but less suitable for detailed structural modelling of taxes and benefits. 

# Identification logic

The paper uses two distinct identification logics. The first is quasi-experimental: a matching difference-in-differences design compares potentially eligible single mothers with single women without children, following the spirit of Eissa and Liebman (1996). The identifying assumptions are separability, common trends across groups, and invariance of group heterogeneity over time, conditional on matching covariates such as age, education, region, and ethnicity. 

The second is structural. The labour-supply model is identified from variation in taxes, benefits, housing costs, local taxation, childcare costs, and the detailed rules of the tax-benefit system. The paper is explicit that this relies on the maintained hypothesis that individuals perceive and respond to these budget-constraint features in the way the researcher models them. 

A major methodological claim of the paper is that the quasi-experimental estimates are used not as the main policy-design tool, but as a validation device for the structural model. This is important: the difference-in-differences design yields average impact effects, but not the full parameter set needed for policy simulation and optimality analysis. The structural model then provides the elasticities and behavioural responses required for optimal tax design. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy proceeds in stages. First, the paper estimates average employment effects of WFTC using a matching difference-in-differences approach. Table 3 reports employment increases of about 3.57 percentage points using the FRS and 3.81 percentage points using the LFS. 

Second, it estimates or imports a structural discrete-choice labour-supply model with unobserved heterogeneity, fixed costs, childcare costs, and take-up costs. The model is simulated over the full set of budget constraints generated by the tax-benefit system. The unobserved utility shocks at each hours choice are assumed Type-I extreme value, while other heterogeneity components are integrated out by simulation. 

Third, it simulates reform effects under two policy concepts: WFTC alone, and “all reforms,” where contemporaneous changes in income support and related benefits are included. This distinction is crucial for the interpretation of impact. Finally, the same structural model is used to solve for optimal tax schedules conditional on demographic group and government expenditure. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are represented through the structural labour-supply model. They include heterogeneity in the utility trade-off between consumption and hours, plus heterogeneity in take-up or stigma costs, childcare costs, and fixed work-related costs. The model thus goes materially beyond a simple static hours-choice equation. 

At the same time, preferences remain a behavioural object rather than an explicit welfare-theoretic object. The paper does not provide a direct ethical interpretation of heterogeneity in preferences. Instead, preferences are estimated for behavioural simulation, and then a separate social welfare function is imposed for the normative exercise. This separation is analytically important. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

The paper does not model opportunities explicitly as feasible job or ability sets. There is no latent offer distribution, no RURO-type opportunity density, and no direct treatment of unequal job access across individuals. This should be stated clearly: opportunities in the sense central to your (W(z,R,A;y)) framework are not explicit objects here. 

What the paper does model very carefully are institutional and financial constraints. These include budget-set nonconvexities, interactions between WFTC and housing benefit, take-up costs, childcare costs that vary with hours and demographics, and fixed costs of work. In that narrower sense, the paper is strong on constraints, but these are budget and participation constraints rather than opportunity sets. 

The paper therefore helps distinguish preference heterogeneity from budget-constraint heterogeneity, but not preference heterogeneity from opportunity heterogeneity in the job-set sense. It does not decompose observed disadvantage into preferences versus opportunities. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is both positive and explicitly normative. The positive part evaluates labour-supply impacts of reforms. The normative part asks whether an earned income tax credit can be justified as an optimal transfer policy for lone parents under plausible social welfare weights. 

The welfare object is not EV or CV, and not an individual well-being measure of the (W(z,R,A;y)) type. Instead, the government maximizes a social welfare function defined over transformed individual utilities, where the transformation parameter (\theta) determines inequality aversion. The tax schedule is then chosen subject to budget feasibility and behavioural responses from the structural model. 

This is therefore a genuine welfare-design exercise, but not one centered on responsibility for opportunities, compensation for unequal opportunity sets, or reference opportunity sets. It is closer to optimal tax analysis with structural behavioural responses than to fairness-based opportunity-sensitive welfare economics. 

# Main findings

The difference-in-differences estimates suggest that the WFTC reform package increased employment among single mothers by roughly 3.5 to 4 percentage points. Table 3 reports 3.57 percentage points using the FRS and 3.81 using the LFS. The paper also reports that low-education groups exhibit slightly larger responses from a lower initial employment base. 

The structural simulations show that WFTC alone would have produced a larger labour-supply effect than the full set of contemporaneous reforms actually did. Table 4(a) reports an employment increase of 5.95 percentage points for WFTC alone, while Table 4(b) reports 3.86 percentage points when all reforms are included. The difference is attributed to simultaneous increases in income support and other out-of-work child-related transfers, which dampened work incentives. 

The paper also resolves the apparent UK–US puzzle. Although the UK WFTC looked much more generous than the US EITC in gross-transfer terms, the impact in the UK was smaller because the UK reform interacted strongly with other benefits, especially housing benefit, and because out-of-work support also rose at the same time. The paper is explicit that the smaller UK response should not be read as evidence of smaller labour-supply elasticities. 

On optimality, the paper finds that an earned income tax credit is easier to justify for lone parents with school-age children than for those with pre-school children. Fig. 9(a) shows optimal schedules by age of youngest child, with stronger in-work support as the youngest child gets older. Fig. 10 suggests that, for relatively redistributive welfare weights, the actual WFTC schedule is fairly close to an optimal schedule for a representative lone-parent case. 

# Main limitations

The first limitation, from your perspective, is the absence of an explicit opportunity-set object. The paper is rich on budget constraints, take-up, and childcare costs, but silent on latent jobs, job-search frictions, and unequal access to employment opportunities. That weakens its usefulness for a project centered on (A) as a feasible set. 

A second limitation is that the normative analysis depends on a structurally estimated utility model and on a specific welfare transformation function. The paper validates the structural model against difference-in-differences moments, which is a real strength, but the optimality results remain conditional on that modelling architecture. 

A third limitation is that dynamic margins are acknowledged but not integrated fully into the main optimality analysis. The conclusion explicitly notes open questions concerning human-capital investment, dynamic work experience effects, and other longer-run channels. The paper therefore provides a static or quasi-static optimality analysis, not a fully dynamic one. 

A fourth limitation is that the paper does not offer a decomposition of inequality or welfare into opportunities, preferences, taxes, and related factors. It is concerned with policy impact and optimal design, not decomposition. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing the junction between structural labour-supply modelling and normative policy design. It offers a disciplined example of how to move from behavioural responses to a question of optimal tax-transfer design without pretending that reduced-form average impacts are enough for that task. 

## possible use for model design

It is useful for model design because it shows what a rich structural labour-supply model for lone parents can look like when budget constraints, take-up, childcare costs, and fixed costs matter. It is especially helpful if your project needs to think carefully about the distinction between extensive and intensive margins. It is not useful for modelling explicit feasible job sets. 

## possible use for identification

It is very useful as an example of combining quasi-experimental validation with structural estimation. The methodological lesson is not that difference-in-differences replaces the structural model, but that it can be used to discipline or validate it. That is an important template if you want policy design claims to remain empirically grounded. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

It is useful, but only at the level of social-welfare-based policy design. It does not provide an individual well-being measure like (W(z,R,A;y)), yet it does show how behavioural utility, social welfare weights, and tax design can be linked in practice. For your project, this is an intermediate reference rather than a direct solution. 

## possible use for decomposition

Direct use is limited. The paper does not decompose outcomes or welfare into preference and opportunity components. At most, it suggests a way to separate policy-schedule effects from behavioural elasticities, but not a full decomposition framework. 

## possible use for comparative application

It is relevant for comparative application because the paper explicitly interprets UK reforms relative to US EITC policy and explains why apparently more generous UK transfers generated smaller labour-supply effects. The cross-country comparison is not the main empirical design, but it is analytically important.

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is that the paper treats the labour market primarily through wages and hours incentives, not through the structure of opportunities. That is sufficient for a sophisticated optimal-tax exercise, but it is not sufficient for a welfare framework in which unequal feasible sets themselves are morally central. In your terms, the paper optimizes over a rich (y)-type institutional environment without modeling (A).

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

The paper is significantly more informative about preferences versus institutional constraints than about opportunities versus preferences. It shows how behavioural responses differ across extensive and intensive margins and across demographic groups, but it does not tell us whether unequal outcomes reflect unequal preferences or unequal opportunity sets, because the latter are not modeled. 

Its main lesson for your project is therefore indirect. It demonstrates how much one can already do with a well-specified budget-set model and credible behavioural elasticities, but also how much remains outside the analysis when the opportunity set itself is absent. For a jobs-and-wellbeing project, that is both useful and limiting. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

A central substantive claim is the UK–US “puzzle”: the UK policy appeared about twice as generous in real transfer terms as the US policy, yet the observed labour-supply response was smaller. The paper’s answer is that interactions with other parts of the tax-benefit system, plus contemporaneous increases in out-of-work support, explain much of this. 

The key empirical benchmarks are Table 3, which reports difference-in-differences employment effects of 3.57 to 3.81 percentage points, and Table 4(a)–4(b), which separate the structural impact of WFTC alone from the impact of the broader reform package. 

The most useful visual objects are Fig. 4, comparing WFTC and EITC gross transfers; Fig. 5 and Fig. 8, showing the dampening effect of interactions with other benefits for a typical lone parent; and Fig. 9–10, which compare optimal schedules with the actual WFTC system.

# My quick takeaway

This is one of the more useful papers in your corpus if your aim is to understand how structural labour-supply models can be taken all the way to optimal policy design. Its strongest contribution is not the raw impact estimate by itself, but the combined argument that a validated structural model can explain the UK–US difference and can rationalize an earned income tax credit as close to optimal under plausible welfare weights. But it remains a paper about preferences, elasticities, and institutional schedules, not about explicit opportunity sets. For your JMP, it is best used as a bridge from positive structural modelling to normative design, while keeping in mind that the (A)-dimension is still missing.
