# Full citation

Choné, Philippe, and Guy Laroque. 2010. “Negative Marginal Tax Rates and Heterogeneity.” *American Economic Review* 100(5): 2532–2547. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper shows that even within a standard intensive-margin Mirrlees framework, negative marginal tax rates can be optimal when agents are heterogeneous not only in productivity but also in work opportunity costs, because this heterogeneity changes the social-weight profile conditional on income. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it directly challenges the standard inference that optimal marginal tax rates must be nonnegative in the intensive-margin model. Its central claim is not that one needs an extensive-margin participation model, as in Saez (2002), to rationalize negative marginal tax rates at the bottom. Rather, negative rates can emerge even in an intensive setup if multidimensional heterogeneity makes low-income individuals carry below-average social weights. 

For your project, this is important because it sharply separates two issues that are often conflated: the behavioral margin of response, and the normative/social-weight structure generated by heterogeneity. The paper is not about feasible job sets (A), but it is highly relevant for the role of heterogeneity in welfare evaluation and for the question of when redistribution toward the very bottom may be weakened or reversed by fairness considerations tied to work opportunity costs. 

# Core research question

Under what conditions can negative marginal tax rates be optimal in a Mirrlees-type intensive labor-supply model once agents differ along multiple dimensions, specifically productivity and opportunity cost of work, and how do these heterogeneities affect social weights conditional on income? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is a theoretical optimal-taxation paper with one empirical illustration based on UK data. It is situated against the standard result in the Mirrlees-Seade-Werning tradition that marginal tax rates are everywhere positive when labor supply is distorted downward and the planner has redistributive preferences toward lower incomes. The authors argue that this result is fragile once one allows multiple dimensions of heterogeneity. 

The context is therefore not transfer-program design in the Saez (2002) extensive-margin sense, but the sign of optimal marginal tax rates in a standard nonlinear income-tax problem. The paper explicitly revisits Saez’s claim that negative marginal tax rates at the bottom require below-average social weights for low earners, and then asks when that condition can arise naturally through heterogeneity in utilities and work opportunity costs. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a Mirrlees-type optimal nonlinear income-tax model with intensive labor supply and multidimensional heterogeneity. Agents differ by productivity ( \theta^P ) and opportunity cost of work ( \theta^C ). Utility is separable:
[
U = u(c) + v(y,\theta),
]
where (c) is after-tax income and (y) is before-tax income. Utility from consumption is increasing and concave, while the disutility term varies with both productivity and the onerousness of work. The government observes only earnings and sets a nonlinear tax schedule (T(y)), equivalently an after-tax schedule (R(y)=y-T(y)). 

The key simplifying restriction is that behavior depends only on a one-dimensional index (a=A(\theta)), increasing in productivity and decreasing in work opportunity cost. This is crucial. It allows the authors to incorporate multiple heterogeneity dimensions while preserving the usual single-crossing structure and avoiding the full technical difficulties of truly multidimensional screening. The model is therefore not fully multidimensional in incentive space, even though heterogeneity is multidimensional in primitives. 

The framework is explicitly normative and positive. Positive behavior is captured by incentive compatibility and the induced earnings allocation (y(a)). Normative evaluation comes from a utilitarian objective defined over a cardinal social utility function (K[U,\theta^C]), so work opportunity cost may affect social weights directly, not only through observed earnings. 

# Key objects

The central objects are the reduced type index (a), the after-tax income schedule (R(y)), the incentive-compatible earnings allocation (y(a)), the indirect utility (U^R(a)), and the modified social weights ( \pi^*(a) ). The average modified social weight above type (a) is denoted (p^*(a)). These objects determine the sign of the marginal tax rate. 

The key analytical result is that, at regular non-bunching points, the sign of the marginal tax rate depends on the gap between the marginal cost of public funds and the average social weight of those above the type in question. Negative marginal tax rates arise when the relevant low-income agents have sufficiently low social weights relative to the economy-wide average. This is the substantive content of Lemma 2 and the discussion around pages 2536–2537. 

A second key object is the conditional distribution (F(\theta^C \mid a)). This is where heterogeneity enters normatively. The social weight at a given observed income depends not only on the utility specification but also on how the distribution of work opportunity costs shifts with income. Proposition 3 formalizes that interaction. 

# Data

The paper is primarily theoretical. It contains one numerical illustration based on UK annual earnings and marginal tax-rate data for 2003. The authors state that they use the specification of Brewer, Saez, and Shephard (2009), which was also used in the Mirrlees Review, and that they are using UK data for 2003 with annual earnings and marginal tax rate information. 

There is no structural estimation of the heterogeneity distribution from microdata in the paper. Instead, the UK section is a calibration-style illustration meant to show how small changes in unobserved heterogeneity in work opportunity costs can produce large changes in the optimal tax schedule. Figure 2 on page 2544 is central here. 

# Identification logic

There is no causal identification strategy in the quasi-experimental or empirical structural sense. The paper is analytical. Its main “identification” problem is conceptual: given a utilitarian planner, cardinal utility specification, and a conditional distribution of work opportunity costs, what social-weight schedule is implied, and therefore what marginal-tax pattern follows? 

In the UK illustration, the crucial identifying inputs are assumed rather than estimated from the paper’s own design: the labor-supply elasticity parameter, the cardinal utility form, and especially the conditional variance of work opportunity costs as a function of income. The authors are explicit that they have no empirical evidence for the particular shape they impose on this heterogeneity profile. The exercise is therefore illustrative, not identified in a strong empirical sense. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

There is no estimation in the econometric sense. The strategy has three steps. First, the paper characterizes second-best allocations in the Mirrlees model with arbitrary social weights. Second, it derives conditions under which utilitarian cardinal utilities and heterogeneity imply decreasing or nondecreasing social weights. Third, it calibrates an illustration with UK data to show how modest changes in heterogeneity can generate negative marginal tax rates at the bottom. 

The UK illustration assumes a standard labor-supply specification with elasticity (e=0.25), recovers the reduced type parameter (a) from observed earnings and marginal tax rates, and then imposes a normal distribution for work opportunity cost conditional on (a). The standard deviation of this work opportunity cost is then allowed to vary with income, especially in the first quartile, to study the resulting optimal tax schedules. This is discussed on pages 2542–2544 and displayed in Figure 2. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences enter in two distinct ways. First, private preferences are embedded in the utility function (u(c)+v(y,\theta)), with heterogeneity in work opportunity costs and productivity. Second, and more importantly for the paper’s contribution, the *cardinal* social valuation of private utility is allowed to depend on work opportunity cost through (K[U,\theta^C]). This is what drives the possibility of nonstandard social weights. 

This is not a model of heterogeneous tastes in the discrete-choice labor-supply sense. It is a model in which heterogeneity in the disutility or onerousness of work alters both private behavior and social valuation. If higher work opportunity cost is interpreted as a handicap, then social weights may rise with ( \theta^C ); if instead it reflects enjoyable nonmarket opportunities, social weights may fall with ( \theta^C ). This is a central theme of the paper. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is not a paper about feasible opportunity sets. It does not model job availability, latent job offers, hours restrictions, demand-side rationing, or set-valued feasible menus. There is no object analogous to a feasible job set (A). 

What the paper does model is “work opportunity cost,” which is a fundamentally different concept. It is closer to the utility cost or onerousness of supplying labor than to an opportunity set in the sense of available jobs or attainable bundles. Thus, the paper treats a form of heterogeneity in the *cost of work*, not heterogeneity in actual opportunities or constraints. 

Accordingly, the paper helps distinguish heterogeneity in welfare weights from heterogeneity in productivity, but it does not help distinguish preference heterogeneity from feasible-set heterogeneity in your sense. It is therefore relevant to your project only indirectly on the “opportunities” side. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative. The government solves a second-best utilitarian problem with a cardinal social utility function (K[U,\theta^C]). The normative question is how the sign of optimal marginal tax rates depends on the social weights implied by this cardinalization. 

This is highly relevant to responsibility versus compensation. The sign of the marginal tax rate depends on whether high work opportunity cost is treated as something deserving compensation, like a handicap or poor living conditions, or as something that lowers deservingness, like attractive nonmarket opportunities or leisure taste. The paper is unusually explicit on this point. 

However, the welfare object is not a well-being measure over realized bundles and feasible sets. Nor is it a decomposition of inequality. It is a social-weight-based optimal tax criterion inside a utilitarian second-best framework. 

# Main findings

The main theoretical finding is that negative marginal tax rates can be optimal in an intensive-margin Mirrlees framework once multidimensional heterogeneity changes the pattern of social weights conditional on income. This breaks the standard one-dimensional positive-tax result without needing an extensive-margin participation model. 

A second result is that standard positive marginal tax rates are recovered in several important cases. In particular, if the cardinal utility is concave in utility and work opportunity cost is independent of the income-relevant type (a), then marginal tax rates are everywhere nonnegative. Proposition 2 and Corollary 1 establish these sufficient conditions. 

A third result is the bottom-income condition emphasized in Section III.B: for negative marginal tax rates to be optimal near the bottom, the social weight on low-income agents must be below the economy-wide average. The paper then derives a formula, Proposition 3, showing how this depends jointly on the conditional distribution of work opportunity costs and on how social weights vary with those costs. 

The UK illustration shows that a relatively small change in the heterogeneity of work opportunity costs at the bottom can generate negative marginal tax rates. The authors report that increasing the standard error of work opportunity cost from £100 at the bottom to about £200 at the first quartile is enough to warrant negative marginal tax rates. The chart on page 2544 shows the marginal-tax schedule dipping below zero for sufficiently high low-end heterogeneity. 

# Main limitations

The most important limitation is that the multidimensional heterogeneity is not fully carried into the incentive problem. Behavior is reduced to a single index (a=A(\theta)). This is analytically convenient and important, but it means the paper is not a full multidimensional screening model. 

A second limitation is empirical. The UK illustration imposes rather than estimates the conditional distribution of work opportunity costs. The authors explicitly state that they have no evidence supporting the specific assumption that the dispersion of work opportunity costs rises with earnings in the way they calibrate. This weakens the direct policy force of the numerical result. 

A third limitation for your project is that the paper has no explicit concept of feasible opportunity sets or job availability. It therefore cannot be integrated directly into a (W(z,R,A;y)) framework as a paper about (A). At best, it informs the role of heterogeneity in the normative valuation layer. 

A fourth limitation is that the result is highly sensitive to the cardinal utility specification. Whether high work opportunity cost raises or lowers social weights depends on an ethically and empirically contestable choice of (K[U,\theta^C]). The paper is aware of this, but the framework remains underdetermined without external justification. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing if your JMP wants to argue that the sign of optimal redistribution cannot be inferred from behavior alone. It shows that heterogeneity in welfare-relevant characteristics can overturn standard positive-tax conclusions even in a conventional Mirrlees environment. 

## possible use for model design

It is not directly useful for RURO or latent-job model design. But it is useful for thinking about how multidimensional heterogeneity might be compressed into a lower-dimensional behavioral object while still leaving normatively important residual heterogeneity. That is relevant if your eventual empirical model has a one-dimensional sufficient statistic for behavior but richer heterogeneity for welfare. 

## possible use for identification

Directly limited. The paper offers no strong empirical identification strategy. Indirectly useful, because it tells you exactly which empirical object matters for the normative result: the conditional distribution of a welfare-relevant heterogeneity variable given observed income or type. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

Strongly useful in a narrow sense. The paper does not build your preferred well-being measure, but it is a very good paper on how welfare weights can vary with unobserved heterogeneity that is not reducible to income. It is especially relevant if your project wants to justify why observed income should not mechanically determine social priority. 

## possible use for decomposition

Limited for direct decomposition of inequality. The paper does not decompose inequality into preferences, opportunities, and pay. But it does decompose the determinants of social weights into utility curvature and heterogeneity composition. That may be conceptually useful for a later decomposition layer. 

## possible use for comparative application

Potentially useful if you later compare settings with different conditional heterogeneity patterns. The UK illustration suggests that different societies or labor markets could warrant different tax schedules purely because the distribution of work-cost heterogeneity conditional on income differs. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How would the sign-of-the-marginal-tax result change if the relevant heterogeneity were not work opportunity cost but heterogeneity in feasible job sets (A)?

Can one construct a (W(z,R,A;y))-type model in which social weights depend on both realized income and the distribution of feasible opportunities conditional on that income?

When a low-income individual has a high disutility of work, under what ethical conditions should that be treated like a handicap to compensate rather than a preference to respect?

Can the Choné-Laroque social-weight mechanism be combined with a structural job-choice model so that the conditional distribution of heterogeneity is estimated rather than imposed?

Is there a fairness-based characterization of when low-income agents should have below-average social weights, or is that always an artifact of the chosen cardinal utility representation?

# Challenge to this paper

The paper’s deepest vulnerability is that it gets its most striking result from the interaction between an assumed cardinal utility specification and an assumed conditional heterogeneity distribution. That is analytically legitimate, but it leaves the normative conclusion highly sensitive to objects that are difficult to justify empirically and ethically. In practice, one may accept the theorem while remaining unconvinced by any specific application unless those primitives are much better grounded.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper sits exactly on an ambiguous boundary between opportunities and preferences. Its key heterogeneity variable is the “work opportunity cost,” which could be read as a preference for leisure, a burden from poor life conditions, a handicap, or an outside option. The paper itself stresses that the normative interpretation depends on which of these readings is appropriate. 

For your purposes, that ambiguity is both useful and limiting. It is useful because it forces an explicit ethical distinction between compensable burdens and preference-like heterogeneity. It is limiting because the paper does not provide a clean formal criterion for classifying the work-cost variable on one side or the other. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

A central reduced-form representation is
[
U = u(c) + v(y,\theta),
]
with heterogeneity in both productivity and work opportunity cost. This is the core behavioral structure. 

The key social-weight objects are
[
\pi^*(a)=\pi(a)u'(R(y_R(a))),
\qquad
p^*(a)=\frac{1}{1-G^*(a)}\int_a^{\bar a}\pi^*(x),dG^*(x).
]
These are important because the sign of the marginal tax rate depends on the comparison between (p^*(a)) and the marginal cost of public funds. 

The chart on page 2544 is especially useful. It shows, for alternative degrees of heterogeneity at the bottom, that the marginal tax rate can cross from positive to negative for low annual incomes, and the corresponding after-tax income schedule becomes steeper at the bottom when low-income social weights are reduced by heterogeneity.
