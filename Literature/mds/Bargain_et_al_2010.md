# Full citation

Bargain, Olivier, Marco Caliendo, Peter Haan, and Kristian Orsini. 2010. “Making work pay” in a rationed labor market. *Journal of Population Economics* 23: 323–351. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper evaluates two individualized in-work transfer reforms for Germany within a static structural labour-supply model augmented with demand-side rationing, and argues empirically that policies targeted at low wages are more employment-efficient than policies targeted at low earnings. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it addresses a central problem for your agenda: labour-supply behaviour cannot be interpreted cleanly if one ignores the fact that some non-employment is involuntary. The paper therefore inserts demand-side constraints into an otherwise standard structural labour-supply model and shows that ignoring rationing can materially bias policy predictions. 

It is also important because it distinguishes low productivity from low effort in policy design. The main policy comparison is between a transfer conditioned on low earnings and one conditioned on low wages. That distinction is not the same as your (A) versus (R) decomposition, but it is closely related to the problem of whether poor outcomes reflect constrained opportunities or chosen effort. 

# Core research question

What are the labour-supply and employment effects of two individualized “making work pay” reforms in Germany, and how much does accounting for involuntary unemployment and labour-market rationing matter for those predictions? 

# Economic setting and context

The setting is Germany in the early 2000s, with particular attention to low-paid employment and in-work transfer design. The paper studies the mini-job reform and a hypothetical reform inspired by the Belgian Employment Bonus. It places these policies in comparison with family-based tax credits in the US and UK, emphasizing that individualized transfers avoid the direct negative participation incentives for secondary earners that arise under family-income conditioning. 

The policy problem is explicitly one of labour-market activation under substantial demand-side constraints. The authors stress that Germany is a context in which rationing matters enough that a pure supply-side model may seriously misstate reform effects. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a static structural discrete-choice labour-supply model combined with a double-hurdle rationing model. Households choose among discrete hours options, with net incomes generated by tax-benefit microsimulation. For singles and couples, utility depends on leisure, disposable income, and household characteristics, plus an extreme-value random term. 

The crucial extension is the second hurdle. After a household chooses desired labour supply, employment may still be blocked by involuntary unemployment. The latent unemployment-risk equation is estimated separately and combined with the labour-supply model so that desired participation and actual employment are distinct. This is the paper’s defining modelling move. 

The framework is positive with policy-design applications. It is not normative in the welfare-economics sense. It does not define a social welfare function or an individual well-being measure. It studies employment and hours responses, budgetary costs, and policy efficiency. 

# Key objects

The main positive objects are discrete desired hours choices, disposable income by choice, utility over leisure and income, and the latent involuntary-unemployment indicator. The rationing equation
[
I_i^*=\beta X_i+\eta_i
]
summarizes the probability that a worker who wishes to participate is constrained by demand-side conditions. 

The key policy objects are the German mini-job reform and the hypothetical Employment Bonus. The mini-job reform targets low earnings and expands earnings exempt from social security contributions. The Employment Bonus targets low wage rates, using full-time-equivalent individual earnings, and is designed to avoid encouraging effort reduction among workers already employed. 

A second key conceptual object is the paper’s “triple bias” from ignoring involuntary unemployment: misspecification, erroneous freedom of choice, and overstatement of the taste for leisure. This is one of the most relevant contributions for your project. 

# Data

The data come from the 2003 wave of the German Socio-Economic Panel, referring to fiscal year 2002. The sample excludes cases where relevant adults are self-employed, retired, disabled, on maternity leave, or in full-time education. Labour supply is modeled separately for single women, single men, couples with both spouses flexible, couples with only the man flexible, and couples with only the woman flexible. Page 8, Table 2 reports descriptive statistics and sample sizes for each group. 

The key identification variables for rationing combine regional labour-market information and individual employment histories. Local labour-office districts are grouped into five clusters with different labour-market conditions, reported on page 9 in Table 3. 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural, not quasi-experimental. The labour-supply component is identified from variation in budget sets, wages, household composition, and observed hours choices. The rationing component is identified using regional labour-market conditions and past employment histories, together with survey information distinguishing involuntary from voluntary unemployment. 

A particularly important identifying device is the use of two survey questions on job search and readiness to work, following the ILO definition, to classify unemployed individuals as involuntarily unemployed. The model also exploits information on desired part-time versus full-time work among the unemployed. This gives the constrained model more structure than a simple reduced-form non-employment equation. 

Identification of opportunities remains partial and indirect. The paper does not observe a job set (A_i). Instead, it identifies the probability of rationing conditional on covariates. # Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy has two components. First, the authors estimate a standard static structural labour-supply model over discrete hours categories using tax-benefit microsimulation to compute net income for each alternative. Second, they estimate a probit-type rationing equation and combine it with the labour-supply model in a double-hurdle framework. 

The authors then simulate the employment and hours effects of the two reforms for the German main labour force, comparing predictions from the constrained and unconstrained models. This permits a direct quantification of the error induced by ignoring involuntary unemployment. Tables 10 and 11 are central for the simulated reform effects. 

The paper also uses graphical budget-line analysis. The diagrams on page 5 compare prereform, mini-job, and Employment Bonus budget lines for a secondary earner in a couple and for a single woman. These figures visually show why the mini-job reform creates a part-time trap while the Employment Bonus shifts incentives toward fuller participation. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled through a standard structural utility function over leisure and disposable income with household taste shifters. In this sense, the paper remains within the conventional discrete labour-supply tradition. Part-time dummies are added to capture specific utility or disutility associated with part-time work. 

However, one of the main messages is that unconstrained models overstate the taste for leisure because they misclassify some involuntary unemployment as chosen inactivity. Thus the paper explicitly shows that what looks like preference heterogeneity may partly reflect unmodeled constraints. This is highly relevant for your project. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is the paper’s main value for your research. The paper does not model a full opportunity set of jobs, but it does model an explicit constraint on participation through involuntary unemployment. Opportunities therefore enter through the probability of obtaining employment rather than through a universal free-choice assumption. 

The paper is very clear that unconstrained labour-supply models impose “erroneous freedom of choice.” That is, they wrongly treat all non-employment as voluntary. The constrained model corrects this by separating desired participation from actual employment. In that sense, the paper helps distinguish preference heterogeneity from opportunity heterogeneity, although only along the employment-rationing margin. 

It also distinguishes low effort from low productivity at the policy-design level. A transfer based on low earnings may reward reduced effort, whereas a transfer based on low wages is meant to target low productivity instead. This is not a full theory of opportunities, but it is directly connected to responsibility-type questions. 

The paper does not model actual feasible job sets, latent job distributions, or reference opportunity sets. It is narrower than RURO-type work. Opportunities are represented as a rationing probability, not as a full (A_i). 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is primarily positive with policy-efficiency applications. It does not evaluate welfare using EV, equivalent income, or a planner’s welfare function. The main outcomes are participation, hours worked, full-time-equivalent employment, and budgetary cost-efficiency. 

Normatively, the paper does discuss the design principle that policy should distinguish low effort from low productivity. That is an ethical-policy distinction, but it is not developed as a formal welfare criterion. The paper does not address compensation for opportunities in an axiomatic way and does not provide a decomposition of inequality into preferences, opportunities, and other factors. 

# Main findings

The main substantive finding is that the Employment Bonus strongly dominates the mini-job reform in employment efficiency. The mini-job reform produces only a modest positive participation effect and a negative intensive-margin effect, yielding a small or negative total hours effect. By contrast, the Employment Bonus generates much larger participation and total-hours effects and does not create the same part-time trap. Tables 10 and 11 report these differences. 

Using the constrained model, the paper predicts about 43,000 new participants from the mini-job reform, but the net hours effect is negative, around minus 11,000 FTE. For the Employment Bonus, the constrained model predicts about 159,000 new participants and roughly 211,000 FTE in additional work. These are large differences. 

A second key finding is methodological. Ignoring involuntary unemployment can seriously overstate reform effects. The paper states that for the Employment Bonus the total employment effect is overstated by about 60% when using the unconstrained model. This is one of the most important methodological conclusions. 

The labour-supply elasticities also differ across constrained and unconstrained models. For several groups, especially singles and men in couples, the unconstrained model produces noticeably larger elasticities because it incorrectly allows rationed workers to respond at the participation margin. Table 8 and the discussion around page 18 are central here. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that opportunities are modeled only through employment rationing, not through a richer job-choice set. The model therefore captures one important margin of constrained opportunity but not the multidimensional structure of feasible jobs. For your framework, that is useful but incomplete. 

A second limitation is the partial-equilibrium treatment of rationing. The individual rationing probability is assumed not to change after reform. This means the paper cannot capture general-equilibrium feedbacks of the reforms on labour demand, vacancy creation, or equilibrium wages. 

A third limitation is the absence of a welfare metric. The paper compares employment efficiency and cost per job created, but it does not tell us how the reforms rank once leisure, consumption, and constrained opportunities are evaluated in a unified well-being framework.

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is highly useful for framing the idea that non-employment should not automatically be interpreted as chosen leisure. For any jobs-and-wellbeing framework, that is a foundational point.

## possible use for model design

It is useful for model design if your empirical work needs to incorporate demand-side rationing. The double-hurdle structure gives a concrete way to separate desired labour supply from actual employment.

## possible use for identification

It is useful because it shows how survey information on job search and readiness to work, together with regional labour-market data and past employment histories, can identify a constrained-employment margin.

## possible use for welfare measurement

Directly, it is limited because the paper does not provide a welfare measure. Indirectly, it is important because it shows that preference-based welfare analysis will be biased if involuntary unemployment is absorbed into leisure.

## possible use for decomposition

It is moderately useful. The paper does not deliver an (R/A/y) decomposition, but it does isolate one opportunity-related source of observed non-employment and shows how it contaminates unconstrained preference estimation.

## possible use for comparative application

It is useful for comparative application because it explicitly contrasts German individualized reforms with US and UK family-based tax credits and with the Belgian Employment Bonus design.

# Research questions this paper inspires

How can a rationing probability model be embedded in a richer framework where feasible job sets (A_i) contain both employment access and job attributes?

Can the distinction between low effort and low productivity be incorporated into an individual well-being measure (W(z,R,A;y)) rather than only into policy targeting rules?

How much of measured preference heterogeneity in standard labour-supply models is actually due to omitted opportunity constraints beyond unemployment rationing?

Would a welfare ranking of the mini-job reform and the Employment Bonus differ if one evaluated households using income-and-leisure well-being rather than employment efficiency alone?

Can the paper’s double-hurdle logic be combined with RURO-style latent job opportunities so that participation rationing and within-employment job scarcity are modeled jointly?

# Challenge to this paper

The main challenge is that the paper identifies an important constraint margin without fully integrating it into a broader opportunity-set framework. It convincingly shows that non-employment may be involuntary and that this matters for prediction, but it still treats opportunities narrowly as a binary employment hurdle rather than as a structured set of feasible jobs. For your project, this means the paper is very useful methodologically but incomplete conceptually.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is highly relevant on the positive side of the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction. Its main claim is precisely that unconstrained models attribute too much to preferences because they ignore involuntary unemployment. In that sense, it provides direct evidence that some observed inactivity reflects constrained opportunities rather than chosen leisure. 

But it does not settle the normative side of the distinction. It does not say how much of opportunity disadvantage should be compensated or how preference differences should be treated ethically. So it is best read as a methodological bridge from constrained labour-market behaviour toward your more explicit opportunity-sensitive well-being framework.
