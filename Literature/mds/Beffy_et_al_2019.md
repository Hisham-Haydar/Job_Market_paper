# Full citation

Beffy, Magali, Richard Blundell, Antoine Bozio, Guy Laroque, and Maxime Tô. 2019. “Labour supply and taxation with restricted choices.” *Journal of Econometrics* 211(1): 16-46. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper develops and estimates a structural labour-supply model in which observed hours reflect both preferences and restricted hours offers, and shows that nonlinear tax-benefit budget constraints can help identify the distribution of feasible hours choices as distinct from preferences. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters a great deal for your project because it attacks directly one of the core difficulties in labour-supply modelling: observed hours need not reveal pure preferences when individuals do not face the full range of hours options. The authors model labour supply as constrained rational choice on a random subset of possible hours, and they make the restriction empirically operational using nonlinear tax-benefit schedules and dominated hours regions. 

For your research agenda, this is one of the clearest papers linking labour-supply estimation to an explicit treatment of opportunity restrictions. It is not an axiomatic paper and it does not formulate well-being as (W(z,R,A;y)), but it is highly relevant as a positive empirical framework in which the feasible set is no longer universal and identical across agents. 

# Core research question

Under what conditions can one identify labour-supply preferences separately from restrictions on available hours choices, and how can a structural model with restricted hours offers be estimated in the presence of nonlinear tax and benefit schedules? 

# Economic setting and context

The empirical setting is the labour supply of women with children in the UK, over 1997-2002, a period that includes important reforms to the tax-credit and welfare system. The paper uses UK Family Expenditure Survey data together with a detailed tax-benefit simulator, and focuses on how hours restrictions interact with nonlinear budget constraints generated by taxes, tax credits, income support, and related benefits. 

The sample contains 10,575 women with children, including single and cohabiting mothers. Table 1 provides the core sample composition, Table 2 relates observed hours to education and family characteristics, and Table 3 reports the distributions of consumption, wages, and hours. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a structural labour-supply model with nonlinear budget constraints, fixed costs of work, and restricted choice sets. The starting point is an intertemporal labour-supply problem in which the woman chooses hours (h_t) and household consumption (c_t), subject to an intertemporal budget constraint, taxes and benefits (R(w,h)), and fixed work costs (b). In the unrestricted benchmark, hours belong to a full feasible set (H); in the paper’s restricted-choice version, choices are made from a small random subset of possible hours points. 

The agent chooses the hours alternative that maximizes utility. In the discrete-hours restricted-choice framework, utility from option (h) is written generically as (V(h,Z,\beta,\varepsilon)). The paper’s preferred empirical implementation uses a utility function over consumption and leisure,
[
u(c,h)=\frac{c^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}+a\frac{(L-h)^{1-\phi}}{1-\phi},
]
with heterogeneity in (a), fixed work costs, wages, and consumption processes. 

What is especially important is the feasible-set treatment. The paper does not assume unrestricted continuous hours choice. Instead, individuals are assumed to face a random set of offers. In the main empirical specification, each person has at most two hours offers: the observed one if working positive hours, and one alternative offer that was turned down; non-employment is always available. This “two-offer model” is a constrained rational-choice model over a random subset of hours, not a universal-choice-set model. 

The framework is positive rather than normative. It is concerned with identification and estimation of preferences versus hours restrictions, and with policy-relevant behavioural simulation under taxation. It is not a welfare-evaluation or fairness framework. 

# Key objects

The central positive objects are the indirect or choice-specific utility function (V(h,Z,\beta,\varepsilon)), the nonlinear net-income schedule (R(w,h)), the fixed cost of work (b), the offer distribution (g(h\mid Z_o)), and the observed choice probabilities induced by the restricted choice set. The two-offer likelihood is central:
[
\ell_{2i}(Z,\beta)=g_i^2+2g_i\sum_{j\neq i}g_j p_i({i,j},Z,\beta),
]
which shows observed hours probabilities as a combination of offer probabilities and pairwise choice probabilities. 

Another key object is the “irrational” or dominated set of hours (H^{ir}), derived from the after-tax income schedule. If some lower-hours choice yields weakly higher net income than a higher-hours choice, the higher-hours point is strictly dominated under standard monotonicity assumptions. This dominated set becomes informative about restrictions on feasible hours. The discussion in Section 2.2 and the budget-constraint examples in Figure 1 are central here. 

# Data

The data are from the UK Family Expenditure Survey for 1997-2002. The paper combines detailed information on hours, wages, expenditure/consumption, demographics, and family composition with the IFS-Taxben simulator to compute nonlinear household-specific budget constraints under the UK tax, tax-credit, and welfare system. 

The analysis sample contains 10,575 women with children. The sample includes single and cohabiting mothers, with substantial heterogeneity in education, number of children, and age of youngest child. The paper uses the expenditure data to construct a consumption measure consistent with a life-cycle approach. Descriptive information is reported in Tables 1-3. 

# Identification logic

Identification is the core theoretical contribution of the paper. The authors show three main identification results. First, if the offer distribution is known, then preferences can be identified in a random-utility framework. Second, if preferences are known, then the offer distribution can be recovered. Third, under suitable restrictions, both preferences and the offer distribution can be jointly identified. These results are laid out in Section 3 through a sequence of lemmas. 

The main empirical identification lever is heterogeneity in nonlinear budget constraints. Different tax-benefit schedules can generate dominated hours ranges that reveal features of the offer distribution. In particular, flat or decreasing regions of the budget constraint imply hours choices that would be irrational under unrestricted choice but can arise under restricted offers. The existence of such dominated regions helps identify the offer distribution nonparametrically, while variation in budget constraints and exclusion restrictions helps identify preference parameters. This is one of the paper’s most important insights. 

The paper also considers semiparametric identification using exclusion restrictions: variables that affect the budget constraint but not the offer distribution. Wage and other-income variation are the conceptual examples. The authors are explicit that enough variation in these exclusion variables is necessary for joint identification. 

This is therefore not identification in the reduced-form quasi-experimental sense. It is structural identification based on model restrictions, budget-set nonlinearities, dominated regions, exclusion restrictions, and parametric/semiparametric structure. # Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy is maximum-likelihood estimation of a restricted-choice labour-supply model. Preferences over consumption and leisure are parameterized, fixed costs of work are modeled as stochastic, wages have a reduced-form process, and offers are modeled as a mixture of two truncated normal distributions over hours, discretized to generate offer probabilities (g(h\mid Z_o)). Tables 4-6 report the key parameter estimates for preferences, offer distribution, and the wage equation. 

To handle endogeneity of consumption in the determination of labour supply and wages, the authors use a two-step control-function approach. First, they estimate a reduced-form consumption equation; then the estimated consumption residual enters the conditional likelihood for wages, hours, and employment. This is an important practical feature of the empirical implementation. 

The model is estimated in three versions: a baseline model, a model allowing correlation between wages, consumption, and preferences, and a model that also allows observable heterogeneity in the offer distribution. Model 3 is the richest specification and generally fits the hours distribution best. Tables 7-11 and Figures 2-7 report fit, rejections of the unrestricted model, elasticities, and simulations. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled explicitly and structurally. The paper specifies preferences over consumption and leisure using a utility function with curvature parameters (\gamma) and (\phi), and with a heterogeneity term (a) governing the substitution between consumption and leisure. Preference heterogeneity is allowed through observable covariates and unobserved components. 

This is not a simple multinomial logit over reduced-form utilities. The authors take preference heterogeneity seriously and embed it within a life-cycle-consistent framework using consumption, wages, fixed costs, and nonlinear taxation. The paper is therefore useful for structural labour-supply estimation beyond purely descriptive hours modeling. 

Crucially, however, preferences are not the whole story. The paper’s central message is precisely that observed hours cannot be interpreted as pure preference revelation if individuals face hours restrictions. In this sense, the paper is explicitly anti-reductionist with respect to preferences. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is the main reason the paper is so important for your project. The paper models opportunities explicitly, though in a restricted form. It does not use the language of “feasible set (A)” in the axiomatic sense, but it does replace universal hours choice with a random restricted choice set. The offer distribution (g(h\mid Z_o)) is the paper’s empirical representation of the opportunity side. 

The mechanism is straightforward. Individuals do not choose from all possible hours. They choose from a random subset of feasible hours offers. In the main empirical model, they have two offers, plus the option of non-work. This makes the feasible set heterogeneous across individuals and partially latent. It is therefore very close in spirit to an opportunity-set approach, even if the paper formulates it in hours-offer rather than full job-package notation. 

The paper is also explicit that restrictions come from the demand side of the labour market, namely the hours offered by employers. That is important. The hours constraints are not merely ad hoc frictions or fixed effects; they are interpreted as limitations on available job packages. This is conceptually very close to the distinction between preferences and opportunities that matters for your work. 

The model helps distinguish both preference heterogeneity and opportunity heterogeneity. Preferences are captured through utility parameters and unobserved taste shifters. Opportunity heterogeneity is captured through the distribution of available hours offers, which may vary with observable covariates and is identified partly through the tax-benefit-induced shape of the budget constraint. 

That said, opportunities are modeled only on the hours dimension. The paper does not model general job sets including wages and nonpecuniary characteristics, except in the concluding discussion where the authors note that a natural extension would be to allow offers over wage-hours packages. So relative to a full (A\subseteq J) job-set framework, this is a partial but important opportunity model. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is overwhelmingly positive rather than normative. Utility is used as a behavioural object for labour-supply decisions, identification, and simulation. There is no explicit social welfare function, no money-metric welfare analysis, and no fairness criterion of responsibility or compensation. 

There are policy-relevant simulations, such as comparing constrained and unconstrained employment rates and labour-supply responses to wage increases, but these are behavioural rather than normative welfare exercises. The paper does not construct a welfare measure over realized bundles and opportunity sets. 

For your purposes, this means the paper is not directly useful for responsibility for opportunities, compensation for opportunities, or reference opportunity sets. It is useful upstream of those questions because it gives a credible empirical representation of the opportunity side that a later welfare or fairness analysis could build on. # Main findings

First, the paper provides direct evidence that a nontrivial share of observed hours choices are inconsistent with unrestricted hours optimization under the nonlinear UK tax-benefit system. Table 8 reports that about 2.56 percent of working women are nonparametric rejections of the unrestricted model because they work at strictly dominated hours. Using the estimated preference specification, the broader parametric rejection share rises to 7.93 percent in the richest specification. 

Second, the estimated offer distribution is bimodal, with offers concentrated around part-time and full-time work. Table 5 and the surrounding discussion show that the two-offer model generates an hours distribution with twin peaks similar to those observed in the data, which the unrestricted model does not reproduce as well. Figures 2 and 6 are especially informative here. 

Third, women who appear constrained are poorer and work shorter hours on average. Table 9 shows that women rejecting the unrestricted model have lower wages, lower consumption, lower in-work and out-of-work income, and are more often lone mothers. This is a substantively important result because hours restrictions are not randomly distributed across the population. 

Fourth, hours restrictions materially affect behavioural implications. Simulations show an employment rate of about 62.5 percent in the constrained two-offer model versus roughly 71 percent in the unconstrained case. Thus the restrictions depress employment relative to what women would choose if they faced unrestricted hours options. Figures 6 and the text in Section 5.5.1 make this point clearly. 

Fifth, labour-supply elasticities differ between constrained and unconstrained settings. Table 11 shows that the average extensive-margin elasticity is slightly larger in the two-offer model than in the unconstrained case, while the average intensive-margin elasticity is substantially smaller in the constrained model. This matters directly for taxation analysis. 

# Main limitations

The most obvious limitation is the very restrictive two-offer specification. Each woman is assumed to face at most two hours offers plus non-employment. The authors are explicit that this is restrictive and suggest extending the model to (n)-offer settings or wage-hours packages in future work. For your purposes, this means the paper is a strong proof of concept rather than a final general model of opportunities. 

A second limitation is that opportunities are represented only along the hours dimension. Wage-hours packages, job characteristics, occupational attributes, and broader opportunity sets are not modeled directly. This limits its direct fit with a full RURO or jobs-and-wellbeing framework where jobs differ on multiple attributes. 

A third limitation is that the offer distribution is effectively treated as independent of past choices and is modeled rather parsimoniously as a mixture of truncated normals. # Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is extremely useful for framing the claim that labour-supply data cannot be interpreted solely through preferences. It gives a precise econometric statement of why observed hours may reflect restricted opportunities rather than unconstrained choice, and it ties that statement to real tax-benefit systems rather than purely abstract theory.

# Research questions this paper inspires

How can a random restricted-hours offer model be extended to a full random job-set model in which jobs differ jointly in wage, hours, and nonpecuniary attributes?

Can one define an individual well-being measure (W(z,R,A;y)) where (A) is estimated from restricted labour-market offers rather than treated as observed or universal?

How much of observed labour-supply inequality is attributable to preference heterogeneity versus opportunity heterogeneity when opportunities are modeled as latent constrained choice sets?

Can tax-benefit-induced dominated regions be exploited to identify opportunity sets in richer discrete-choice labour-supply models with occupation or sectoral choices?

How would policy prescriptions change if optimal tax or welfare analysis were based on estimated restricted opportunity sets rather than unrestricted hours choice?

# Challenge to this paper

The main challenge is that the paper’s opportunity structure is still narrow. It convincingly rejects unrestricted hours choice and shows that restricted hours offers matter, but the two-offer hours-only specification remains a stylized representation of labour-market opportunities. A richer model allowing wage-hours-job-characteristic packages might change both the identification and the quantitative importance of the constraints.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This is one of the most relevant papers in your corpus for the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction. The paper’s central claim is that observed hours conflate preferences and restrictions, and that the econometrician should model both. Preferences alone do not explain hours peaks and dominated observed choices; restricted opportunities are part of the explanation. 

At the same time, the paper does not collapse everything into opportunities either. It estimates preferences and restrictions jointly. That balance is precisely why the paper is valuable: it does not replace one reductionism with another. Instead, it provides a concrete structural strategy for disentangling the two.
