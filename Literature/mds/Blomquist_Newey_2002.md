# Full citation

Blomquist, Sören, and Whitney Newey. 2002. “Nonparametric Estimation with Nonlinear Budget Sets.” *Econometrica* 70(6): 2455-2480. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper develops a nonparametric estimator for labour-supply models with piecewise-linear nonlinear budget sets, exploiting restrictions implied by utility maximization with convex preferences and convex budget sets to avoid the curse of dimensionality and to estimate tax-reform effects more robustly than standard parametric maximum-likelihood methods. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is one of the clearest methodological attempts to estimate labour supply under nonlinear tax schedules without committing to a fully parametric distribution for preferences or errors. In the labour-supply literature, this is important because many policy questions concern tax systems that create nonlinear budget constraints, and standard parametric estimators may be highly sensitive to functional-form assumptions. 

For your project, the paper is especially useful as a benchmark for what can be done when the main nonlinearity comes from the budget set rather than from explicit opportunity-set heterogeneity. It does not model job-offer sets, latent jobs, or restricted hours offers. But it is highly relevant for understanding how far one can go in separating behavioural responses to the pay/tax schedule from stronger assumptions on tastes. 

# Core research question

How can one estimate labour-supply behaviour nonparametrically when individuals face nonlinear, piecewise-linear budget sets generated by tax systems, while still exploiting the structure implied by utility maximization to keep the estimation problem tractable? 

# Economic setting and context

The empirical application is to Swedish male labour supply around major tax changes. The paper pools data from Swedish “Level of Living” surveys for 1973, 1980, and 1990, which gives variation both across individuals and across tax systems, including a large tax reform between 1980 and 1991. The sample used for estimation contains married or cohabiting men aged 20-60, with 777 observations in 1973, 864 in 1980, and 680 in 1990, for a total of 2,321 observations. Appendix A reports sample statistics. 

The paper focuses on applications where there are relatively few nonworkers, so that sample-selection issues are minor. The empirical setting is therefore not one of female participation or large extensive-margin non-employment, but rather of hours choice among working men under complicated tax schedules. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a static labour-supply model with nonlinear piecewise-linear budget constraints, embedded in a nonparametric conditional-mean framework. The basic object of interest is the expected hours function
[
E[h_i\mid x_i]=\bar h(x_i),
]
where (x_i) denotes the entire budget set faced by individual (i). The conceptual move is to treat hours as a function of the whole budget set and then estimate that relationship nonparametrically. 

The budget set is assumed piecewise linear. A generic piecewise-linear budget constraint is characterized by the intercept and slope of each segment and the kink locations. Without further structure, this makes (x) very high-dimensional and nonparametric estimation infeasible. The paper’s main theoretical result is that utility maximization with convex preferences and convex budget sets implies strong structure on (\bar h(x)), leading to an additive representation involving a basic labour-supply function for linear budget sets plus a three-dimensional correction term for kinks. Theorem 2.1 is the key result here. 

The framework is positive rather than normative. The agent chooses hours, not jobs. The feasible set is a budget set generated by the tax schedule, not an explicit opportunity set of job offers. There is no direct modeling of labour-demand constraints, restricted hours offers, latent jobs, or job characteristics. Instead, the model assumes desired hours under a budget set, with individual heterogeneity summarized by a scalar (v), and focuses on recovering the conditional mean of labour supply. 

# Key objects

The main objects are the piecewise-linear budget set (x), the conditional mean labour-supply function (\bar h(x)), the desired-hours function under a linear budget set (\tilde h=\psi(y,w,v)), the average labour-supply function (\bar\psi(y,w)), and the correction term (\phi(y,w,\ell)) associated with kink points. The basic additive representation is
[
\bar h(x)=\bar\psi(y_J,w_J)+\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\left[\phi(y_j,w_j,\ell_j)-\phi(y_{j+1},w_{j+1},\ell_j)\right],
]
for convex piecewise-linear budget sets. This expression is the central theoretical contribution. 

Other key objects are the wage elasticity and the predicted tax-reform effect. The wage elasticity for a linear budget set is constructed from derivatives of the estimated nonparametric function, and the tax-reform effect (M) is defined as the percentage change in average labour supply from shifting budget sets from a pre-reform to a post-reform regime. Equation (3.5) defines the latter object. 

# Data

The data come from three waves of the Swedish “Level of Living” survey: 1973, 1980, and 1990. The surveys were conducted in 1974, 1981, and 1991, and include both survey and administrative information, including tax and social insurance records. The pooled estimation sample contains married or cohabiting men aged 20-60 after several exclusions. Appendix A reports means and variances for hours, marginal wage rates, and virtual income by year and for the pooled sample. 

The budget sets are constructed from the Swedish tax system and, for 1980 and 1990, housing allowances. The authors note that housing allowances create nonconvexities, but they assume these are small, convexify the budget sets for parametric estimation, and ignore these nonconvexities in the main nonparametric implementation except for robustness discussion. 

# Identification logic

Identification comes from variation in entire budget sets, not just in a single net wage or virtual income measure. The paper argues that if one thinks of hours as a function of the full budget set, then nonparametric regression is in principle possible, but only after exploiting the restrictions implied by utility maximization with convex preferences and convex budget sets. These restrictions drastically reduce dimensionality. 

More specifically, the additive decomposition implied by convex utility maximization lets the authors replace a high-dimensional nonparametric regression in all budget-set characteristics with a lower-dimensional problem involving an additive component (\bar\psi(y,w)) and a three-dimensional correction term. Identification of (\bar\psi(y,w)) depends on support conditions. Theorem 2.2 states that a necessary condition is that the target set (YW) be included in the intersection of the supports of ((y_1,w_1)) and ((y_J,w_J)), that is, initial and final virtual incomes and wages. 

This is not causal identification in the quasi-experimental sense. It is structural/nonparametric identification based on budget-set variation, convexity restrictions, and support conditions. The tax reform helps because pooling years generates richer variation in the shapes of budget sets, which helps identify the labour-supply response function and its policy counterfactuals. # Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper proposes series estimators of the conditional mean labour-supply function. The estimator is a least-squares series regression in which the approximating functions are chosen to impose the additivity and equality restrictions implied by the theory. The authors consider both raw power-series approximations and “Gaussian-centered” approximations that use the Gaussian correction as a leading term and then flexibly perturb it. Section 3 develops these estimators, and Section 4 provides consistency and asymptotic normality results. 

The number of series terms is chosen by cross-validation. The resulting estimator can handle budget sets with many segments because the number of segments affects only the form of the approximating functions, not the statistical rate of convergence in the way a naive fully nonparametric estimator would. This is one of the main practical advantages of the method. 

Empirically, the paper estimates a parametric maximum-likelihood benchmark based on a linear labour-supply function with Gaussian heterogeneity and then compares it with the nonparametric estimators. The main policy application is a stylized estimate of the effect of Swedish tax reform. Tables I and II report the power-series and Gaussian-centered nonparametric results. Figures 1 and 2 on pages 2474-2475 graph the estimated IV residual density and a Monte Carlo residual density, respectively, to illustrate strong departures from normality in the data relative to the Gaussian benchmark. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are present, but only indirectly. The paper starts from a desired-hours function under a linear budget set,
[
h^*=\psi(y,w,v),
]
where (v) is scalar individual heterogeneity. This is a highly parsimonious treatment of heterogeneity. It does not aim to recover the full distribution of preferences nonparametrically; instead, it focuses on the conditional mean of labour supply as a robust object for estimation and policy prediction. 

The paper is explicit that, by estimating only the conditional mean ( \bar h(x)), one generally cannot identify both the structural labour-supply function (\psi(y,w,v)) and the distribution (g(v)). The approach therefore sacrifices full preference recovery for robustness and tractability. This is an important limitation relative to fully structural random-utility models. 

Preferences are also assumed to be convex, because the central decomposition relies on convex preferences and convex budget sets. This assumption is part of the economic structure used to reduce dimensionality. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is important for your project because it clarifies the sense in which “constraints” enter the paper. The paper does not model opportunities or constraints as latent job-offer sets, hours restrictions from employers, or demand-side rationing. The feasible object is the budget set induced by the tax system. Thus, constraints are entirely budgetary/institutional in the main model. 

The paper therefore does not distinguish preference heterogeneity from opportunity heterogeneity in the sense that matters for RURO or job-opportunity-set models. The individual does not choose from a restricted set of jobs; the paper instead asks how hours respond to a nonlinear budget frontier. In that sense it assumes a universal hours opportunity set, conditional on the budget frontier, except for limited discussion of nonconvexities and their approximation. 

There is one related but narrower contribution: the paper shows that nonlinear budget sets themselves are rich enough to matter, and that misspecifying them can distort estimated labour-supply responses. That is useful for your project because it highlights the role of (y)-type objects, i.e. pay/tax schedules, even when (A)-type opportunity sets are not explicitly modeled. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is almost entirely positive. Its main objects are labour-supply functions, wage elasticities, and predicted changes in expected hours under tax reform. There is some discussion of possible consumer-surplus calculations based on (\bar\psi(y,w)), but welfare analysis is not developed as the main object of the paper. 

There is no explicit social welfare function, no fairness principle, and no analysis of responsibility or compensation. The paper is not designed to evaluate well-being in a normative sense; it is designed to estimate behavioural responses to nonlinear budget sets in a robust way. 

For your purposes, the main normative relevance is indirect: the paper provides a method for estimating how choices vary with the pay/tax schedule without strong parametric assumptions. # Main findings

The main methodological finding is that nonparametric estimation of labour supply with nonlinear budget sets is feasible once one uses the restrictions implied by utility maximization with convex preferences and convex piecewise-linear budget sets. The resulting estimators are consistent, asymptotically normal, and can handle different numbers of budget segments across individuals without a deterioration in accuracy driven by the number of segments itself. 

Empirically, the paper finds large differences between parametric and nonparametric estimates in the Swedish application. The parametric MLE implies a wage elasticity of about 0.123 and a stylized tax-reform effect estimate ( \hat M ) of about 0.0546, whereas the nonparametric series estimates produce substantially smaller reform effects, around 0.026 to 0.034 depending on the specification, with the preferred nonparametric estimates around 60 percent of the parametric estimate. Tables I and II are the key evidence. 

The paper also finds evidence against the Gaussian specification underlying the parametric benchmark. The IV-based specification checks and the density plots in Figures 1 and 2 suggest strong departures from normality in the residual distribution. This is part of the reason the authors give for preferring the more flexible nonparametric approach. 

# Main limitations

The first main limitation is that the paper estimates only the conditional mean of hours rather than the full structural distribution of preferences. The authors are explicit that one generally cannot identify both the structural labour-supply function (\psi(y,w,v)) and the distribution (g(v)) from the single conditional-mean restriction alone. This is a deliberate trade-off. 

A second limitation is that the method is tailored to nonlinear budget sets, not to explicit opportunity-set heterogeneity. It therefore cannot separate preferences from job availability, restricted hours offers, or labour-demand-side constraints. This is a major limitation for projects centered on feasible job sets (A). 

A third limitation is the treatment of nonconvexities. The authors allow for some nonconvexities by additional small-dimensional terms, and they argue that nonconvexities may be small in practice or averaged out by heterogeneity. But the core theory and estimator are driven by convex preferences and convex budget sets. Where nonconvexities are substantively important, the approximation may be less satisfactory. 

A fourth limitation is that the empirical application is confined to working Swedish men with very few unemployed. This makes the method less immediately informative about extensive-margin participation, female labour supply, or environments where sample selection is a first-order issue. The authors explicitly note that extending the framework to simultaneous participation and hours choice is beyond the scope of the paper. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is useful for framing the importance of the pay/tax schedule as a nontrivial determinant of observed labour supply. It shows that getting the budget frontier right matters greatly, and that parametric assumptions can materially distort estimated policy responses. That is a useful point in any project that wants to distinguish the role of (y)-type objects from preferences. 

## possible use for model design

It is useful for model design if your empirical model keeps the feasible set broad and focuses on the nonlinear budget schedule. In particular, the paper suggests a way to estimate behaviour flexibly when the main source of nonlinearity is the tax schedule rather than latent job offers. It is less useful if your design goal is to model restricted job sets directly. 

## possible use for identification

High relevance. The paper is fundamentally about identification and estimation under nonlinear budget sets. It provides a clean example of how economic theory can reduce dimensionality and make semistructural nonparametric estimation feasible. # Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is that the paper’s robustness is purchased by narrowing the estimand. Once the object of interest is only the conditional mean of hours, the approach no longer identifies the full distribution of preferences or the underlying labour-supply function. For applications where the distinction between preferences and opportunities is central, that is a serious limitation.
