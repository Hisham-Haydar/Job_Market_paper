# Full citation

Brunori, Paolo, Francisco H. G. Ferreira, and Vito Peragine. 2013. “Inequality of Opportunity, Income Inequality and Economic Mobility: Some International Comparisons.” IZA Discussion Paper No. 7155. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper provides a cross-country comparative synthesis of two empirically implementable ex-ante opportunity-sensitive measures—an inequality of economic opportunity index (IEO) and the Human Opportunity Index (HOI)—and relates them to development, overall inequality, and intergenerational mobility. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is one of the clearest comparative empirical overviews of the inequality-of-opportunity literature, and it is explicit about both the normative principles behind the measures and the practical limits of comparability across studies. It is especially useful if one wants a broad empirical map of how “unfair” inequality due to circumstances varies across countries. 

For your research direction, the paper is useful less for model mechanics and more for conceptual positioning. It clarifies the compensation-versus-responsibility logic, the ex-ante/ex-post distinction, and the empirical meaning of between-type inequality attributed to circumstances. It does not, however, model feasible job sets or latent opportunities in a structural labor-supply sense. 

# Core research question

Can one make reasonably meaningful international comparisons of inequality of opportunity using the two families of measures that have been applied widely enough across countries—ex-ante inequality of economic opportunity and the Human Opportunity Index—and what are their empirical associations with GDP per capita, overall inequality, and intergenerational mobility? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is situated in the development and distributive-justice literature. Its starting point is that inequality is often viewed as objectionable not only for instrumental reasons but also because some of it is regarded as unfair. The relevant unfairness criterion is the now-standard equality-of-opportunity idea that inequalities due to circumstances beyond individual control should be compensated, whereas inequalities due to effort or responsibility may be acceptable. 

The empirical setting is international and synthetic rather than structural. The paper assembles country-level evidence from eight studies for the inequality of economic opportunity index and from World Bank-style HOI applications for a separate set of countries. The countries span rich and poor economies, with Europe, Latin America, Africa, North America, Asia, and the Middle East represented in the IEO sample. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The framework is not a structural behavioral model. It is a normative-measurement framework derived from the welfare-economics literature on inequality of opportunity, with specific emphasis on the distinction between circumstances and effort, and on the ex-ante versus ex-post approaches. 

Formally, the paper considers an advantage variable (y), a vector of discrete circumstance variables (C), and an effort variable (e). The population is partitioned into **types**, which are homogeneous in circumstances, and—under the Roemer identification assumption—into **tranches**, which group individuals at the same percentile of their type-specific advantage distribution. This provides the conceptual basis for the ex-ante and ex-post compensation principles. 

The paper itself focuses mainly on ex-ante empirical measures. Under the ex-ante approach, one replaces each individual’s observed advantage with a valuation of the opportunity set of her type, often the type mean, and computes inequality over the resulting smoothed distribution. The framework is normative and descriptive, not positive in the sense of modeling optimizing choice. 

There is no explicit feasible-set model in the sense of job opportunities, hours menus, or latent offers. “Opportunity” here refers to the normative equality-of-opportunity literature, not to explicit economic opportunity sets (A_i) as in a jobs model. 

# Key objects

The main objects are the ex-ante inequality of economic opportunity measures (IEO\text{-}L) and (IEO\text{-}R), where (IEO\text{-}L) is the inequality level in the smoothed distribution and (IEO\text{-}R) is the ratio of that opportunity-inequality measure to total inequality in the underlying advantage variable. The paper also distinguishes parametric and non-parametric estimates of these indices. 

The second core object is the Human Opportunity Index:
[
H_j=\bar p_j(1-D_j),
]
where (\bar p_j) is average coverage of service (j) and (D_j) is a dissimilarity index across circumstance-defined types. The HOI is explicitly described as a development index penalized for unequal opportunities, not as a pure inequality-of-opportunity measure. 

Other key comparative objects are GNI per capita, total inequality, the intergenerational elasticity of income, and the intergenerational correlation of education. 

# Data

The paper does not estimate one harmonized micro dataset. Instead, it synthesizes previously estimated country-specific measures from eight studies for the IEO side and from World Bank/related HOI applications for the HOI side. 

For IEO, the sample covers 41 countries. The underlying data differ by study: EU-SILC 2005 for many European countries; Latin American household surveys such as PNAD, ECV, ENV, and ENAHO; Turkey’s TDHS and HBS; South Africa’s NIDS; the US PSID; India’s IHDS; and several African household surveys such as GLSS, EICVM, EPAM, and NIHS. The outcome variable also varies across studies: post-tax individual earnings, gross income, household per capita income, household per capita consumption, or imputed per capita consumption. 

For HOI, the paper reports values for 39 countries in Africa and Latin America, with dimensions including school attendance, access to water, access to electricity, access to sanitation, and completion of primary school on time. 

# Identification logic

This is not an identification paper in the causal or structural-econometric sense. The key inferential move is normative and statistical: define types by observed circumstances, compute type-level opportunity valuations, and measure inequality across those type-based valuations. 

For the IEO measures, the crucial practical claim is that the observed-circumstance measures are **lower bounds** on inequality of opportunity. The intuition is that the empirically observed circumstance variables form only a strict subset of all true circumstances. If more circumstance variables were observed, the estimated opportunity-inequality component could only rise. The paper stresses that this lower-bound logic holds for the overall IEO measures, whether parametric or non-parametric. 

There is therefore no clean exogenous variation isolating one channel. Instead, the method attributes between-type variation in smoothed outcomes to observed circumstances and interprets that as a lower-bound unfair component. This is fundamentally different from identifying preference parameters or opportunity sets in a structural labor-supply model. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy has two parts. First, the paper collects existing estimates of ex-ante inequality of economic opportunity from eight studies, reports them for 41 countries, and compares them descriptively. Second, it assembles HOI values for 39 countries and compares those as well. It then studies cross-country correlations between these measures and other macro/distributional indicators. 

The IEO measure is based on a smoothed distribution in which individuals are assigned the mean advantage of their type. In some studies the smoothed distribution is estimated non-parametrically from observed type means; in others it is approximated parametrically by predicting advantage from a reduced-form regression on circumstance variables. The paper notes that parametric estimates tend to be slightly lower than non-parametric ones, consistent with their conservative character. 

The paper is candid about comparability problems. Studies differ in the outcome variable used, the set of observed circumstances, the number of types, and whether estimation is parametric, non-parametric, or semi-parametric. These differences do not invalidate broad comparison, but they do require caution in interpretation. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are not modeled explicitly in the empirical comparison. The equality-of-opportunity framework distinguishes effort from circumstances, but not heterogeneous preferences in the usual structural or welfare-comparison sense. The empirical measures attribute observed between-type inequality to circumstances, without separately identifying preference heterogeneity. 

Conceptually, the paper does touch the responsibility domain by discussing effort, reward, and compensation principles. But effort is largely treated through Roemer’s identification assumption or through the structure of the ex-ante measurement approach, not through direct modeling of preferences or choice behavior. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is central for your project, but the paper’s notion of opportunity differs from yours. Opportunities are represented by **type-level advantage prospects** associated with observed circumstances, or by access probabilities to basic services, not by explicit feasible job sets, latent menus, or demand-side labor-market constraints. 

So the paper does not model opportunities explicitly in a RURO or discrete-choice labor-supply sense. It does not distinguish observed choices from a latent availability distribution of jobs, and it does not model hours restrictions, occupational menus, or personalized wage-offer sets. 

What it does do is distinguish **circumstance-driven inequality** from inequality associated with effort or responsibility, at least under the ex-ante lower-bound methodology. It therefore helps with normative classification of inequality sources, but not with structural separation of preference heterogeneity from opportunity heterogeneity in the sense of (R) versus (A). # Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative in motivation, but its operative objects are inequality indices rather than a direct welfare function. The central normative idea is that inequalities due to circumstances are unfair and should be compensated, whereas inequalities due to effort may be acceptable. 

The ex-ante IEO measures are the paper’s main operationalization of unfair inequality. In particular, (IEO\text{-}R) is interpreted as the lower-bound share of total inequality due to observed circumstances. The HOI, by contrast, is not itself an inequality-of-opportunity measure; it is a development index penalized by dissimilarity across types. 

The paper is useful for thinking about compensation for circumstances. It is much less useful for responsibility for opportunities in a formal feasible-set sense, for reference opportunity sets, or for an explicit welfare object that combines realized bundle, preferences, feasible set, and pay schedule. 

# Main findings

The paper reports substantial cross-country variation in the lower-bound share of total inequality attributable to opportunity. In Table 1, (IEO\text{-}R) ranges from 0.02 in Norway to 0.34 in Guatemala; Brazil is also very high at about 0.32. In level terms, (IEO\text{-}L) ranges from 0.003 in Norway and 0.005 in Slovenia to 0.199 in Guatemala and 0.223 in Brazil. 

For the HOI, the paper reports a range from roughly 10 in Niger to about 92 in Chile across 39 countries. Figure 7 on page 29 visually shows the near-separation between the African and Latin American subsamples, with the Latin American countries mostly concentrated at much higher HOI levels. 

The paper finds an inverted-U relationship between inequality of economic opportunity and development, measured by log per capita GNI. The authors describe this as a “Kuznets curve” for opportunity inequality. Figure 3 on page 27 displays this relationship, and the text reports significant coefficients on both the linear and quadratic terms. 

The paper also finds a positive cross-country correlation between inequality of opportunity and overall inequality. Figure 4 on page 27 shows the association, and the text reports a correlation coefficient of 0.523. 

A further result is that the IEO index is negatively associated with intergenerational mobility. Figure 5 on page 28 shows a positive relationship between (IEO\text{-}R) and the intergenerational income elasticity, with a correlation of 0.5853 across the countries for which both are available. Figure 6 on page 28 shows a similarly strong positive association between (IEO\text{-}R) and the intergenerational correlation of education, with a correlation of 0.5965. Since both mobility indicators are persistence measures, these findings imply that more inequality of opportunity is associated with less intergenerational mobility. 

The HOI is found to be extremely highly correlated with the Human Development Index. Figure 8 on page 29 shows a correlation of 0.941. The paper interprets this as evidence that most of the cross-country variation in HOI is driven by mean service coverage rather than by the inequality penalty term. 

Finally, the paper emphasizes that the HOI’s internal dissimilarity index and the IEO-R index can produce very different country rankings. In the common subsample, the correlation is actually negative, and Figure 9 on page 30 illustrates this contrast. The authors explicitly warn that different ex-ante measures of opportunity may capture quite different things. 

# Main limitations

The first limitation is comparability. The country estimates are assembled from different studies using different outcome variables, different survey years, different circumstance vectors, different numbers of types, and different estimation methods. The paper is transparent about these issues, and they must temper any strong ranking claims. 

A second limitation is that the IEO measures are only lower bounds, because observed circumstances are inevitably incomplete. This is conceptually useful, but it means the paper cannot claim to measure the full unfair component of inequality. 

A third limitation for your project is that the opportunity concept is not a feasible-set concept. The paper does not model actual job opportunities, offer distributions, or constraints on labor-market access in the structural sense. # Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is useful primarily for the **circumstances vs. effort** distinction, not for the **opportunities vs. preferences** distinction in the sense relevant to structural labor economics. It does not model heterogeneous preferences directly, and it does not define opportunities as explicit individualized sets of feasible jobs or options. 

Its main lesson for that debate is that empirical inequality-of-opportunity work often uses observed type-level circumstances as proxies for unfair opportunity differences. That is informative, but it is much cruder than a framework in which opportunities are modeled as explicit sets (A_i) and are normatively evaluated jointly with realized outcomes and preferences. # Useful quotations / formulas

A central formula is the relative inequality of economic opportunity measure:
[
IEO\text{-}R=\frac{IEO\text{-}L}{\text{total inequality}},
]
where (IEO\text{-}L) is the inequality level in the smoothed distribution generated by type means. This is the paper’s core comparative statistic. 

A second central formula is the Human Opportunity Index:
[
H_j=\bar p_j(1-D_j),
]
which the paper interprets as average access to service (j) penalized by unequal opportunities across types. 

A short quote that captures the paper’s basic principle is: “inequalities due to circumstances beyond individual control are unfair, and should be compensated for, while inequalities due to factors for which people can be held responsible … may be considered acceptable.”

# My quick takeaway

This is a useful benchmark paper for the broad empirical equality-of-opportunity literature. Its strongest value for your project is not in model design but in framing and comparison: it shows how the literature operationalizes “unfair inequality” across countries, what kinds of correlations emerge, and how much depends on the precise measure chosen. It is therefore a good contextual reference for a jobs-and-well-being project, but it is not itself a direct template for modeling (A), (R), and (y) separately.
