# Full citation

Blundell, Richard, Alan Duncan, Julian McCrae, and Costas Meghir. 2000. “The Labour Market Impact of the Working Families’ Tax Credit.” *Fiscal Studies* 21(1): 75–104. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper uses a structural discrete-hours labour-supply model combined with tax-benefit microsimulation to predict how the UK Working Families’ Tax Credit (WFTC) would affect labour-market participation and hours among lone parents and couples with children. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is a clear early example of the modern tax-benefit microsimulation tradition in labour economics: detailed household budget constraints are fed into a structural labour-supply model, and policy effects are obtained by simulation rather than reduced-form extrapolation. That makes it directly relevant for your interests in structural labour supply, discrete choice, and microsimulation. 

It is also useful because it shows, in a transparent way, how a reform aimed at “making work pay” can generate opposite labour-supply responses across household members. In particular, the paper highlights the asymmetry between lone mothers and secondary earners in couples. This is conceptually important for any project that wants to distinguish who is affected by the budget set, and through which margin. 

# Core research question

What are the likely effects of replacing Family Credit with the Working Families’ Tax Credit on labour-force participation and hours of work among lone parents and couples with children in the UK? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper studies the UK reform that replaced Family Credit with WFTC in October 1999. WFTC increased the generosity of in-work support through a higher threshold, a lower taper rate, larger child credits for younger children, and a substantially more generous childcare subsidy. The policy target groups are lone parents and couples with children. 

The institutional setting is therefore a tax-benefit system with strong non-linearities, interactions with housing benefit and council tax benefit, and eligibility conditions tied to hours of work. The paper is explicitly about in-work support and employment incentives, not about job search frictions, matching, or labor-demand-side rationing. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a structural discrete labour-supply model with tax-benefit microsimulation. Household behaviour is modelled over a discrete set of hours choices rather than over a continuous hours range, and the model incorporates non-convex budget sets induced by taxes and benefits. The paper states that it uses an econometric model of the work behaviour of lone parents and couples developed in Blundell et al. (1999), and that this model includes controls for fixed costs, childcare costs, and unobserved heterogeneity. The exact functional form is not reproduced in full in this paper. 

The agent’s choice variable is hours of work. For lone parents, this is the labour-supply decision of one adult. For couples, labour supply is modelled at the household level, with the paper later reporting marginal responses for each household member. The set of hours choices used in simulation is discrete, specifically ({0,10,20,30,40}). 

The feasible set is represented by the household-specific budget constraint over these discrete hours points, where net income at each point is generated by the TAXBEN tax-benefit simulator. Childcare costs are added as work-related costs, using observed relationships between hours worked and childcare use. Opportunities in the sense of job availability or feasible job sets are not modeled explicitly. The framework is positive and policy-evaluative, not normative in the axiomatic welfare sense. 

# Key objects

The central empirical objects are household net income at alternative hours choices under Family Credit and under WFTC, the resulting budget constraints, estimated wages for workers and imputed wages for non-workers, childcare costs by demographic type and hours, and the probabilities of choosing each hours point under the discrete labour-supply model. 

The key policy objects are the elements of WFTC itself: higher child credits for children under 11, a higher income threshold, a lower taper rate, and the childcare credit worth 70 per cent of childcare costs up to stated weekly caps. These objects matter because they reshape the budget constraints differently across lone parents, first earners, and second earners. 

# Data

The main data source is the UK Family Resources Survey for 1994–95 and 1995–96, combined to give information on over 50,000 households. For estimation, after exclusions, the paper uses 1,807 single parents and 4,694 two-adult households. Population counts for relevant target groups are also reported using the same data source. 

The tax-benefit system is simulated using TAXBEN, the IFS tax and benefit microsimulation model, which computes taxes and entitlements for each household. Childcare use and expenditure are drawn from the FRS, especially for households whose youngest child is below school age. Tables 2–4 and Appendix Tables A.1–A.2 provide the key descriptive information on childcare types, expenditure, hourly prices, and the relationship between hours of work and hours of childcare. 

# Identification logic

Identification in the causal sense is not the main contribution of this paper. The results are generated by structural simulation. The crucial variation comes from the change in the tax-benefit schedule between Family Credit and WFTC, translated into alternative budget constraints via TAXBEN, and then mapped into behaviour through an estimated discrete labour-supply model. 

The key assumptions are therefore parametric and structural rather than quasi-experimental. They include the validity of the labour-supply model estimated in the companion paper, the wage imputation procedure for non-workers, the discrete-hours representation, the treatment of childcare costs and use, and the assumption that the childcare market structure does not itself change in the baseline simulations. The paper is explicit that it does not model adaptation of the childcare market and does not incorporate other contemporaneous labour-market reforms in detail. 

Identification is therefore mainly parametric and model-based. If one asks what variation separately identifies preferences from constraints, the answer is: only through the maintained structure of the model and observed heterogeneity, not through a design-based source of exogenous variation. # Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy combines three ingredients. First, the FRS provides a large sample of the relevant family types. Second, TAXBEN computes household-specific net incomes under the pre-reform and post-reform systems at multiple hours points. Third, a structural discrete labour-supply model maps these budget sets into probabilities over hours choices. 

For workers, current observed wages are used; for non-workers, wages are predicted from a wage equation based on characteristics. Labour-supply responses are obtained by comparing predicted hours distributions before and after the reform. The paper also conducts sensitivity exercises for alternative assumptions on childcare-credit take-up, childcare prices, and entry wages for non-workers. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are represented through the estimated structural labour-supply model with unobserved heterogeneity. The paper does not develop a preference theory beyond labour-supply behaviour over hours and net income, plus fixed and childcare costs. It is therefore a standard structural behavioural treatment of preferences rather than a welfare-theoretic one. 

Heterogeneity in preferences is allowed statistically, but the paper does not attempt to identify a deep decomposition between preference heterogeneity and opportunity heterogeneity. Nor does it discuss whether heterogeneous preferences should be respected or neutralized in welfare evaluation. Those are outside its scope. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is crucial for your project. The paper does not model opportunities explicitly as feasible job sets, latent offer distributions, or individual-specific opportunity correspondences. There is no RURO-type structure, no job density, and no demand-side model of job availability. The framework is therefore not an opportunity-set model in your sense. 

What the paper does model very carefully are financial and work-related constraints. These include nonlinear budget constraints generated by taxes and benefits, the 16-hour eligibility threshold for Family Credit/WFTC, childcare costs, housing benefit interactions, and fixed costs of work. In other words, it treats constraints primarily as institutional and cost-based rather than as job-availability constraints. 

The paper therefore helps distinguish preference heterogeneity from budget-constraint heterogeneity, but not preference heterogeneity from opportunity heterogeneity in the labour-market-access sense. It is strongest on the former, largely silent on the latter, and explicitly assumes away adjustments in the childcare market in the main simulation design. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is primarily positive with policy applications. Its core outputs are participation changes, hours changes, and implied programme-cost effects under alternative assumptions. It does not construct EV, CV, equivalent income, or any explicit individual well-being measure. 

There is no explicit normative object corresponding to responsibility for opportunities, compensation for opportunities, or a social welfare function over heterogeneous households. The closest the paper comes to welfare language is in discussing improved work incentives, increased in-work incomes, and changes in programme cost. That is not a welfare analysis in your (W(z,R,A;y)) sense. 

It is therefore not useful as a direct source on reference opportunity sets or decomposition of inequality into opportunities and preferences. Its value lies instead in the positive modelling of how reforms reshape household budget constraints and work behaviour. 

# Main findings

The headline finding is that WFTC is predicted to raise participation among single mothers by about 2.2 percentage points in the baseline full-take-up scenario. Table 7 reports the implied transition matrix, and the conclusion section translates this into about 34,000 individuals. 

For married women with employed partners, the paper predicts a fall in participation of about 0.57 percentage points, equivalent to around 20,000 individuals. The mechanism is an income effect operating through improved in-work benefit eligibility of the husband, which makes non-participation more attractive for some secondary earners. This is shown in Table 8 and discussed explicitly in the text. 

For married women with non-working partners, participation is predicted to rise by about 1.32 percentage points. For men in couples, the net overall effect is close to zero, because entry into work among some men is nearly offset by exit among others whose partners are employed. Tables 9 and 10 contain these results. 

Aggregating the behavioural effects across groups, the paper finds a small overall increase in participation of roughly 30,000 individuals. It also reports that behavioural responses reduce the cost of the WFTC programme by about 14 per cent relative to a no-behaviour, full-take-up scenario. 

Sensitivity analysis in Table 11 shows that results depend on childcare-credit take-up, childcare prices, and assumed entry wages. For example, lower take-up of the childcare credit reduces the positive response of single parents, while higher childcare prices weaken or can offset favourable participation effects. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that opportunities are not modeled explicitly. The paper has no direct object corresponding to feasible job sets or job availability. This matters for your project because the estimated response is driven by observed or imputed wages and institutional budget constraints, not by variation in (A). 

A second limitation is that identification is heavily structural. The simulations depend on the validity of the discrete labour-supply model, wage predictions for non-workers, and the maintained relationship between hours of work and childcare use. There is no quasi-experimental validation within this paper. 

A third limitation is the treatment of childcare. The paper explicitly states that it does not allow the childcare market to adapt to WFTC in the baseline case. Yet the reform itself could alter both demand for childcare and equilibrium childcare prices. The authors recognise this and only approximate the issue through sensitivity exercises. 

A fourth limitation is that the paper does not provide a welfare interpretation beyond labour-supply and programme-cost effects. # Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is useful for framing the empirical side of how tax-benefit reforms affect labour supply differently across household roles. It provides a clear example of why “making work pay” for households need not imply stronger work incentives for all individuals within those households. 

## possible use for model design

It is useful for model design if you want to build a structural labour-supply model with detailed tax-benefit rules, childcare costs, and discrete hours choices. It is especially helpful as a template for combining microsimulation with structural behaviour. It is not useful as a template for modelling latent opportunity sets. 

## possible use for identification

Its main use here is cautionary. It is a good example of structurally simulated policy evaluation where identification is maintained by model assumptions rather than research design. # Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is that the paper interprets labour-supply responses through a rich budget-constraint model but leaves labour-market opportunities implicit. For a reform whose practical impact may depend on whether suitable jobs and childcare arrangements are actually available, that is a non-trivial omission. The structural simulations are internally coherent, but the opportunity side of the environment is thinner than the tax-benefit side.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

The paper is much stronger on preferences versus budget constraints than on opportunities versus preferences. It allows heterogeneous behavioural responses and models complex institutional constraints carefully, but it does not introduce unequal feasible job sets as a separate explanatory factor. 

That makes it informative for your project only up to a point. It shows how much can be learned when the environment is summarized by wages, hours choices, childcare costs, and tax-benefit rules. It does not tell you whether observed labour-supply inequality is due to different opportunity sets, because those are not modeled. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The core methodological description in the abstract is that the authors use “a discrete behavioural model of household labour supply with controls for fixed and childcare costs, and unobserved heterogeneity.” That is the key model-class description in the paper. 

The paper explicitly states that the simulation uses the discrete choice set ({0,10,20,30,40}) hours, with TAXBEN generating net incomes at each point. This is central for understanding what is and is not being modeled. 

The most important numerical result is the baseline participation increase of about 2.2 percentage points for single mothers, alongside a participation fall of 0.57 percentage points for married women with employed partners, yielding only a small overall participation increase. Tables 7–10 and the conclusion on page 101 are the key locations for these findings.
