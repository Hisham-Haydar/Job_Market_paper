# Full citation

Fleurbaey, Marc, and François Maniquet. 2007. “Help the Low Skilled or Let the Hardworking Thrive? A Study of Fairness in Optimal Income Taxation.” *Journal of Public Economic Theory* 9(3): 467–500. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper studies optimal income taxation under heterogeneous skills and heterogeneous consumption-leisure preferences, derives two fairness-based families of social objectives—egalitarian-equivalent and equivalent-budget—and shows that both can justify tax exemption for incomes below the minimum wage, with stronger results in some cases such as maximizing the minimum income when the lowest skill is zero.

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it directly addresses a central normative tension in redistribution with heterogeneous preferences: should society primarily compensate low skill, or should it protect the rewards of those who choose to work harder? The paper turns that ethical tension into explicit social objectives and then studies what those objectives imply for the shape of the optimal tax schedule under incentive constraints. 

For your project, the paper is especially valuable because it sits exactly at the interface between fairness theory and optimal tax design in a model where both skills and preferences vary. It is not a paper about latent job sets or empirical opportunity distributions, but it is highly relevant for thinking about which inequalities should be compensated and which should be respected when labour choices reflect heterogeneous preferences.

# Core research question

When individuals differ both in earning ability and in consumption-leisure preferences, what kinds of fairness principles can justify particular social objectives, and what tax-schedule features follow from those objectives once incentive compatibility is imposed? 

# Economic setting and context

The setting is a theoretical optimal-income-tax model with two goods, labour and consumption, and a population of agents differing in wage rates and ordinal preferences over labour and consumption. The paper explicitly positions itself against both indiscriminate equalization of income differences and purely welfarist aggregation of heterogeneous utilities. 

The paper is not a country study. It does, however, connect its normative dilemma to contemporary welfare-state debates and occasionally comments on policy ideas such as basic income, unemployment support, and low-income tax exemption. These policy remarks are illustrative rather than empirically estimated applications.

# Model / theoretical framework

The model is a second-best optimal-tax model with heterogeneous skills and heterogeneous consumption-leisure preferences. A bundle is (z_i=(\ell_i,c_i)), where (\ell_i) is labour and (c_i) consumption. Agents have continuous, convex, monotonic preferences (R_i), and skill is summarized by a wage rate (w_i). Earnings are (y_i=w_i\ell_i). The paper assumes that different preferences can be ordered by willingness to work through a single-crossing-type property in the ((\ell,c)) space. 

In the first-best benchmark, redistribution is via lump-sum transfers and budgets are (B^f(t_i,w_i)). In the second-best setting, only earned income is observed, so redistribution is via an income tax function (\tau(y)), with budget set (B^s(\tau,w_i)={(y,c)\in[0,w_i]\times\mathbb{R}_+ \mid c\le y-\tau(y)}). The relevant constraints are feasibility and incentive compatibility, the latter formalized in the earnings-consumption space using rescaled preferences (R_i^*). 

The framework is explicitly normative. The paper does not begin from a fixed utilitarian social welfare function. Instead, it introduces fairness principles and then derives two classes of social preferences from them: egalitarian-equivalent preferences and equivalent-budget preferences. These become the evaluative criteria used for optimal tax design.

# Key objects

The first key object is the implicit budget (IB(z,w,R)): the budget line of slope (w) tangent to the agent’s indifference curve at bundle (z). The associated scalar measure is the implicit transfer (IT(z,w,R)), defined as the smallest lump-sum transfer needed at wage (w) to make the agent weakly able to attain a bundle at least as good as (z). The paper interprets (IT) as a money-metric representation based only on ordinal preferences. 

The second key object is the family of egalitarian-equivalent social preferences, denoted EE, which apply maximin to the vector ((IT(z_1,\tilde w,R_1),\dots,IT(z_n,\tilde w,R_n))) for a chosen reference skill (\tilde w). These social preferences are aligned with helping the low skilled. 

The third key object is the family of equivalent-budget social preferences, denoted EB, which apply maximin to ((\tilde U\max IB(z_1,w_1,R_1),\dots,\tilde U\max IB(z_n,w_n,R_n))) for a chosen reference preference (\tilde R) represented by (\tilde U). These social preferences are aligned with letting the hardworking thrive. 

A fourth key object is the tax schedule (\tau(y)), especially its behavior over low incomes and at the minimum skill (w_m). In the general results, the paper characterizes optimality through bounds on (\tau(y)) and, in particular, through properties of (\tau(0)) and (\tau(w_m)). 

# Data

There is no empirical dataset and no estimation exercise. The paper is theorem-driven and uses geometric illustrations, a stylized two-by-two type structure for intuition, and general-population results under a No Identification assumption.

# Identification logic

This is not an econometric identification paper. The key logic is normative and analytical. Fairness principles are used to justify two kinds of social objectives, and these objectives are then embedded in a Mirrlees-type second-best tax problem. The question is not how to identify preferences from data, but how particular ethical commitments restrict the shape of optimal taxes.

Within the second-best model, the critical informational restriction is that only earned income is observed. This creates the standard self-selection constraints. In the general case, the paper adds a No Identification assumption requiring that pretax income not perfectly reveal higher productivity over low-income ranges; formally, for any higher-skill agent and any lower wage (w), there exists a wage-(w) agent with the same restricted preferences over ([0,w]\times\mathbb{R}_+). This assumption is what allows the low-income part of the envelope to be interpreted as relevant for the least-skilled subpopulation. 

Identification is therefore structural in the mechanism-design sense and ethical in the welfare sense. It is not quasi-experimental, not semiparametric, and not designed to recover primitives from observed microdata. [explicit in paper]

# Estimation / empirical strategy

There is no estimation. The paper proceeds by defining the model, introducing fairness principles, deriving social preferences, analyzing a stylized two-by-two type case, and then proving general results for tax design. Figures 12–19 are central for the geometric reasoning in the second-best problem. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are central and heterogeneous. The paper explicitly allows individuals to differ in their willingness to work, and it treats that heterogeneity as ethically relevant. In particular, one of the two fairness principles—Let Hardworking Thrive—states that differences in outcomes due purely to different preferences, holding skill fixed, need not be corrected and may deserve respect. 

At the same time, the paper does not aggregate raw utilities. Instead, it constructs welfare criteria from ordinal preferences through implicit transfers or implicit budgets. This is one of the paper’s main theoretical moves: it uses preferences, but not in a standard utilitarian way. 

The choice of the reference skill (\tilde w) in EE and the reference preference (\tilde R) in EB matters materially. The paper shows that these choices change the degree and direction of redistribution. A larger (\tilde w) in EE tends to favor hardworking low-skilled agents more strongly; a lower willingness-to-work reference preference in EB can reduce redistribution substantially and in the limit can make laissez-faire optimal. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

The paper does not model opportunities explicitly in your sense of feasible job sets, latent offers, or opportunity correspondences (A_i). The relevant environment is a tax-distorted budget set plus an incentive-compatibility structure generated by unobservable skill. 

Constraints are therefore budget and mechanism-design constraints, not opportunity-set constraints. The key objects are first-best and second-best budgets, implicit budgets, and self-selection constraints. The paper also assumes an upper bound on labour time, and this matters in the two-by-two case because it can prevent certain mimicking deviations even when after-tax income comparisons might otherwise suggest them. Figure 12 on page 15 is the clearest illustration.

The paper clearly helps distinguish preference heterogeneity from skill heterogeneity, but it does not distinguish preference heterogeneity from opportunity heterogeneity. It is therefore useful for responsibility-versus-compensation questions in a skill-preference environment, but not for explicit treatment of actual or reference opportunity sets. # Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative. Its welfare objects are not utilitarian welfare functions over heterogeneous utilities. Instead, it studies two maximin families: EE based on implicit transfers relative to a reference skill, and EB based on the value of implicit budgets according to a reference preference. 

This is highly relevant for responsibility versus compensation. EE embodies the priority of helping the low skilled: it satisfies Help Low Skilled and only a limited special case of Let Hardworking Thrive. EB embodies the opposite priority: it satisfies Let Hardworking Thrive and only a limited special case of Help Low Skilled. The whole paper is structured around this ethical tension.

The paper is therefore directly useful for thinking about whether low-income redistribution should be targeted at low skill, at hardworking behaviour, or at some ethically chosen compromise. It is not, however, a paper about compensation for unequal opportunity sets, and it does not develop a decomposition of inequality into opportunities, preferences, and other factors.

# Main findings

The first main finding is conceptual: the fairness principles “Help Low Skilled” and “Let Hardworking Thrive” generate two different classes of social preferences, EE and EB, and these classes generally point in different redistribution directions in first-best settings. Figures 8 and 11 illustrate how the chosen reference skill or reference preference changes redistribution. 

The second main finding is the two-by-two result. In the stylized case with two skills and two preference types, Proposition 1 states that redistribution among high-willingness-to-work agents must go from high-skilled to low-skilled agents in three of the four benchmark social objectives. Proposition 2 states that an allocation is optimal for (w_1)-EE if and only if it is optimal for (R_2)-EB. This is a sharp example of convergence between the two rival fairness logics in second best. Pages 16–18 and Figure 13 are central here.

The third main finding is the general-population result for the case (w_m=0). If every preference class contains zero-skill agents, the optimal tax for all EE preferences and for EB with reference preference (R^c) maximizes the minimum income (-\tau(0)). This is the paper’s strongest justification for a basic-income-type result. Theorem 1 on page 21 is explicit on this point, and Figure 14 shows the relevant EB intuition. 

The fourth main finding is the general EE result for (w_m>0). Under No Identification and for (\tilde w\ge w_m), the optimal tax can be sought by minimizing (\tau(w_m)) under bounds implying that agents earning (w_m) receive the greatest subsidy and that low incomes are taxed very lightly. In particular, if (\tilde w=w_m), the marginal tax rate is exactly zero over ([0,w_m]); more generally, it can even be negative on that interval. Theorem 2 and Figures 15–16 are central. 

The fifth main finding is the general EB result. Under No Identification, the optimal tax for EB can be sought by minimizing (\tau(w_m)) under an upper bound built from the expenditure function (\tilde E(w,\tilde U_0)). Again, this implies that agents earning (w_m) receive the greatest subsidy and that the marginal tax rate is exactly zero over ([0,w_m]). Theorem 3 and Figures 18–19 are the key locations. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that the framework abstracts from actual opportunity heterogeneity. Skill and preferences are the only relevant individual characteristics, and the whole argument proceeds through tax-distorted budget sets. This is a serious limitation for any project in which unequal feasible job sets matter independently of skill.

A second limitation is that the paper’s sharp general tax results depend on the No Identification assumption. The authors justify it as a way to exclude implausible cases in which pretax income perfectly identifies high-productivity types over some interval, but it remains a substantive richness condition on the joint distribution of skills and preferences. 

A third limitation is that the reference objects (\tilde w) and (\tilde R) are ethically consequential and not uniquely pinned down from within the model. The paper is clear that different reference choices imply different redistribution patterns. So the framework clarifies ethical trade-offs, but does not eliminate them.

1. Can one build an analogue of EE or EB in a framework (W(z,R,A;y)) where (A) is an explicit feasible job set rather than an implicit budget object?

2. When skills are equal but feasible job sets differ, should a “Let Hardworking Thrive” principle still prohibit redistribution, or does unequal opportunity overturn that conclusion?

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

The paper is much stronger on preferences versus skills than on opportunities versus preferences. Its whole normative architecture is built around whether unequal outcomes should be corrected when they come from earning ability rather than from different willingness to work.

For your project, the main value is therefore indirect but substantial. It gives a rigorous example of how one can derive welfare criteria that are neutral with respect to some forms of heterogeneity and compensatory with respect to others. What it does not provide is the next step you need, namely replacing “skill” by, or supplementing it with, an explicit opportunity-set object.

# Useful quotations / formulas

The definition of the implicit transfer is central:
[
IB(z,w,R)=B^f(t,w),
]
where (t) is the minimal transfer such that some bundle in (B^f(t,w)) is weakly preferred to (z). This yields the money-metric object (IT(z,w,R)). 

The two main social objectives are:
[
\text{EE: } \maximin ,(IT(z_1,\tilde w,R_1),\dots,IT(z_n,\tilde w,R_n)),
]
and
[
\text{EB: } \maximin ,(\tilde U\max IB(z_1,w_1,R_1),\dots,\tilde U\max IB(z_n,w_n,R_n)).
]
These formulas summarize the paper’s evaluative core. 

The general EE tax result says that for (\tilde w\ge w_m), one may compute the optimal tax by minimizing (\tau(w_m)) subject to
[
\tau(w_m)\le \tau(y)\le \tau(w_m)+y-w_m+(w_m-y)^+\left(\frac{\tilde w}{w_m}\right).
]
This is the theorem-level expression behind the zero-marginal-tax result on low incomes. 

The EB analogue is:
[
\tau(w_m)\le \tau(y)\le \min_{w\in W}{(y-w)^+-\tilde E(w,\tilde U_0)},
]
with (\tilde U_0=\tilde U\max(B^s(-\tau(w_m),w_m))). This again implies (\tau(y)=\tau(w_m)) on ([0,w_m]).

# My quick takeaway

This is a very important paper for the normative side of your literature corpus. Its main contribution is not empirical realism but ethical precision: it turns a deep fairness conflict into two tractable classes of social preferences and then extracts concrete tax implications from them. For your JMP, it is especially useful as a model of how to derive welfare criteria from principles rather than postulating utility aggregation. But it remains a skill-preference framework, not an opportunities framework. If you want to move toward (W(z,R,A;y)), this paper is a strong guide for the (z)-(R)-(y) side of the evaluative problem, while leaving the (A)-dimension largely untouched.
