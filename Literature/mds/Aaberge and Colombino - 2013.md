# Full citation

Aaberge, Rolf, and Ugo Colombino. 2013. “Using a Microeconometric Model of Household Labour Supply to Design Optimal Income Taxes.” *The Scandinavian Journal of Economics* 115(2): 449–475. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper replaces closed-form optimal-tax formulas with a simulated optimum obtained by iteratively running a structural random-utility labour-supply model with heterogeneous preferences and opportunity densities, under a balanced public-budget constraint, for Norway. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is unusually close to the interface between structural labour supply estimation and welfare design. It does not merely estimate labour-supply elasticities and then plug them into an analytical tax formula; instead, it uses the estimated behavioural model itself as the computational engine for the optimal-tax exercise. That makes it directly relevant for any project that wants to keep together preferences, constraints, and policy simulation in one internally consistent framework. 

It is also important for your project because the paper treats opportunities more explicitly than standard discrete-hours labour-supply models. Agents choose among jobs, and the choice set is represented by a density of available jobs (p(h,w,s)), so heterogeneity in job availability is part of the model rather than being fully collapsed into tastes or wages. This is not yet a fully axiomatic (W(z,R,A;y)) treatment, but it is one of the clearest empirical precursors to separating realized choices from opportunity structure. 

# Core research question

How can one design optimal income-tax schedules empirically by using a flexible microeconometric model of household labour supply, rather than relying on restrictive analytical optimal-tax formulas calibrated with external elasticity inputs? 

# Economic setting and context

The empirical setting is Norway. The behavioural model is estimated on 1994 data from the 1995 Norwegian Survey of Level of Living, with income information from tax records. The authors restrict the sample to individuals aged 20 to 62, exclude the self-employed and those receiving permanent disability benefits, and include couples as well as single women and single men. The sample contains 1,842 couples, 309 single females, and 312 single males. 

The policy context is a personal income-tax design problem. The optimization keeps non-income-tax dimensions of the broader tax-transfer system fixed at their 1994 Norwegian values and searches over a class of piecewise-linear income-tax rules under a constant net-revenue constraint.

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a structural random-utility discrete-choice labour-supply model with continuous job attributes and explicit opportunity densities. For expositional purposes, the paper writes the single-person problem as choosing a job characterized by wage rate (w), hours (h), observed job characteristics (s), and unobserved match characteristics (j), to maximize
[
U(c,h,s,j)
]
subject to
[
c=f(wh,I),
]
where (I) is exogenous non-labour income, (f) is the tax rule, and (B) is the set of available opportunities, including non-market opportunities. 

The framework is positive at the behavioural level and normative at the policy-design level. Behaviour is generated by household-specific utility maximization under heterogeneous preferences and constraints. Normative evaluation is then imposed by the social planner through a common welfare function and a social welfare aggregator.

Opportunities are modeled explicitly, though not as observed individual set-valued objects. Instead, the set (B) is represented probabilistically by a density (p(h,w,s)) of available jobs. This allows different agents to face different numbers and types of market opportunities, including different distributions of hours and wages. Thus, the paper does not assume a universal identical feasible set across individuals. 

For couples, the paper models joint labour-supply decisions. The full couple framework is not rederived in detail in the article itself because it refers back to Aaberge and Colombino (2011), but the optimization problem and later simulations clearly treat the household as making joint choices over both partners’ labour outcomes.

# Key objects

The main behavioural objects are the utility function (U), the systematic component (v), the random component (\varepsilon(j)), the tax rule (f), and the job-opportunity density (p(h,w,s)). The choice density is proportional to systematic attractiveness times opportunity density, so availability enters directly in the likelihood contribution. 

The key welfare object is the planner’s common individual welfare function (V(y,h)), where (y) is equivalent after-tax income and (h) maps into leisure (L=1-(h/0.8736)). The planner then aggregates individual welfare levels using rank-dependent social welfare functions (W_k), including Bonferroni-like, Gini-like, and utilitarian cases.

The tax-policy object is a piecewise-linear income-tax schedule with five marginal tax rates, four bracket cutoffs, and a lump-sum term (d), which may be positive or negative. This is the policy space over which the optimization is conducted. 

# Data

The estimation uses 1994 Norwegian data from the 1995 Norwegian Survey of Level of Living, including detailed tax-record income data. The out-of-sample validation uses 2001 covariates from the 2002 Norwegian Survey of Level of Living to predict 2001 outcomes under the new tax schedule.

The sample used for estimation includes 1,842 couples, 309 single females, and 312 single males. Ages are restricted to 20–62. Self-employed individuals and those on permanent disability benefits are excluded. 

# Identification logic

Identification is primarily structural and parametric. The likelihood contribution comes from the probability density of observed job choices, where the probability of choosing ((h,w,s)) depends on the systematic utility component and the opportunity density (p(h,w,s)), under a type III extreme value assumption for the unobserved match term. Thus, identification relies on functional-form assumptions, the extreme-value structure, and the parametric specification of both preferences and opportunity densities. 

There is no quasi-experimental identification in the modern reduced-form sense. The paper’s strategy is instead to estimate a flexible structural model and then simulate behavioural responses to alternative tax rules. The crucial identifying assumptions are therefore the random-utility specification, the representation of opportunities via (p(h,w,s)), and the stability of estimated behavioural parameters under counterfactual tax changes. That does not solve identification concerns, but it strengthens credibility for policy simulation. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy is maximum likelihood estimation of a random-utility labour-supply model in which the choice density combines systematic utility and opportunity density. The model contains 78 parameters capturing heterogeneity in preferences and opportunities. 

After estimation, the paper computes wage elasticities by stochastic simulation rather than deriving them analytically. It then performs optimal-tax design computationally: for each candidate tax schedule, it simulates household opportunity sets, draws unobserved utility shocks, computes utility-maximizing choices, maps household outcomes into comparable individual welfare levels, aggregates them via a social welfare function, and searches over the tax-parameter space subject to a revenue constraint. Each optimization reportedly evaluates about 200,000 tax rules.

# Treatment of preferences

The paper allows heterogeneous preferences in the behavioural model. Preferences over consumption, leisure, and job characteristics vary across households, and the model includes both observed and unobserved heterogeneity. The systematic component is flexible, and the random component captures utility-relevant match features unobserved by the analyst. 

However, these heterogeneous estimated utility functions are not used directly as interpersonally comparable welfare measures. The authors are explicit that heterogeneous behavioural utility functions cannot simply be aggregated normatively, especially because the sample includes both singles and couples. They therefore introduce a common welfare function (V) for social evaluation. 

This distinction between behavioural preferences and welfare evaluation is analytically important. It is one of the paper’s strongest features for your purposes because it keeps separate the positive model of choice and the normative metric used by the planner.

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is central for your project, and the paper is unusually useful here. It does model opportunities explicitly, though in a probabilistic rather than directly observed set-theoretic way. The household chooses from a set (B) of jobs and non-market opportunities, and the analyst represents this choice set through the density (p(h,w,s)) of available jobs. Jobs may differ by hours, wage rates, and other characteristics; the number and composition of available jobs may differ across agents. 

The paper therefore does not assume a universal deterministic common choice set. Instead, opportunities are heterogeneous across individuals, but they are latent and modeled statistically. This is much closer to RURO or latent-job approaches than standard deterministic discrete-hours labour-supply models. [reasonable inference for my project] supported by 

The model also incorporates quantity constraints and complex, non-convex budget sets created by the tax-transfer system. The conclusion explicitly emphasizes that the approach can accommodate multidimensional heterogeneity of households and jobs, complicated constraints, and quantitative restrictions. 

At the same time, the paper does not identify individual-specific feasible sets (A_i) in the literal sense used in axiomatic opportunity-set models. Instead, it estimates an opportunity distribution. So it helps distinguish both preference heterogeneity and opportunity heterogeneity, but only within a structural-statistical framework rather than a fully explicit observable-feasible-set framework. 

The paper also states that the model does not fully capture all dimensions of effort or taxable-income response. Responses through wage rate, job characteristics, or other effort margins are only partly represented, some through the random utility component. This is a real limitation for opportunity interpretation at the top of the distribution. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is positive with explicit welfare applications. Its normative object is not utility as estimated from the behavioural model, but a planner-imposed common individual welfare function (V(y,h)) defined over equivalent after-tax income and leisure. Couples’ income is equivalized by dividing by (\sqrt{2}), and the welfare function is estimated using the same sample conditional on estimated opportunity densities. 

These individual welfare levels are then aggregated using rank-dependent social welfare functions
[
W=\int_0^1 q(t)F^{-1}(t),dt,
]
with alternative weight profiles corresponding to Bonferroni-like, Gini-like, more moderate inequality aversion, and utilitarian aggregation. The paper explicitly discusses the distributive-weight interpretation and the differing inequality-aversion profiles. 

This is not an explicit responsibility-versus-compensation theory of opportunities. The paper does not ask whether individuals should be held responsible for the opportunity sets they face, nor does it construct a reference opportunity set. It is nevertheless useful for those questions because it separates actual behavioural heterogeneity from the normative standard used by the planner, and because opportunity densities enter the positive model rather than being collapsed into tastes. # Main findings

The main substantive result is that the computed optimal tax schedules feature monotonically increasing marginal tax rates, negative marginal rates on the lowest income bracket, and a 100 percent top marginal tax rate on very high incomes under all social-welfare criteria considered. Relative to the 1994 Norwegian system, the optimal rules imply a lower average tax rate, lower marginal rates on low and average incomes, and higher marginal rates at very high incomes.

Table 7 reports the optimal schedules. Across welfare criteria, the top rate is always 1.00, the first-bracket rate is negative, and the more egalitarian the welfare function, the more progressive the schedule. The paper interprets the negative bottom rate as close in spirit to earnings subsidies such as the EITC or in-work tax credits. 

The behavioural results behind these tax designs are that labour supply is much more elastic for low-income households than for high-income households, and married women are especially elastic relative to married men. Aggregate wage elasticity is low overall, but heterogeneity is substantial across household types and income deciles. Table 1 is central here. 

The out-of-sample exercise indicates that the model reproduces the 2001 income distribution reasonably well. Table 4 shows observed and simulated relative decile incomes for couples, single females, and single males, with simulated values close to observed values in most cells. 

# Main limitations

The central limitation is that identification is structural and heavily parametric. The model’s credibility depends on the random-utility specification, the extreme-value assumption, and the parametric representation of opportunity densities. There is no external experimental or quasi-experimental source of identification. [reasonable inference for my project] supported by 

A second limitation is that opportunities are treated as latent densities rather than observed feasible sets. This is valuable for empirical labour-supply modeling, but it is not equivalent to observing or normatively evaluating actual opportunity sets (A_i). For your (W(z,R,A;y)) framework, this means the paper is closer to a reduced-form structural proxy for (A) than to a direct object of normative analysis. A third limitation is welfare interpretation. The paper solves interpersonal comparability by imposing a common welfare function, but this is a planner’s normative construction rather than a derived ethical theorem about responsibility or compensation. The paper is explicit about this practical move, but it does not justify it through a developed fairness theory of the kind you are interested in.

A fourth limitation is that the model does not fully capture other dimensions of effort, taxable-income adjustment, or mobility responses, especially at the top of the income distribution. The authors themselves state that the 100 percent top marginal rate may be sensitive to omitted behavioural margins such as intercountry mobility and taxable-income response.

Finally, the paper does not provide a decomposition of inequality into preferences, opportunities, and related factors. It is therefore useful more for model architecture than for direct decomposition results.

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is highly useful for framing a bridge between structural labour-supply estimation and normative evaluation. It shows that one can estimate heterogeneous behavioural responses while separately imposing a common welfare metric for policy analysis. That architecture is close to the distinction your project wants between positive choice behavior and normative well-being evaluation.

It is very useful for model design because the opportunity side is not collapsed into a single wage or deterministic hours grid. # Research questions this paper inspires

How can a latent-opportunity labour-supply model with estimated job-availability densities be translated into an explicit well-being framework (W(z,R,A;y)) where (A) is normatively meaningful rather than merely statistically latent?

Under what conditions can a planner’s common welfare function (V) be interpreted as a legitimate reference metric rather than an ad hoc interpersonal-comparability device?

Can one decompose welfare inequality in a structural labour-supply model into components attributable to estimated preferences, estimated opportunity densities, and the tax schedule?

How sensitive are optimal-tax results in latent-job models to alternative representations of the top-end opportunity set, especially when taxable-income responses and mobility are introduced?

Can one construct a responsibility-sensitive variant of this framework in which part of opportunity heterogeneity is compensated and part is treated as attributable to acquired traits or choices?

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is conceptual rather than econometric. Once the paper acknowledges that behavioural utilities are heterogeneous and non-comparable, it solves the problem by imposing a common welfare function. That move is operationally sensible, but normatively under-argued. Why this particular common function, and what fairness principle justifies evaluating all realized bundles through it, remains largely external to the paper’s own argument.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This is one of the more useful papers in the literature for separating opportunities from preferences on the positive side. Preferences are heterogeneous and enter the utility function; opportunities are heterogeneous and enter through the density of available jobs. That is stronger than the usual labour-supply setup in which everyone faces essentially the same menu apart from wages and budget constraints. 

But on the normative side, the paper does not build an explicit theory of opportunities versus preferences. It does not ask which part of disadvantage due to poor opportunities should be compensated, nor whether preference heterogeneity should be neutralized or respected. It therefore helps much more with empirical architecture than with the ethical classification itself.

# Useful quotations / formulas

The key behavioural formula is the choice density:
[
\varphi(h,w,s)=\frac{v[f(wh,I),h,s],p(h,w,s)}{\iiint v[f(xy,I),y,z],p(y,x,z),dx,dy,dz},
]
which makes clear that realized choices reflect both attractiveness and availability. 

The key welfare formula is the planner’s common individual welfare function:
[
\log V(y,h)=\gamma_2\frac{(y^{\gamma_1}-1)}{\gamma_1}+\gamma_4\frac{(L^{\gamma_3}-1)}{\gamma_3}.
]
This is central because it explicitly separates behavioural preferences from the welfare metric used for interpersonal comparison. 

The key social aggregation formula is:
[
W=\int_0^1 q(t)F^{-1}(t),dt,
]
which the paper uses to represent alternative inequality-averse welfare criteria.
