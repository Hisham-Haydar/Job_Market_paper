# One-sentence contribution

The chapter shows how the social ordering functions axiomatized in the unequal-skills model generate concrete second-best tax criteria under private information, and derives strong qualitative implications for optimal tax schedules, especially zero or nonpositive marginal taxation at low incomes. 

# Why this paper matters

This chapter matters because it is the policy-implementation chapter for the axiomatic framework of Chapter 10. It moves from abstract social orderings to actual second-best tax design under informational constraints, which is exactly where a fairness theory becomes operational. 

For your project, the chapter is especially important because it does not merely say that different fairness criteria imply different tax schemes. It shows how the precise ethical compromise between compensation and responsibility maps into concrete properties of tax functions under incentive compatibility. That is a major bridge between axiomatic welfare economics and public-finance design. 

# Core research question

Given the social ordering functions derived in the unequal-skills model, what tax criteria and optimal income tax properties follow once skills and preferences are private information and the policymaker is restricted to incentive-compatible allocations? 

# Economic setting and context

The chapter keeps the unequal-skills labor-supply model of Chapter 10. Agents differ by skill and preferences over labor-consumption bundles, and the policymaker knows the distribution of types but not who is who. The chapter is explicitly about income taxation, because once private information is imposed, incentive-compatible allocations can be represented as tax schemes. 

The setting is purely theoretical and second-best. The policymaker has already chosen an ethical social ordering function and must now implement it under informational constraints. The chapter studies two informational environments: one in which labor time and earnings are observed, and one in which only earnings are observed. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The framework is the unequal-skills model from Chapter 10. An economy is (E=(s_N,R_N)), where each agent has a skill (s_i\in [s_{min},s_{max}]) and preferences (R_i) over bundles ((\ell_i,c_i)). The social objective is given by one of the previously characterized social ordering functions, especially (R^{slex}), (R^{sminlex}), and (R^{\tilde EW}). 

The chapter is normative and second-best rather than positive. It does not estimate preferences or behavioral elasticities from data. Instead, it asks which feasible, incentive-compatible allocations maximize a chosen social ordering under information constraints. 

In the first informational structure, labor time and earnings are observable but skill is not. Agents may then choose to work at a wage rate below their true skill if it benefits them. Incentive compatibility is characterized by the no-envy condition across bundles attainable at one’s own skill. In the second informational structure, labor time is not observable and only earnings are. Incentive compatibility is then defined over earnings-consumption bundles, since agents can imitate the gross earnings of others by adjusting labor supply at their own skill. 

The framework does not model opportunities as explicit job sets. Opportunity heterogeneity remains summarized by scalar skills, as in Chapter 10. The new ingredient is informational structure: the policymaker cannot condition transfers on true skills or preferences directly. 

# Key objects

The central positive-implementation objects are incentive-compatible allocations and their representation as tax schemes. When labor time is observable, allocations can be decentralized by a menu of skill-contingent tax functions ({\tau^w}) on labor time. When labor time is unobservable, allocations can be decentralized by a single earnings tax function (\tau(y)). Lemmas 11.1 and 11.2 are the key formal statements. 

The central normative objects are the three social ordering functions inherited from Chapter 10: (R^{slex}), (R^{sminlex}), and (R^{\tilde EW}). The chapter’s main practical point is that, because these orderings are of the leximin/maximin type and their well-being indexes are informationally simple, the relevant policy problem reduces to identifying the worst-off under the current tax scheme and then studying optimal tax properties. 

A second key object is the reform-evaluation criterion. For each social ordering and each informational structure, the chapter derives a simple expression that identifies who should benefit in priority from a reform. Table 11.1 is the compact summary. 

# Data

There are no empirical data. The chapter is purely theoretical. Its “applications” are analytical characterizations of tax schedules under different assumptions on information and type distributions. 

# Identification logic

There is no econometric identification in this chapter. The relevant analogue is implementation under asymmetric information. The policymaker knows the distribution of skills and preferences but not individual identities, so the feasible policy set is restricted by incentive compatibility. 

The crucial logical step is representability: any incentive-compatible allocation can be represented as a menu of tax functions when labor time is observable, and as a single earnings tax function when labor time is not observable. This is what allows the social-ordering approach to be translated into tax-design criteria. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

None in the empirical sense. The chapter’s strategy is analytical. It first characterizes incentive-compatible allocations via tax functions, then derives criteria for welfare-improving reforms, then studies optimal tax properties first in a simple four-type economy and finally in the general model. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences remain ethically central, as in Chapter 10, because the underlying social orderings differ in how much responsibility they assign to preference-driven labor choices. But operationally, preferences are private information and cannot be directly conditioned upon by the tax authority. 

The chapter’s strong result is that even though preferences are private, the chosen social ordering still induces tractable second-best criteria because the relevant well-being indexes can be inferred from observed choices and tax schedules. This is one of the main reasons the framework remains implementable under informational constraints. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

Opportunities are again represented only through skill. This is narrower than your (A)-type feasible job set framework. The chapter is therefore about unequal earning ability under hidden information, not about explicit opportunity sets, latent jobs, or rationing. 

The main “constraint” object is informational rather than market-structural. The interesting problem is not that agents face different job menus, but that the policymaker cannot observe true skill and, in one case, cannot even observe labor time. Incentive constraints, therefore, are the key mechanism through which opportunity heterogeneity enters tax design. 

# Welfare / normative object

The chapter is explicitly normative. The policy objective is to maximize a chosen social ordering function under incentive compatibility. The main social orderings are exactly those characterized earlier as different compromises between compensation and responsibility. 

This chapter is directly relevant to compensation for opportunities and responsibility for preferences. It is not about opportunity sets as such, but it is squarely about how far one should redistribute when skill differences are ethically salient and when labor-supply choices should partly be respected. 

The chapter is also directly relevant to tax design. Its headline normative result is that all the highlighted social orderings imply zero or nonpositive marginal taxation at low incomes in the relevant second-best settings. That is the strongest policy conclusion of the chapter. 

# Main findings

The first main finding is conceptual: the social ordering approach accommodates different informational structures very naturally. Because the relevant social orderings are of the leximin type and their well-being indexes are informationally simple, the policymaker can evaluate tax systems without needing full information on the distribution of types. 

The second main finding is comparative: social orderings satisfying equal-preferences transfer call for more redistribution than those satisfying equal-skill transfer. This is stated explicitly at the start of the chapter and then reflected in the properties of the resulting tax schemes. 

The third and most important finding is that all the highlighted social orderings imply nonpositive marginal taxation on low incomes. More precisely, when labor time is observable, every second-best optimal allocation for (R^{sminlex}) or (R^{\tilde EW}) can be implemented by tax schedules with zero marginal taxation for the low-skilled, while (R^{slex}) implies nonpositive average marginal taxation for low-skilled agents. This is Theorem 11.1. 

The fourth main finding concerns reform assessment. Table 11.1 shows that under both informational structures, (R^{slex}) and (R^{sminlex}) always recommend focusing reform attention on low-skilled agents, whereas (R^{\tilde EW}) may recommend that higher-skilled agents benefit from reform. This is one of the chapter’s sharpest differences across ethical criteria. 

The fifth main finding concerns labor-unobservable taxation. Under Assumption 11.1, every second-best optimum for (R^{sminlex}) or (R^{\tilde EW}) can be implemented by an earnings tax function that is constant on ([0,s_{min}]). This is Theorem 11.3, and it is the chapter’s most direct zero-marginal-tax result in the unobservable-labor case. 

The sixth main finding is that (R^{sminlex}) and (R^{slex}) admit tractable partial characterizations under unobservable labor. For (R^{sminlex}), optimal taxes can be studied by minimizing (\tau(s_{min})) under the constraints that (\tau(y)=\tau(s_{min})) for all (y\le s_{min}) and (\tau(y)\ge \tau(s_{min})) for all (y>s_{min}). For (R^{slex}), optimal taxes can be studied by maximizing net income of the hardworking poor subject to an average-tax-rate constraint on low incomes, a global lower-bound tax constraint, and (\tau(0)\le 0). These are Theorems 11.4 and 11.5. 

The seventh main finding is that for (R^{\tilde EW}), an (\tilde \ell)-type tax may be optimal if it is second-best efficient. This (\tilde \ell)-type tax has a constant subsidy on ([0,s_{min}]) and then piecewise-linear segments that approximate a flat tax with slope (\tilde \ell) when skills are densely distributed. This is Theorem 11.6. 

The eighth main finding is more subtle: even though (R^{\tilde EW}) has an egalitarian flavor, it may call for low redistribution when (\tilde \ell) is small. At the limit, for (\tilde \ell=0), laissez-faire is optimal. This is one of the chapter’s strongest reminders that “leximin-like” does not automatically mean “high redistribution.” 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that skills remain the only opportunity heterogeneity. The chapter does not model occupation sets, job availability, search, rationing, or multidimensional feasible opportunities. For your project, this means it is a strong tax-design chapter but still built on a thin opportunity model. 

A second limitation is analytical tractability. The chapter itself emphasizes that once agents differ both in skills and in preferences, it becomes almost impossible to derive exact optimal-tax formulas analytically in the style of the classical literature. Many results are therefore partial characterizations or “if second-best efficient, then optimal” results. 

A third limitation is that several results require Assumptions 11.1 or 11.2, which restrict how informative income is about type. These are plausible anti-separation assumptions, but they are still nontrivial restrictions on the distribution of types. 

A fourth limitation is the maintained assumption that the policymaker knows the distribution of types, though not identities. The chapter is therefore informationally constrained, but not informationally minimal. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This chapter is extremely useful for framing the transition from fairness axioms to actual policy design. It shows that once an ethical position is fixed, the next question is implementation under information constraints, and that this changes the policy implications substantially.

## possible use for model design

It is moderately useful for model design on the normative side. It does not help with explicit job-set modeling, but it gives a template for how a richer individual well-being theory could later be translated into tax or policy constraints under asymmetric information.

## possible use for identification

Directly, it is not an empirical identification chapter. Indirectly, it is highly relevant because it clarifies which kinds of private information matter for implementation: hidden skills, hidden preferences, and hidden labor effort.

## possible use for welfare measurement

This is one of its strongest uses. It shows how axiomatic welfare criteria can be rendered operational under second-best constraints without collapsing back into ad hoc utilitarianism.

## possible use for decomposition

It is useful for normative decomposition. The whole logic of the chapter preserves the compensation-versus-responsibility distinction and then asks how each side of that ethical compromise changes tax design under asymmetric information.

## possible use for comparative application

Limited directly, because the model is abstract. But the policy implications, especially about low-income marginal tax rates, are highly portable as normative benchmarks for applied tax analysis.

For your framework, this makes the chapter a strong normative-public-finance benchmark rather than a final model of labor-market justice.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This chapter is highly relevant to the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction in a narrow but very important sense. Skills are the ethically compensable opportunity variable, and preferences are the object of responsibility. The whole chapter studies how that distinction survives the move from first-best fairness to second-best taxation. 

But the opportunity notion remains wage-skill-based rather than feasible-set-based. So this is not yet a jobs-and-wellbeing treatment of opportunities versus preferences. It is a rigorous tax-design application of the scalar version of that distinction. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The two central incentive-compatibility conditions are:
[
s_i \ge w_j \Rightarrow (\ell_i,c_i) R_i (\ell_j,c_j)
]
when labor time and earnings are observed, and
[
s_i \ge s_j \ell_j \Rightarrow (\ell_i,c_i) R_i \left(\frac{s_j}{s_i}\ell_j,c_j\right)
]
in the unobservable-labor case, written in the chapter as conditions (11.1) and (11.2). These are the chapter’s core implementation constraints. 

Theorem 11.1 is the load-bearing policy result: under observable labor, second-best optima for (R^{sminlex}) and (R^{\tilde EW}) can be implemented with zero marginal taxation for the low-skilled, while (R^{slex}) implies nonpositive average marginal taxation for the low-skilled. 

Theorem 11.3 is the corresponding low-income result under unobservable labor: under Assumption 11.1, every second-best optimum for (R^{sminlex}) or (R^{\tilde EW}) can be implemented by a tax function constant on ([0,s_{min}]). 

Table 11.1 is especially useful because it condenses the reform-evaluation criteria for all three social orderings and both informational structures.
