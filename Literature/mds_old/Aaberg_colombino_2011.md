# Full citation

Aaberge, Rolf, and Ugo Colombino. 2011. *Empirical Optimal Income Taxation: A Microeconometric Application to Norway*. ChilD Working Paper No. 16/2011. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper uses a structurally estimated labor supply model with heterogeneous preferences and heterogeneous job opportunities to compute, by simulation, optimal piecewise-linear income tax rules for Norway under alternative social welfare criteria. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it departs from the usual empirical optimal-tax practice of plugging external elasticity values into analytical formulas and instead solves the optimal-tax problem computationally inside a structurally estimated micro model. That move is important both methodologically and substantively: it allows the tax-design exercise to be consistent with richer assumptions about preferences, couples, nonconvex constraints, and opportunity sets. 

For your potential JMP, the paper is especially relevant because it is not only a labor-supply/optimal-tax paper but also an opportunities paper. It models job opportunities explicitly and separately from preferences, and then carries that structure into policy design. Among the papers you are collecting, this is one of the strongest examples of a bridge between structural labor supply, constrained choice, and normative analysis. 

# Core research question

What income tax rule maximizes social welfare, for a given revenue requirement, when labor supply choices are generated by a structurally estimated model with heterogeneous preferences and heterogeneous job opportunities across singles and couples? 

# Economic setting and context

The application is Norway in the mid-1990s. The benchmark institutional environment is the 1994 Norwegian tax system, and the authors search for alternative piecewise-linear tax schedules that keep total net tax revenue constant. The paper is motivated by dissatisfaction with standard optimal-tax applications that rely on restrictive theory and empirically imported parameters that may not be consistent with the theoretical assumptions. 

The empirical population includes singles and couples. This is important because the paper explicitly avoids the standard simplification of studying only one-earner singles. It models both single-person households and married/cohabiting couples making joint labor supply decisions, while excluding self-employed individuals and disability recipients. The underlying sample comprises 1,842 couples, 309 single females, and 312 single males. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a static structural labor supply model of the random utility / discrete-choice type, embedded in a computational optimal-tax framework. On the positive side, households choose among job opportunities characterized by hours, wages, sector, and other observed and unobserved job attributes. On the normative side, the social planner chooses the parameters of a piecewise-linear tax rule to maximize a social welfare function subject to behavioral response and constant-revenue constraints. The framework is therefore both positive and normative. 

The key choice object is not merely hours but jobs. For singles, the household chooses from opportunities indexed by ((h,w,s,j)), where (h) is hours, (w) the wage, (s) sector, and (j) other job/household characteristics. For couples, the choice set is joint over spouses’ hours, wages, and sectors. This is a major difference from simpler labor supply models that treat only hours as chosen and wages as fixed. 

The feasible set is modeled explicitly as an opportunity set (B) that includes market and non-market opportunities. The density of opportunities varies across individuals and across job types. The paper therefore does not assume a universal homogeneous hours menu. It is static, not dynamic. It is also explicitly designed to handle nonlinear and nonconvex tax-transfer constraints. 

# Key objects

The central positive-model objects are the household utility functions, the job opportunity densities, labor supply elasticities implied by the estimated model, and the exact tax rule mapping gross into disposable income. The model contains 78 parameters capturing heterogeneity in preferences and opportunities across singles and couples. 

The core opportunity object is the density of available jobs over hours, wages, and sector. For singles, opportunities are modeled through (p(h,w,s)); for couples, through a joint density over both spouses’ job opportunities. The model also distinguishes market from non-market opportunities and allows sector-specific wage densities and peaks at part-time and full-time hours. 

The main normative objects are the common individual welfare function (V), equivalent-income-like individual welfare comparisons based on after-tax income and leisure, and rank-dependent social welfare functions (W_1, W_2, W_3,) and (W_\infty). The tax instrument is a 9-parameter piecewise-linear rule with exemption level, four marginal tax rates, three kink points, and a lump-sum term that may be positive or negative. 

# Data

The paper uses the 1995 Norwegian Survey of Level of Living, which includes detailed income information from tax records. The working sample restricts age to 20–62 and excludes self-employed individuals and permanent disability beneficiaries. The data support estimation for singles and couples separately and jointly. 

The operational environment is the 1994 tax system. The paper uses 1994 tax rules for estimation and main simulation, and then performs an out-of-sample validation exercise by using the model estimated on 1994 behavior to predict the distribution of disposable income under the 2001 tax regime with 2002 survey data. 

The key variables are gross earnings, disposable income, taxes, hours of work, sector of employment, age, education, children, and non-labor income. For welfare evaluation, the authors also construct an individual income measure for couples by dividing household disposable income by (\sqrt{2}). 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural and simulation-based rather than quasi-experimental. Preferences are identified from observed labor supply choices conditional on detailed tax rules, observed demographics, wage equations, and modeled opportunity sets. The exact representation of the tax system and the richness of the opportunity set are central parts of the identifying structure. 

A particularly important feature is that the model separates preferences from constraints by modeling the density of job opportunities explicitly. Jobs differ by hours, wage, and sector, and not all opportunities are equally available. This is the paper’s main identification advantage relative to simpler models that force all heterogeneity into the preference side. 

The optimal-tax stage is identified computationally, not analytically. The planner’s problem is solved by repeatedly simulating household choices under candidate tax rules and selecting the rule that maximizes the chosen social welfare function subject to constant revenue. This is only as credible as the structural labor supply model underneath it, which the authors partially validate through an out-of-sample prediction exercise for 2001. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The labor supply model is estimated by maximum likelihood using a McFadden-type sampled-alternatives procedure. For each household, the continuous opportunity set is approximated by 200 alternatives consisting of the observed choice plus 199 sampled market and non-market opportunities. This is used to estimate the 78 parameters governing preferences, job opportunities, and wage densities. 

After estimation, the paper computes labor supply elasticities by stochastic simulation. Wage rates are increased and individual responses aggregated to obtain participation and hours elasticities by household type and income decile. The model then undergoes an out-of-sample validation exercise: estimated on the 1994 environment, it predicts 2001 distributions reasonably well. 

The optimal-tax exercise is then implemented by iterative global search over a 9-parameter class of piecewise-linear tax rules. For each candidate rule, the model simulates labor supply choices, computes individual welfare levels using a common welfare function, aggregates them through alternative social welfare criteria, and checks the constant-revenue constraint. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled as heterogeneous over consumption and leisure, with different specifications for singles and couples and with demographic shifters such as age, children, and sector. Couples’ preferences include an interaction term between spouses’ leisure, so the model allows non-separable joint household labor supply. 

The paper is clear that actual behavior is generated by households’ own utility functions (U), not by the planner’s welfare criterion. This is important conceptually: the planner does not impose a common behavioral preference ordering on households. Instead, heterogeneous actual preferences generate choices, and only afterward are outcomes evaluated through a common welfare metric. 

Normatively, this is therefore not a preference-respecting welfare evaluation in the Bargain et al. (2013) sense. The social planner uses a common utility function (V) to achieve interpersonal comparability, precisely because actual heterogeneous utilities are not taken to be interpersonally comparable. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is one of the paper’s most important contributions.

The paper models opportunities explicitly. The feasible set is an opportunity set (B) of jobs, including non-market opportunities, and the density of jobs varies with hours, wages, and sector. This means that labor supply is not represented as free choice over a common hours grid. Instead, individuals differ in the relative number and type of jobs available to them. 

The model also allows for quantity constraints in the labor market. The authors state directly that the framework can accommodate the presence of constraints in hours and job availability, including the extreme case of involuntary unemployment. Moreover, for couples the opportunity set is joint across spouses, so the model goes beyond one-person hours rationing. 

This makes the paper highly relevant to your opportunities-versus-preferences agenda. It is much closer to a latent-jobs or RURO logic than the standard discrete-hours labor supply model, although it still simplifies the opportunity side to a reduced-form density over hours, wages, and sector rather than a richer multidimensional job-characteristics space. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative. Its welfare object is not compensating variation or equivalent income in the Fleurbaey-Maniquet sense, but a common individual welfare function (V(y,h)) used to evaluate the outcomes generated by heterogeneous actual preferences. The social welfare functions (W_1, W_2, W_3,) and (W_\infty) are then rank-dependent aggregators over these comparable individual welfare levels. 

The common welfare function is introduced because the authors reject the idea that heterogeneous actual utility functions can be aggregated directly as if they were interpersonally comparable. This is a central conceptual move in the paper. The planner evaluates after-tax equivalent income and leisure using common parameters rather than actual household-specific parameters. 

The four social welfare criteria differ in inequality aversion. (W_1) corresponds to the strongest downside sensitivity among the reported criteria, (W_2) is associated with the Gini-related case, (W_3) is more moderate, and (W_\infty) is utilitarian. More egalitarian criteria generate more progressive optimal tax rules. 

# Main findings

First, all optimal tax rules found in the exercise have monotonically increasing marginal tax rates. This is a major substantive result because it contrasts with many earlier numerical optimal-tax exercises that tended to produce declining marginal rates at low incomes combined with a negative-income-tax logic. 

Second, compared with the 1994 Norwegian tax system, all optimal rules imply a lower average tax rate while keeping total net tax revenue constant. The mechanism is stronger labor supply under the optimized schedules, especially among lower-income households. 

Third, all optimal rules imply lower marginal tax rates on low and/or average income levels and higher marginal tax rates on sufficiently high income levels than the current rule. The top optimal marginal tax rate reaches the imposed ceiling of 75 percent for very high income levels, around 700,000 NOK (1994), covering only a small fraction of taxpayers. 

Fourth, more egalitarian social welfare criteria imply more progressive tax schedules. Bonferroni is more progressive than Gini, which is more progressive than (W_3), while the utilitarian rule is closest to the current system. 

Fifth, the strongest labor supply responses come from low-income households and especially from married women. The paper’s elasticity tables show sharply larger responses in the bottom income deciles than at the top. 

Sixth, the optimal rules generate a strong majority of winners relative to the 1994 system. The paper reports that all optimal rules would “win the referendum” against the current rule, though gains and losses are heterogeneous across household types and income deciles. 

# Main limitations

The paper is application-specific. The authors are explicit that the computational results are Norway-specific and depend on the estimated structural model and the chosen class of tax rules. This is the price paid for realism relative to highly stylized analytical formulas. 

A second limitation is the normative workaround for interpersonal comparability. The common welfare function (V) is a coherent and established solution, but it is still an imposed normative device rather than an endogenous or preference-respecting welfare concept. For a project interested in heterogeneous preferences as normatively relevant, this is a nontrivial limitation. 

A third limitation is that the tax-rule search is restricted to a 9-parameter piecewise-linear family with a capped top marginal rate. The optimal rule is therefore optimal only within that class, not over all conceivable tax schedules. 

A fourth limitation is that the opportunity set, while explicitly modeled, remains reduced-form. Jobs differ by hours, wages, and sector, but not by a richer vector of amenities or occupations. This is closer to your agenda than standard hours-choice models, but it is not yet a full latent-job or RURO representation of labor-market opportunities. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing a project that wants to move beyond the standard practice of importing elasticities into an optimal-tax formula. It gives you a clear example of a structurally integrated approach in which behavior, opportunities, and tax design are solved within one empirical architecture. 

## possible use for model design

It is highly relevant for model design because it treats jobs, not only hours, as the objects of choice, and because it allows the opportunity set to differ across individuals. If your JMP moves toward a RURO or latent-job framework, this paper is a natural precursor and benchmark. 

## possible use for identification

The paper is useful for the identification side because it shows how a structural model can separate preferences from opportunities and then validate itself through out-of-sample prediction. That is a stronger template than simply fitting current hours choices. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

It is relevant for welfare measurement mainly as a contrast case. It shows one coherent way to do normative evaluation when actual preferences are heterogeneous but not treated as interpersonally comparable. If your project instead wants to preserve heterogeneous preferences in welfare comparisons, this paper gives you a disciplined alternative to argue against or build upon. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

The paper is not cross-country, but it is very useful for cross-country design questions. It implies that if opportunities and elasticities vary by institutional environment, then cross-country welfare or tax comparisons should not rely on a common hours-choice model with homogeneous feasibility. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How would optimal tax design change if the opportunity density over jobs differed systematically across countries rather than being estimated for one country only? 

Can one construct a preference-sensitive welfare criterion that retains heterogeneous actual preferences, while still maintaining interpersonal comparability, inside an opportunity-sensitive structural labor supply model? 

How much of the progressivity of empirically optimal tax schedules is driven by low-income households’ high opportunity-constrained labor supply elasticities rather than by pure redistributive taste? 

What happens to optimal tax design when the opportunity set is expanded from hours-wage-sector jobs to richer latent jobs with occupation and job-quality characteristics? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest simplification is not on the preference side but on the normative and opportunity sides together. Normatively, the paper solves interpersonal comparability by imposing a common welfare function rather than preserving heterogeneous actual preferences. On the opportunity side, it models jobs as hours-wage-sector bundles but not as richer multidimensional opportunities. A future paper could challenge both elements simultaneously by combining a richer latent-job opportunity set with a welfare criterion that is more explicitly attentive to heterogeneous preferences and opportunities. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

This paper is closely related to Bargain et al. (2013) in its broad ambition to connect structural labor supply estimation with normative analysis, but the normative architecture is very different. Bargain et al. (2013) studies individual welfare rankings across countries and takes heterogeneous preferences seriously as objects that affect welfare comparison. Aaberge and Colombino instead solve a single-country optimal-tax problem and explicitly avoid interpersonal comparison of actual heterogeneous utilities by introducing a common welfare function. The paper is also much stronger on the opportunity side than Bargain et al. (2013), because it models heterogeneous job opportunities explicitly rather than relying mainly on a common hours menu plus tax-benefit constraints. 

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper substantially helps separate preferences from opportunities. Households choose according to heterogeneous utility functions over jobs, but the feasible set is itself modeled through an explicit opportunity density over hours, wages, and sector. That is exactly the kind of separation often missing in standard labor supply papers. The main remaining limitation is that opportunities are modeled in a reduced-form way, not as a richer latent-job structure with full job characteristics. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The core positive model is:
[
\max_{(w,h,s,j)\in B} U(c,h,s,j)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad c=f(wh,I),
]
with utility factorized as
[
U(f(wh,I),h,s,j)=v(f(wh,I),h,s)\varepsilon(j).
]
The choice density then depends on both utility and the density of available opportunities (p(h,w,s)). This is the paper’s most important formal link between preferences and opportunities. 

The planner’s problem is to choose the tax-rule parameters (\vartheta) to maximize social welfare subject to household optimization and constant total net tax revenue. The implemented tax rule is piecewise linear with exemption level (E), four marginal tax rates, three kink points, and lump-sum term (T). 

A short quotation that captures the paper’s core methodological stance is that the authors “do not start from theoretical results dictating conditions for optimal tax rules” but instead “use a microeconometric model of labour supply in order to identify by simulation the tax rule that maximizes a social welfare function.”

# My quick takeaway

If I only remember one thing from this paper for my JMP, it should be this: once you model labor supply as choice over heterogeneous job opportunities rather than free choice over hours, the empirical optimal-tax problem looks different, and the resulting tax design can become more progressive at the top while still lowering average tax rates through stronger responses among low- and middle-income households.
