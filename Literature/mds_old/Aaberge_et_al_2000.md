# Full citation

Aaberge, Rolf, Ugo Colombino, and Steinar Strøm. 2000. “Labor Supply Responses and Welfare Effects from Replacing Current Tax Rules by a Flat Tax: Empirical Evidence from Italy, Norway and Sweden.” *Journal of Population Economics* 13(4): 595–621. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper uses country-specific structural labour-supply models for married couples in Italy, Norway, and Sweden to simulate what would happen to labour supply, income distribution, and household welfare if the existing 1992 progressive tax systems were replaced by revenue-neutral flat taxes. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is an early cross-country attempt to connect structural labour supply estimation, tax microsimulation, and welfare analysis in a common framework. It does not merely compare elasticities; it compares full reform counterfactuals across institutional environments.

For your potential JMP, it is especially relevant because it sits close to your agenda on three dimensions: it uses structural labour-supply models rather than reduced form evidence, it treats opportunities/quantity constraints explicitly through job-opportunity sets, and it evaluates reform outcomes through a money-metric welfare concept rather than income alone. At the same time, it remains distinct from your preferred direction because the welfare object is Hicksian EV, not a preference-sensitive or opportunity-sensitive well-being metric in the Fleurbaey-Maniquet sense. 

# Core research question

What are the labour-supply, distributive, and welfare consequences of replacing the then-current progressive labour-income tax systems in Italy, Norway, and Sweden with revenue-neutral flat taxes, and how do these consequences differ across countries? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is motivated by the late-1980s and early-1990s movement in many OECD countries toward less progressive labour-income taxation. The authors frame the issue in terms of efficiency costs of progressive taxation, possible tax competition in Europe, and the political economy of moving toward proportional or flatter tax systems. 

The comparison is explicitly cross-country. Italy is treated as a lower-progression case than the Scandinavian countries, while Norway and Sweden provide more progressive welfare-state benchmarks. The simulations use the 1992 tax systems in each country as the baseline and then replace them with revenue-neutral flat taxes, chosen to reproduce the same aggregate tax revenue. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a static structural labour-supply model of the random utility / random opportunities type, applied to married couples. Labour supply is analyzed as a discrete choice among “job packages” characterized by wage rates, hours of work, and non-pecuniary attributes. The framework also allows for quantity constraints in the labour market, in the sense that not all jobs are equally available to every household. 

The key choice object is therefore not simply hours, but a feasible job opportunity. The household budget constraint maps labour income and non-labour income into disposable income under the country-specific tax rule. The opportunity sets are allowed to vary across households, and the analyst models them through opportunity densities rather than observing them directly. 

The framework is both positive and normative. It is positive in the modelling of labour-supply behaviour under taxes and institutional constraints. It is normative insofar as reform outcomes are evaluated through equivalent variation. But it is not a social-choice or fair-allocation framework; welfare is measured within the household’s own utility representation. 

# Key objects

The main behavioural objects are participation, hours supplied, gross income, disposable income, and own- and cross-wage labour-supply elasticities for husbands and wives. The paper reports these both in the aggregate and by household income position. 

The central structural objects are the utility function over consumption and leisure, random taste shifters, and opportunity densities over wage-hours job packages. These opportunity densities are meant to capture restrictions on available jobs and hours and thus to distinguish preferences from the feasible set. 

The main welfare object is equivalent variation (EV), defined at the household level as the money amount that, under the initial tax rule, makes the household indifferent between the initial and alternative tax systems. Because the model contains unobserved heterogeneity, EV itself is treated as a random variable and its distribution across households is studied. 

# Data

The paper uses three country-specific married-couple datasets underlying previously estimated structural labour-supply models: Italy 1987, Norway 1986, and Sweden 1981. All samples are restricted to married couples, and households with self-employment income exceeding 20 percent of total gross income are excluded. Spouses’ ages are restricted to the working-age range used in the underlying models. 

The sample sizes vary by country, from about 1,640 households in Sweden to about 2,950 in Italy. The data are not harmonized in the later EUROMOD-style sense; rather, the authors compare reform simulations generated from separate country-specific structural models. 

The core variables are spouses’ wages, hours, non-labour income, demographic characteristics, and the institutional details of country-specific tax systems. The simulations then replace the 1992 country tax systems with flat taxes calibrated to keep national tax revenue unchanged. 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural and assumption-driven. The model separates preferences from opportunities by representing observed choices as outcomes of utility maximization over unobserved opportunity sets summarized by opportunity densities. Thus, identification relies on the joint structure of the utility function, the tax rule, and the assumed distributions of feasible jobs. 

A crucial identifying restriction is that offered hours are not freely chosen along a continuous budget frontier; they come from a distribution of feasible jobs, with peaks at full-time and, for women in some countries, part-time work. Wage opportunities are also modeled parametrically. This is central to the paper’s behavioural interpretation, because labour-supply responses are identified conditional on institutional constraints and job availability rather than under a universal hours-choice set. 

The reform analysis is partial equilibrium. Opportunity densities and offered wage/hour distributions are kept fixed when tax rules change. That is not a mere computational detail; it is a strong maintained assumption that rules out feedback from tax reform to labour demand or job creation. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper does not newly estimate one pooled cross-country model. Instead, it takes as given the previously estimated country-specific structural labour-supply models and uses them as behavioural engines for simulation. Those models are microeconometric married-couple labour-supply models with random opportunities and exact tax-rule representation. 

The empirical strategy is then stochastic simulation. For each household, the authors draw wage offers, hours opportunities, and random taste shocks; compute the utility-maximizing job package under the current tax system and under a flat-tax alternative; and aggregate the resulting labour-supply, income, tax-revenue, inequality, and EV outcomes. 

The flat-tax rates are chosen so that simulated tax revenue equals the revenue under the 1992 systems. The resulting rates are about 23 percent in Italy, 25 percent in Norway, and 29 percent in Sweden. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are heterogeneous and enter both the behavioural and welfare analysis. The utility functions vary across households through observed and unobserved heterogeneity, and the simulations explicitly preserve this heterogeneity when generating responses and EV distributions. 

However, this is not a paper about heterogeneous preferences as a normative object in the Bargain et al. (2013) sense. Preferences matter because they determine labour supply and therefore EV, but the welfare evaluation remains within a money-metric/Hicksian framework rather than a framework that asks how interpersonal welfare comparisons should be made when preferences differ. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is one of the paper’s strongest dimensions.

The paper models opportunities explicitly. Labour supply is choice over feasible job packages, and the opportunity sets differ across households. Jobs differ in wages, hours, and non-pecuniary attributes, and not every household faces the same opportunity distribution. This is much closer to a RURO logic than to a standard Hausman-type labour-supply model. 

The paper is explicit that quantity constraints matter: different job types are not equally available to all agents, and those constraints are part of the structural explanation of observed behaviour. The opportunity densities are therefore not nuisance terms; they are core structural objects. 

The main limitation is that these opportunity sets are kept fixed under reform and remain stylized. The model does not contain an explicit labour-demand side, matching process, or richer occupation/task-level job characteristics. So it helps separate preferences from opportunities, but only within a partial-equilibrium reduced-form opportunity framework. 

# Welfare / normative object

The welfare object is equivalent variation (EV). It is used as a money-metric measure of the household’s welfare change from replacing the current tax system by a flat tax. The paper emphasizes both the mean EV and the distribution of EV across households, rather than only reporting an aggregate average. 

The normative stance is therefore positive with welfare applications. It is not a social welfare function paper, and it is not responsibility-sensitive or opportunity-sensitive in its welfare criterion. It remains within the Hicksian money-metric tradition. The authors also note that identifying winners and losers does not require interpersonal cardinal comparability in the same way as aggregating EV into one social welfare measure would. 

# Main findings

First, the revenue-neutral flat tax rates differ across countries: roughly 23 percent in Italy, 25 percent in Norway, and 29 percent in Sweden. This already shows that institutional baseline differences matter even before behaviour is discussed. 

Second, female labour supply is more responsive than male labour supply in all three countries, and labour-supply elasticities decline sharply with pre-reform disposable income. The poor are much more responsive than the rich. 

Third, the labour-supply response to a flat tax is strong in Norway, much weaker but still positive in Italy, and weak in Sweden. The authors link the weak Swedish and Italian responses partly to institutional rigidity in offered hours and, in Sweden, to already high female labour-force attachment. 

Fourth, the mean welfare effect of moving to a flat tax is positive in all three countries, implying efficiency costs of the existing 1992 tax systems relative to a flat-tax benchmark. But the magnitude differs sharply: the efficiency cost is very high in Norway and much lower, though still positive, in Italy and Sweden. 

Fifth, rich households tend to gain more in welfare terms than poor households. The poor often increase labour supply substantially under the flat tax, but much of the income gain is offset by the utility cost of reduced leisure. The rich, by contrast, benefit more directly from lower tax burdens with smaller effort responses. 

Sixth, the distributional effects on income are country-specific. Norway shows a substantial reduction in disposable-income inequality under the flat tax because strong labour-supply responses among poor households compress the income distribution. Italy shows only minor changes in income inequality, while Sweden shows increased disposable-income inequality under the flat tax. 

Seventh, the distribution of winners and losers differs strongly across countries. A large majority wins in Norway, whereas a majority is predicted to lose in Italy and Sweden. 

# Main limitations

The most important limitation is the partial-equilibrium assumption. The opportunity densities of offered wages and hours are held fixed when tax systems change. This excludes feedback from tax reform to labour demand, job creation, wage-setting, and equilibrium opportunity sets. 

A second limitation is the narrow population focus on married couples. This is substantively relevant for labour-supply elasticity, but it limits generalization to singles, single parents, or broader population welfare comparisons. 

A third limitation is the welfare concept. EV is operationally useful, but it does not solve the deeper normative problem of interpersonal welfare comparison under heterogeneous preferences. For your agenda, this is a serious limitation because the paper is normatively much thinner than later welfare-comparison work. 

A fourth limitation is comparability across countries. The paper compares simulated reforms across separate country models estimated on different datasets and different years, not on a harmonized common data platform. That weakens the interpretation of cross-country differences as purely institutional. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is useful for framing a JMP that links labour-supply modelling to welfare evaluation and cross-country comparison. It shows that one can compare not just elasticities but full reform counterfactuals across countries. 

## possible use for model design

It is particularly useful for model design because it treats labour supply as choice among constrained job packages rather than unconstrained hours. If your project moves toward latent jobs or opportunity sets, this is one of the clearest empirical precedents. 

## possible use for identification

The paper is useful as an example of structurally identifying behavioural responses through the interaction of preferences, taxes, and opportunity densities. It also clarifies the price of that strategy: strong parametric and partial-equilibrium assumptions. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

This paper is useful as a benchmark, but also as a foil. It shows how far one can go with EV in a structural labour-supply setting, while also making clear why a richer welfare concept may be needed if one wants to distinguish preferences from opportunities in well-being comparisons rather than only in behaviour. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

It is highly relevant here. The core lesson is that the same “flat tax” experiment can generate very different behavioural, distributive, and welfare results across countries because the underlying tax systems, labour-supply elasticities, and opportunity structures differ. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How much of the cross-country difference in flat-tax welfare effects is due to preference heterogeneity, and how much is due to differences in opportunity sets and hours rigidities? 

Would the Norwegian result that flat taxation can reduce disposable-income inequality survive in a model where opportunity sets and wages respond endogenously to labour-supply changes? 

Can one replace EV in this framework with a welfare measure that remains sensitive to both heterogeneous preferences and heterogeneous opportunities? 

How much of the welfare advantage for richer households under flatter taxes is driven by lower effort responses rather than by tax arithmetic alone? 

Could a richer latent-job model with occupation and job-quality characteristics overturn the weak Italian and Swedish flat-tax labour-supply responses? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest omission is that the opportunity structure is treated as exogenous under reform. This is analytically convenient and standard in partial-equilibrium microsimulation, but it is exactly where a future paper could challenge the conclusions. If flatter taxation changes the number, type, and distribution of available jobs, then both the behavioural responses and the welfare incidence could look different from what this paper finds. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

The relation is substantial but not identical. Both papers connect structural labour-supply modelling to welfare analysis and both are cross-country in spirit. But this 2000 paper uses EV to evaluate tax reform and models opportunity sets more explicitly, whereas Bargain et al. (2013) focuses on welfare rankings in the consumption-leisure space and gives preference heterogeneity a much more explicit normative role. Relative to Bargain et al. (2013), Aaberge et al. (2000) is stronger on constrained job opportunities and weaker on the theory of welfare comparison under heterogeneous preferences.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper clearly helps separate preferences from opportunities on the behavioural side. The model does not interpret all observed labour-supply variation as preference variation; it explicitly introduces opportunity densities and quantity constraints. But on the welfare side it does not fully resolve the opportunities-versus-preferences problem, because EV is still computed within each household’s utility representation and the opportunity set is held fixed under reform. So it is an important step toward your agenda, but not the endpoint. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The key behavioural idea is that labour supply is modeled as choice among feasible “job packages” characterized by hours, wages, and non-pecuniary attributes, with opportunity densities summarizing the distribution of available jobs. This is the central conceptual formula of the paper even when the exact notation differs by country appendix. 

The welfare metric is equivalent variation, defined implicitly so that the household is indifferent between the initial tax system and the alternative tax system after adding/subtracting a money amount under the initial regime. This is the core normative device used to classify winners and losers. 

A compact statement of the main result is that the mean welfare effect of moving to a flat tax is positive in all three countries, but “rich” households tend to benefit more in welfare terms than “poor” households.
