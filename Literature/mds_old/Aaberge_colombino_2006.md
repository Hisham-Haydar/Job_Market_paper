# Full citation

Aaberge, Rolf, and Ugo Colombino. 2006. *Designing Optimal Taxes with a Microeconometric Model of Household Labour Supply*. Discussion Papers No. 475. Statistics Norway, Research Department. September 2006. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper estimates a structurally rich household labour-supply model with heterogeneous preferences and heterogeneous job opportunities, and then uses it to compute optimal Norwegian income-tax schedules by direct welfare maximization under a fixed-revenue constraint rather than by plugging empirical elasticities into Mirrlees/Saez-style formulas. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is an explicit alternative to the usual empirical optimal-tax workflow. Instead of taking analytical optimal-tax formulas and calibrating them with externally estimated behavioural parameters, it embeds tax design directly inside the same microeconometric model that generates labour-supply behaviour. The authors present this as a way to avoid inconsistency between theory and empirical inputs. 

For your potential JMP, the paper is especially important because it combines three things that are often kept separate: structural labour supply, explicit opportunity modeling, and normative evaluation. It is also unusually relevant for your preferences-versus-opportunities agenda, since the model distinguishes heterogeneous tastes from heterogeneous opportunity sets and then carries that distinction into tax-design simulation. 

# Core research question

What income-tax rule maximizes social welfare, subject to fixed total net tax revenue, when labour supply is generated by a microeconometric model with heterogeneous preferences, household joint decisions, and heterogeneous job opportunities? 

# Economic setting and context

The setting is Norway, with the current tax rule taken as the 1994 rule. The paper treats the exercise as empirical optimal taxation under a fixed-revenue requirement, with tax design restricted to piecewise-linear personal income tax rules. Transfers, income support, and social assistance policies are held fixed at their current 1994 levels so that the exercise focuses on the profile of marginal tax rates. 

The paper positions itself against the standard empirical optimal-tax literature descended from Mirrlees. Its criticism is twofold: first, operational optimal-tax formulas usually require strong simplifying assumptions; second, the empirical measures used to implement them are often estimated under assumptions different from those of the theory. The proposed alternative is direct computational optimization inside a structural labour-supply model. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The framework is a static structural labour-supply model combined with a computational optimal-tax problem. Behaviourally, it is a random-utility model that can be viewed as an extension of the multinomial logit model. Normatively, it maximizes social welfare over a class of piecewise-linear tax schedules. The paper is therefore explicitly both positive and normative. 

The key choice object is a job, not just hours. For singles, the agent solves
[
\max_{(whj)\in B} U(c,h,j)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad c=f(wh,I),
]
where (h) is hours, (w) is the wage, (j) denotes other job/household characteristics, (I) is exogenous non-labour income, and (B) is the set of available market and non-market opportunities. For couples, the model treats spouses’ decisions jointly. 

The feasible set is modeled explicitly through an opportunity density (p(h,w,j)) rather than a universal common hours grid. This is central to the paper. Jobs differ across individuals not only because of wages but because the number and type of opportunities available can differ across decision units. The model therefore allows for quantity constraints and limits in the choice of hours. 

# Key objects

The main behavioural objects are labour-force participation, annual hours, own-wage and cross-wage labour-supply elasticities, and the distribution of disposable income across singles and couples. The paper repeatedly emphasizes heterogeneity of responses by household type, gender, and position in the income distribution. 

The central structural objects are the systematic utility component (v), the stochastic taste shifter (\varepsilon), and the opportunity density (p(h,w,j)). For singles and couples, the model separately parameterizes preferences and opportunity distributions, including wages, hours, and sector of employment. The full model contains 78 parameters. 

The main normative objects are the common individual welfare function (V), the equality-of-outcome social welfare functions (W_k), and the equality-of-opportunity social welfare functions (\widetilde W_k). The tax design object is a 6-parameter piecewise-linear income-tax rule with exemption (E), three marginal tax rates ((\tau_1,\tau_2,\tau_3)), and two bracket thresholds ((Z_1,Z_2)). 

# Data

The paper states that the model is based on Norwegian household data for 1994, and the appendix specifies estimation using the 1995 Survey of Level of Living. The appendix also states that the estimation sample is restricted to ages 18–54, while the optimal-tax simulations are described for single females, single males, and couples aged 20–62. The exact alignment of survey year, tax year, and age window is therefore somewhat compressed in the exposition. 

The model covers singles and married/cohabiting couples. The appendix reports separate specifications for single females, single males, and couples, with detailed opportunity densities over hours, wages, and sector. The data include information sufficient to simulate incomes and labour supply under the 1994 Norwegian tax rule. Exact sample sizes for each subgroup are [unclear from paper] in the visible excerpts. 

The main variables are gross income, disposable income, hours, wages, age, children, sector, and exogenous income. For the welfare function, couples’ disposable income is equivalized by dividing by (\sqrt{2}). 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural and assumption-driven rather than quasi-experimental. The observed choices are interpreted as outcomes of utility maximization over heterogeneous opportunity sets under the tax rule. The probability of a choice depends jointly on systematic utility and the density of available opportunities, which is precisely how the model separates preferences from constraints. 

The paper does not identify optimal tax parameters from reduced-form causal variation. Instead, it first estimates the labour-supply model and then searches over tax rules by simulation until the chosen social welfare function is maximized subject to a fixed-revenue constraint. The credibility of the optimal-tax results therefore rests on the structural validity of the estimated behavioural model. 

A crucial identification feature for your agenda is that the model does not force all heterogeneity into preferences. Opportunity densities over jobs are explicit structural objects. This matters because behavioural responses are then interpreted as functions of both preferences and opportunities, not as pure “taste” responses. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper estimates separate utility functions for single females, single males, and couples, together with job, hours, and wage opportunity densities. Estimation is by maximum likelihood using the choice-density representation implied by the random-utility framework. The appendix provides the parametric specifications and estimated coefficients. 

Once estimated, the model is used to simulate labour-supply choices under alternative tax rules. Wage and income elasticities are then computed by stochastic simulation because relevant preference and opportunity components are partly unobserved by the analyst. The paper reports detailed elasticities by household type and income decile. 

Optimal-tax search is then performed iteratively over a class of 6-parameter piecewise-linear rules. The procedure applies a tax rule to gross incomes, simulates labour-supply responses, computes equivalent incomes and individual welfare levels, aggregates them through either EO or EOp social welfare functions, and repeats until welfare is maximized under constant net tax revenue. Both unconstrained and top-rate-constrained exercises are reported. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are heterogeneous and are estimated separately for single females, single males, and couples. Within each category, the utility specification depends on consumption, leisure, age, children, sector, and spouse-leisure interaction for couples. The paper stresses that the model captures both observed and unobserved heterogeneity in tastes. 

At the same time, the paper is explicit that these heterogeneous estimated utility functions are not suitable for direct interpersonal welfare comparison. Because preferences differ and because some people are singles while others are members of couples, the authors reject the idea of simply treating the behavioural utility functions as comparable welfare functions. 

The normative workaround is to estimate a separate common individual welfare function (V), defined over equivalized disposable income and leisure, and then use that function for welfare aggregation. Thus, actual heterogeneous preferences govern behaviour, but a common evaluative function governs normative comparison. This is not preference-respecting welfare analysis in the later Fleurbaey/Maniquet/Bargain sense. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is one of the paper’s core contributions.

The paper models opportunities explicitly. The agent does not freely choose any hours level on a continuous budget set. Instead, labour supply is choice over jobs characterized by wages, hours, and sector, and the availability of such jobs is represented by an opportunity density (p(h,w,j)). The model therefore allows for quantity constraints and limits on the distribution of feasible work arrangements. 

This is not a universal-choice-set model. Individuals differ in the number and type of available jobs, and even the same व्यक्ति can face different wages across jobs. The paper presents this as a major improvement over traditional optimal-tax models, which usually ignore simultaneous household decisions, participation/hours interactions, and choice constraints. 

Relative to your agenda, the paper is highly relevant because it separates preferences from opportunities on the positive side of the model. The main limitation is that opportunities are still represented in reduced form as densities over jobs rather than through a richer market-equilibrium or occupation/task-based latent-job model. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper uses two families of social welfare criteria. The first is equality of outcome (EO), based on rank-dependent social welfare functions
[
W_k=\int_0^1 p_k(t)F^{-1}(t),dt,
]
with varying inequality aversion. The second is equality of opportunity (EOp), based on Roemer’s framework and an extended family
[
\widetilde W_k=\int_0^1 p_k(t)\min_j F_j^{-1}(t),dt,
]
where types are defined by father’s education. 

Individual welfare itself is measured by a common welfare function over income and leisure, not by the heterogeneous behavioural utilities. For couples, disposable income is divided by (\sqrt{2}) before welfare evaluation. This makes the paper explicitly normative, but within a common-function rather than preference-respecting framework. 

The EOp implementation is noteworthy for your interests because it tries to distinguish inequality due to circumstances from inequality due to effort. But it does so through family-background types and a common welfare function, not through direct modeling of opportunities as the welfare object itself. 

# Main findings

First, all optimal tax rules imply an average tax rate lower than the current 1994 one. The mechanism is behavioural: the optimal rules induce sufficiently higher labour supply and gross income to keep total net tax revenue constant with a lower average tax burden. 

Second, all optimal rules imply lower marginal tax rates on low and/or average income levels and higher marginal tax rates on sufficiently high income levels than the current rule. This is the paper’s central substantive result, and the authors stress that it runs against many real-world reforms that lowered top marginal rates. 

Third, in the unconstrained optimization exercise the top optimal marginal rate is always 100 percent for very high incomes, approximately above 700,000 NOK (1994). When the top rate is constrained to be at most 60 percent, the same qualitative pattern remains: low- and middle-income marginal rates stay below current levels, high-income rates stay above them. 

Fourth, more egalitarian social welfare criteria imply more progressive tax rules. Bonferroni is more progressive than Gini, which is more progressive than the utilitarian rule. This holds for both EO and EOp families. 

Fifth, EO and EOp produce very similar optimal tax rules. The paper explicitly notes that the differences between the two criteria seem negligible in this application, despite their conceptual differences. 

Sixth, the strongest labour-supply responses come from low-income households and especially from married women. Table 6.5 shows very large percentage increases in total hours in the bottom deciles, with much weaker or even slightly negative responses at the top. 

Seventh, all optimal rules would “win the referendum” against the current rule in the sense that they generate a strong majority of winners, although the fractions vary across gender, marital status, and income deciles. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that the tax search is restricted to a 6-parameter family of piecewise-linear tax rules. The optimal rule is therefore optimal only within that policy class, not over the space of all conceivable tax systems. 

A second limitation is that the model is static and structural, not quasi-experimental. The behavioural and normative conclusions depend heavily on the validity of the estimated opportunity and preference structure. 

A third limitation, relative to your agenda, is that welfare comparison is based on a common welfare function rather than on the heterogeneous preferences estimated in the behavioural model. So the paper is strong on separating preferences from opportunities behaviourally, but it does not preserve preference heterogeneity at the evaluative stage. 

A fourth limitation is that EOp is operationalized using a narrow circumstance definition: father’s education. That is analytically interesting but limited relative to a richer opportunity-set interpretation of circumstance. 

A fifth limitation is that the opportunity side, though explicit, remains reduced-form. Job opportunities are represented by densities over hours, wages, and sector, not by a fuller latent-job or labour-demand equilibrium process. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing a project that argues empirical optimal taxation should be solved inside the same behavioural model that generates labour supply rather than by importing elasticities into formula-based theory. It gives you a direct methodological critique of the standard approach. 

## possible use for model design

It is highly relevant for model design because it combines singles and couples, joint spousal decisions, exact tax rules, and explicit opportunity densities over jobs. If your JMP moves toward an opportunity-sensitive labour-supply or RURO-style framework, this is a major benchmark. 

## possible use for identification

The paper is useful as an example of identification through detailed structural modeling of both preferences and opportunities. It makes very clear that who responds to tax reform depends on the interaction between elasticities and opportunity sets, not just on tax arithmetic. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

This paper is highly useful as a contrast case. It shows one coherent route for welfare evaluation when heterogeneous actual preferences are not treated as interpersonally comparable: estimate a separate common welfare function and aggregate with EO or EOp social welfare functions. If your project instead wants to preserve heterogeneous preferences, this paper helps define the alternative you are moving away from. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

The paper is single-country, but methodologically it strongly suggests that cross-country optimal-tax or welfare analysis should not transport common elasticities or common feasible sets across institutional settings. Country-specific opportunity structures are likely to matter. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How would the optimal tax schedule change if the opportunity density over jobs were estimated separately across countries rather than within one Norwegian institutional environment? 

Can one preserve the paper’s explicit treatment of opportunity sets while replacing the common welfare function with a welfare criterion that is more sensitive to heterogeneous actual preferences? 

How much of the recommendation to cut low- and middle-income marginal rates is driven by bottom-decile labour-supply elasticities, and how much by the structure of opportunities available to those households? 

Would the similarity between EO and EOp survive if “circumstances” were defined by estimated opportunity sets rather than only by father’s education? 

Could a richer latent-job model with occupation and job-quality characteristics overturn the finding that very high top marginal rates are optimal? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest assumption is the paper’s two-level normative architecture. Behaviour is generated by heterogeneous preferences and heterogeneous opportunities, but welfare is then evaluated with a separate common utility function and fairly stylized circumstance types. That is coherent, but it leaves open the question central to your agenda: whether one can keep the behavioural richness of explicit opportunity sets while using a welfare criterion that is simultaneously more preference-sensitive and more opportunity-sensitive. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

This paper is methodologically upstream of Bargain et al. (2013). Both connect structural labour-supply estimation to normative analysis, but they do so in different ways. Aaberge and Colombino use a common welfare function and EO/EOp aggregation to design optimal taxes in one country, whereas Bargain et al. use estimated heterogeneous preferences to study welfare rankings across countries in the consumption-leisure space. Relative to Bargain et al. (2013), this paper is stronger on explicit opportunity modeling and optimal-tax computation, but weaker on preserving heterogeneous preferences as normatively relevant objects.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper strongly helps separate preferences from opportunities on the behavioural side. The opportunity density (p(h,w,j)) is a structural object, so observed labour-supply responses are not read as pure taste differences. But the paper does not carry that separation fully into the welfare criterion: at the evaluative stage, heterogeneous behavioural utilities are replaced by a common welfare function, and EOp is implemented through family-background types rather than direct opportunity-set comparison. So it is a major contribution for the positive side of the preferences-versus-opportunities distinction, but not the final step for an opportunity-sensitive welfare theory. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The central choice density is
[
\varphi(h,w,j)=
\frac{v(f(wh,I),h,j),p(h,w,j)}
{\iiint_{(x,y,z)\in B} v(f(xy,I),y,z),p(x,y,z),dx,dy,dz},
]
which makes the role of opportunities explicit in behavioural choice. 

The tax-rule search is over
[
y(Z)=
\begin{cases}
Z & \text{if } Z\le E,\
(1-\tau_1)Z+\tau_1E & \text{if } E<Z\le Z_1,\
(1-\tau_2)Z+\tau_2Z_1-\tau_1(Z_1-E) & \text{if } Z_1<Z\le Z_2,\
(1-\tau_3)Z+\tau_3Z_2-\tau_2(Z_2-Z_1)-\tau_1(Z_1-E) & \text{if } Z>Z_2.
\end{cases}
]
This 6-parameter piecewise-linear rule is the policy object optimized in the paper. 

The central welfare-function families are
[
W_k=\int_0^1 p_k(t)F^{-1}(t),dt
\quad\text{and}\quad
\widetilde W_k=\int_0^1 p_k(t)\min_j F_j^{-1}(t),dt.
]
These capture, respectively, equality of outcome and the paper’s extended equality-of-opportunity criterion.
