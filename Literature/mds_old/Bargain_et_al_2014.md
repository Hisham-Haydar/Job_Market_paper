# Full citation

Bargain, Olivier, Mathias Dolls, Dirk Neumann, Andreas Peichl, and Sebastian Siegloch. 2014. “Comparing inequality aversion across countries when labor supply responses differ.” *International Tax and Public Finance* 21: 845–873. DOI: 10.1007/s10797-013-9277-9. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper estimates country-specific labor supply elasticities and then inverts Saez’s discrete optimal tax model to recover the degree of social inequality aversion implicitly embodied in actual tax-benefit systems across 17 EU countries and the US. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it connects structural labor supply estimation to optimal-tax inversion in a way that tries to separate redistributive social preferences from country differences in behavioral responses. Instead of reading redistribution directly from observed tax-benefit generosity, it asks what level of inequality aversion would rationalize those systems once labor supply distortions are taken seriously. 

For your potential JMP, the paper is especially useful as a benchmark on the distinction between individual preferences and normative social preferences. It shows one route for moving from estimated labor supply behavior to a normative object, but it does so within a standard optimal-tax framework rather than an opportunity-sensitive one. That makes it relevant both as a model of how to integrate structural estimation with welfare analysis and as an example of where the opportunity side remains underdeveloped. 

# Core research question

To what extent do social preferences for redistribution, summarized by inequality aversion, differ across countries once one controls for actual cross-country differences in labor supply responsiveness rather than imposing common elasticities? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper studies redistribution through taxes and transfers in 17 European countries and the United States. The policy environment is the existing tax-benefit system in each country, interpreted through the lens of optimal income taxation. The central institutional fact is that redistribution reflects an equity-efficiency trade-off: a country that redistributes more may either have stronger redistributive tastes or face smaller labor supply distortions, so both must be disentangled. 

The empirical focus is on childless single individuals. The authors choose this group because Saez’s discrete optimal tax model is formulated for singles, and because aggregation across heterogeneous household types raises comparability problems linked to household equivalence and joint labor supply. The political process behind observed systems is not modeled explicitly; instead, the paper adopts the fiction of a social planner and assumes the observed system is optimal. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The framework combines two pieces. First, there is a static structural discrete-choice labor supply model estimated separately by country. Second, there is a discrete optimal income tax model following Saez (2002), which is inverted to recover marginal social welfare weights and a scalar parameter of inequality aversion. The paper is therefore both positive and normative. 

On the positive side, individuals choose among a finite set of hours options, with disposable income at each option generated by the tax-benefit schedule. On the normative side, the planner chooses redistribution subject to labor supply elasticities at the intensive and extensive margins. The social objective is welfarist: the planner maximizes a weighted sum of utilities, and the recovered welfare weights summarize the marginal social value of transferring one euro to each income group. 

This is not a RURO or latent-jobs framework. Feasibility is represented through the labor supply model and the tax-benefit schedule, not through explicitly modeled job opportunity sets. The paper does include fixed costs of work in the labor supply model, which the authors say implicitly account for some demand-side constraints, but opportunities are not treated as a first-class object. 

# Key objects

The main behavioral objects are the Saez extensive and intensive labor supply elasticities, (\eta_i) and (\zeta_i), estimated for discrete income groups. The main policy objects are effective taxes (T_i), disposable incomes (C_i), gross incomes (Y_i), and group shares (h_i). The main normative objects are the marginal social welfare weights (g_i) and the inequality-aversion parameter (\gamma). 

The groups are defined as one group of non-workers and five worker income groups. In the baseline, group 0 contains inactive individuals with neither labor nor replacement income, while groups 1–5 are income quintiles among workers. This group structure is central because the inversion of the optimal tax formula is performed at the group level. 

The paper’s single-value summary of social preferences is
[
g_i=\frac{1}{(p\cdot C_i)^\gamma},
]
where (p) is the marginal value of public funds and (\gamma) measures social aversion to inequality. Higher (\gamma) means more redistributive or more “Rawlsian” revealed preferences in the paper’s terminology. 

# Data

The paper uses representative household microdata for 17 European countries and the US, linked to tax-benefit calculators. For EU countries the main tool is EUROMOD; for the US it is TAXSIM combined with the IPUMS version of the CPS. The US analysis uses CPS 2006 data containing information on 2005 incomes. For EU countries, the paper states that it covers policy years 1998 and/or 2001 for the EU-15 and 2005 for the new member states, although appendix tables show country-specific survey/policy-year variation by data source. 

The selected sample includes potential salary workers aged 18–64 and excludes pensioners, students, farmers, and the self-employed. Households with capital income above 25% of total gross income are excluded. The final analysis is restricted to single men and single women without children. The data are cross-sectional survey microdata harmonized for tax-benefit simulation and cross-country comparison. 

The core variables are market income, disposable income, taxes and transfers, hours, wages, non-labor income, and demographic taste shifters such as gender, age, and region. Gross and disposable incomes are aggregated into group averages to match the discrete optimal tax framework. 

# Identification logic

Identification has two layers. Labor supply parameters are identified from observed hours choices, predicted wages, and country-specific nonlinear tax-benefit schedules evaluated at discrete hours points. The authors emphasize that detailed simulation of nonlinear budget sets provides the main parametric source of identification, supplemented in some countries by time variation and spatial variation in tax rules. 

The normative inversion then identifies social welfare weights and (\gamma) from the observed tax-benefit system, group incomes, group shares, and estimated elasticities, under a strong optimality assumption: the observed tax-benefit system must correspond to the optimal one for the planner’s redistributive preferences. Without that fixed-point assumption, the inversion does not go through because taxes, group sizes, and elasticities are jointly endogenous. 

Identification is therefore structural and heavily assumption-driven. It is not reduced-form and not quasi-experimental. The most important identifying assumptions are the structural labor supply specification, wage exogeneity conditional on the model, the appropriateness of the income-group discretization, negligible income effects, and optimality of the observed tax-benefit schedule. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper estimates a structural discrete-choice labor supply model separately for each country. Utility at each hours choice is quadratic in consumption and hours, with coefficients on consumption and hours allowed to vary with taste shifters and unobserved heterogeneity. The model includes fixed costs of work and uses simulated disposable income at each hours choice based on EUROMOD or TAXSIM. Wages are predicted for all observations using a Heckman-corrected wage equation. 

After estimating the labor supply model, the authors simulate individual responses to income changes and aggregate them to the income-group level in order to construct the exact extensive and intensive elasticities used in Saez’s optimal tax formula. They then invert the discrete optimal tax rule to recover group-specific marginal social welfare weights (g_i). Finally, they estimate (\gamma) by fitting the parametric relation between welfare weights and disposable income. 

The paper also performs sensitivity analysis on the elasticity assumptions, the definition of income groups, and the number of groups. A central sensitivity exercise sets the extensive-margin response to zero, motivated by the possibility that policymakers historically underestimated or ignored labor supply distortions when traditional social assistance systems were designed. 

# Treatment of preferences

The paper distinguishes two levels of preferences. At the individual level, it estimates labor supply preferences over consumption and leisure through the structural model. These preferences vary across countries and individuals via observed taste shifters and unobserved heterogeneity. The paper explicitly states that country differences in social preferences should be disentangled from differences in individual consumption-leisure preferences. 

At the social level, the paper recovers redistributive preferences from the tax system itself. These are summarized by marginal social welfare weights and the scalar parameter (\gamma). So the paper is unusual in that it estimates both individual behavioral responses and a social preference parameter from the same empirical setting. 

However, the individual-preference side remains within a conventional labor-supply framework. There is no preference-respecting welfare metric in the Fleurbaey sense here, and no attempt to preserve heterogeneous preferences in interpersonal welfare comparisons. Instead, the normative analysis is standard welfarist optimal-tax analysis with grouped social weights. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

The paper does not model opportunities explicitly. There are no latent job offers, no offer distributions, no rationing model, and no country-specific opportunity sets in the RURO sense. The feasible choice environment is the discrete hours menu plus the tax-benefit schedule. 

The one partial exception is that fixed costs of work are included in the utility specification and are said to account implicitly for some demand-side constraints. But this is still not an explicit opportunity model. It is a reduced-form adjustment within a supply-side labor supply framework, not a first-class modeling of available jobs or hours restrictions. 

For your agenda, this matters because the paper’s effort to separate social preferences from labor supply preferences does not extend to separating either of them from opportunity heterogeneity. It refines the distinction between behavioral preferences and redistributive tastes, but it leaves the opportunity side largely unmodeled. 

# Welfare / normative object

The central welfare object is the tax-benefit revealed degree of social inequality aversion. This is not an equivalent-income measure, compensating variation, or individual welfare ranking. It is a revealed social-planner parameter recovered from the observed tax-benefit schedule under the assumption that the schedule is optimal. 

More precisely, the paper recovers group-specific marginal social welfare weights (g_i) from Saez’s optimal tax formula and then estimates the scalar (\gamma) that summarizes how sharply those weights decline with disposable income. The paper is thus explicitly normative, though implemented through a positive inversion exercise. 

The paper is not a full political-economy model. It is explicit that the social-planner framework is used as a simplification of a more complex political process. It remains agnostic about the actual mechanism through which tax-benefit systems were chosen. 

# Main findings

The first main finding is that labor supply elasticities differ across countries, but not dramatically. Extensive-margin responses are systematically larger than intensive-margin responses, and responses are often especially large for low-income worker groups. 

The second main finding is that allowing country-specific elasticities changes the cross-country ranking of revealed inequality aversion relative to the common practice of imposing uniform elasticities. Even relatively small cross-country elasticity differences matter for the inferred ranking of redistributive tastes. 

The third main finding is that countries can be grouped into three broad clusters. Southern Europe and the US reveal low inequality aversion, close to utilitarian preferences. Continental Europe, the UK, Ireland, and Finland are around intermediate levels, roughly near (\gamma \approx 1). Nordic countries and Belgium reveal the highest inequality aversion, with values the authors describe as close to Rawlsian. 

The fourth main finding is that large extensive-margin elasticities, especially for low-income groups, make traditional social assistance schemes look strongly Rawlsian in revealed-preference terms. If a country retains generous support for the non-working poor despite large participation distortions, the implied social weight on that group must be very high. 

The fifth main finding is that when the extensive-margin response is set to zero, revealed inequality aversion falls mechanically and cross-country differences narrow substantially. This suggests that part of the observed difference in revealed redistributive tastes is tightly linked to how seriously labor supply distortions are taken. 

The sixth main finding is that the broad results are robust to alternative income-group definitions and to using more groups instead of six. 

# Main limitations

The strongest limitation is the optimality assumption. The inversion only works if the observed tax-benefit system is treated as the optimal response of a social planner to the relevant behavioral environment. That is a very strong assumption in any cross-country institutional comparison. 

A second limitation is that opportunities are not modeled explicitly. Fixed costs of work may absorb some demand-side constraints, but the paper does not identify job availability, hours rationing, or opportunity heterogeneity. As a result, the estimated behavioral responses remain partly reduced-form from the standpoint of opportunity-sensitive labor economics. 

A third limitation is the restriction to childless singles. This improves comparability and fits Saez’s framework, but it narrows external validity and excludes the demographic groups most central to many labor-supply and redistribution debates. 

A fourth limitation is the narrow set of behavioral margins. The paper considers labor supply, but not migration, tax evasion, career choice, education, or longer-run margins. The authors acknowledge this directly in the conclusion. 

A fifth limitation is that the welfare object is social inequality aversion within a welfarist optimal-tax framework. This is useful, but it is not a preference-respecting or opportunity-sensitive measure of individual well-being, and it cannot by itself address fairness questions of the sort central to responsibility-sensitive or opportunity-set approaches. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is useful if your framing needs to distinguish three layers that are often collapsed together: observed redistribution, individual labor supply behavior, and deeper social preferences over inequality. It shows how one can argue that observed redistribution alone is not a sufficient indicator of redistributive tastes. 

## possible use for model design

Methodologically, it provides a template for combining a structural labor supply model with a second-stage normative inversion. If your project moves from estimated behavior to welfare or fairness objects, this paper is a good benchmark for how such an empirical architecture can be organized. 

## possible use for identification

The paper is useful as an example of structural identification through nonlinear tax-benefit schedules and then a second identification step through optimal-tax inversion. It also makes the identifying role of the optimality assumption unusually explicit, which is valuable for positioning your own contribution if you want to relax or replace that assumption. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

For welfare measurement, the paper is less directly aligned with your opportunity-sensitive agenda than Bargain et al. (2013), because the normative object here is social inequality aversion rather than an individual welfare metric. Still, it is important if your JMP will discuss how estimated labor supply behavior should or should not be fed into normative evaluation. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

This is a strong reference for disciplined cross-country comparison under harmonized estimation. It shows that even modest cross-country differences in structural behavioral parameters can alter normative rankings. A natural extension for your agenda would be to ask whether those rankings would change again once opportunity sets, rather than just labor supply elasticities, are allowed to differ across countries. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How would tax-benefit revealed social inequality aversion change if labor supply elasticities were estimated in a model with explicit job opportunity sets rather than a standard discrete-hours labor supply framework? 

To what extent do cross-country differences in revealed redistributive tastes survive once one separates labor supply preferences from opportunity heterogeneity and hours availability constraints? 

Can one recover a fairness or inequality-aversion parameter from observed policy while allowing the planner’s objective to depend on opportunity-sensitive well-being rather than standard welfarist utility weights? 

How sensitive are inferred redistributive tastes to the choice of demographic group, especially when moving from childless singles to populations where opportunity constraints differ more strongly across individuals? 

Does the apparent Rawlsian character of some welfare regimes reflect genuine social preference, or does it partly reflect model misspecification on labor-market opportunities and participation constraints? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest omission is that the paper treats the observed tax-benefit system as an optimal response to estimated labor supply distortions while leaving opportunity heterogeneity and the political process largely outside the model. That makes the recovered (\gamma) informative within the model, but not necessarily a clean measure of society’s redistributive ethics. A future paper could challenge this by endogenizing opportunity sets or by replacing the social-planner fixed-point assumption with a more realistic political-economy or fairness-based framework. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

This paper is closely related to Bargain et al. (2013) in that both combine structural labor supply estimation with cross-country normative analysis, but they operate at different normative levels. The 2013 paper studies individual welfare rankings in the consumption-leisure space and emphasizes heterogeneous preferences without using a social aggregator. The 2014 paper instead uses a standard optimal-tax framework to recover social welfare weights and a scalar inequality-aversion parameter from observed tax-benefit systems. Put differently, the 2013 paper is about interpersonal ranking of individuals under alternative welfare metrics, whereas the 2014 paper is about the redistributive preferences of the implicit planner behind observed policy. 

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper helps separate social redistributive preferences from individual labor supply preferences more clearly than much of the redistribution literature. But it does not help much with the deeper opportunities-versus-preferences distinction that matters for your agenda. Because opportunities are not explicitly modeled, any country differences in labor supply responsiveness still reflect a mix of tastes, institutions, and possibly constrained opportunities, even if the paper interprets them as behavioral parameters relevant for optimal taxation. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The core optimal-tax formula is
[
\frac{T_i-T_{i-1}}{C_i-C_{i-1}}
===============================

\frac{1}{\zeta_i h_i}
\sum_{j=i}^{I} h_j
\left[
1-g_j-\eta_j\frac{T_j-T_0}{C_j-C_0}
\right].
]
This is the object the paper inverts to recover social welfare weights from actual tax-benefit systems. 

The Saez elasticities are defined as
[
\zeta_i=\frac{C_i-C_{i-1}}{h_i}\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial (C_i-C_{i-1})},
\qquad
\eta_i=\frac{C_i-C_0}{h_i}\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial (C_i-C_0)}.
]
The paper’s single-value inequality-aversion summary is then obtained from
[
g_i=\frac{1}{(p\cdot C_i)^\gamma}.
]


A concise statement of the main conclusion is that accounting for country-specific labor supply responses changes the ranking of revealed inequality aversion, and that ignoring those responses makes redistributive tastes look “less pronounced and much more similar.”
