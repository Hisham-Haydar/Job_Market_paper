# Full citation

Bloemen, Hans G. 2008. “Job Search, Hours Restrictions, and Desired Hours of Work.” *Journal of Labor Economics* 26(1): 137–179. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper estimates a structural job-search model in which unemployed workers receive random wage-hours job offers, uses subjective desired-hours information to separate preferred from offered hours, and studies how benefits and hours restrictions affect job acceptance and unemployment duration. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it connects three literatures that are often kept separate: neoclassical labor supply, hours-restrictions models, and structural job search. Its core move is to treat a job offer as a wage-hours package and to use desired-hours data to identify preferences over hours separately from the offer distribution. That is a substantive step beyond models that infer both preferences and constraints from observed hours alone. 

For your potential JMP, this is highly relevant on the opportunities-versus-preferences margin. It is not a RURO model with rich multidimensional jobs, but it is very close in spirit: labor market behavior is generated by both preferences and the distribution of opportunities, and the paper is explicit that observed hours alone do not identify the two cleanly. 

# Core research question

How can one estimate a structural job-search model in which hours are a job characteristic, hours are demand-side constrained, and subjective desired-hours data are used to distinguish optimal from offered hours; and what does such a model imply for policy questions about benefits, standard hours, and the distribution of accepted working hours? 

# Economic setting and context

The institutional setting is the Dutch labor market in the second half of the 1980s. The paper studies male workers observed in the Dutch Socio-Economic Panel and uses information on unemployment spells, employment spells, accepted wages and hours, and desired working hours. The motivation is the empirical failure of unconstrained neoclassical labor supply models to account for employer-determined hours, involuntary unemployment, and the observed bunching of hours at institutional norms such as the standard workweek. 

The policy context is also concrete. The paper asks whether lower unemployment benefits induce acceptance of jobs with less preferred hours and whether reducing the standard workweek can change the unemployment hazard and the distribution of accepted hours. These are explicitly modeled as changes in the reservation-acceptance problem and, for the latter, as changes in the hours-offer distribution. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model is a structural job-search model with labor supply and hours restrictions. An unemployed individual receives job offers at a stochastic arrival rate. A job offer is a pair ((w,h)), with wage (w) and employer-chosen weekly hours (h), drawn from a joint wage-hours offer distribution. The worker accepts the offer if its utility exceeds the value of continued search. This is therefore a dynamic search model with constrained job opportunities, not a static unconstrained hours-choice model. 

The period utility function is defined over net weekly income and weekly hours, with a Hausman-style functional form that yields an explicit labor supply function. The model allows unobserved heterogeneity in both preferences and the job-offer arrival rate. Layoffs occur with an exogenous rate, so accepted jobs are not permanent. The key equilibrium object on the worker side is a reservation utility level, equivalently an hours-dependent reservation wage schedule. 

The framework is purely positive. There is no welfare-ranking object, no equivalent income, and no social welfare function. Its policy use is behavioral: unemployment duration, acceptance probabilities, accepted-hours distributions, and comparative statics with respect to benefits and hours-offer distributions. 

# Key objects

The main preference object is the utility function (u(y,h;e)), where income and hours jointly determine flow utility and (e) captures random preferences. This yields optimal hours in the neoclassical sense and, more importantly, an hours-dependent reservation wage schedule. The latter is central because it determines which wage-hours offers are acceptable. 

The main opportunity objects are the job-offer arrival rate (\lambda(v)), the wage-offer distribution, and the discrete hours-offer probabilities. Hours offers are modeled as discrete and institutionally shaped, while optimal hours are continuous. That distinction is central to the identification strategy. 

The key empirical outcome objects are unemployment duration, employment duration, accepted wages, accepted hours, desired hours, the unemployment hazard, reservation wages by hours level, and the simulated distribution of accepted hours under counterfactual policies. 

# Data

The data are from the Dutch Socio-Economic Panel. The paper uses waves from October 1985 through April 1989, exploiting monthly retrospective labor market histories within each survey wave. The analysis sample consists of men younger than 65 who were unemployed or employed during the observation period. 

The paper reports 573 unemployment-spell observations, of which 297 end in a transition to employment. It also uses 4,747 employment-spell observations for the estimation of the layoff rate. Observed working hours and net earnings are available for 3,962 observations in the combined working sample, and desired hours are available for 3,405 observations; among workers with both observed and desired hours, 68.6% report satisfaction with current hours, 25.3% report overemployment, and 6.1% report wanting more hours. Table 1 on pages 152–153 reports these summary statistics. 

The main variables are observed hours, net earnings, desired hours, nonlabor income, unemployment duration, employment duration, age, family size, marital status, education level, education type, and region. The data also contain subjective information on satisfaction with current hours and desired weekly hours, which is crucial for the identification of preferences versus hours offers. 

# Identification logic

Identification is one of the paper’s central contributions. Without desired-hours information, observed hours alone are asked to identify both preferences over hours and the hours-offer distribution. The paper explicitly states that this is a serious problem: many combinations of offer probabilities and preference parameters can fit observed hours reasonably well, because observed hours reflect both demand-side restrictions and worker preferences. 

The paper addresses this by bringing in desired-hours data. In the “optimal desired hours” model, desired hours are linked directly to optimal hours up to measurement error. In the “hours satisfaction” model, the survey structure is used more literally: workers report satisfaction when observed hours are close enough to optimal hours, and only otherwise report a distinct desired-hours number. This gives a second hours-related data series that helps pin down the preference parameters, after which observed hours can identify the hours-offer distribution more credibly. 

Identification of the job-offer arrival rate comes from unemployment duration data, in line with job-search theory, while the layoff rate is identified from employment duration. The wage-offer distribution is identified from accepted wages, conditional on the reservation rule. The model remains structurally and parametrically identified rather than nonparametrically identified; the paper is clear that structure and functional form still do a large amount of work. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The model is estimated by maximum likelihood, using likelihood contributions for unemployed individuals, unemployed individuals with observed post-unemployment job characteristics, and employed individuals. The likelihood combines unemployment duration, accepted wage-hours pairs, desired hours, and job tenure. Because accepted wages are observed with measurement error, the accepted wage is treated as latent and integrated out. 

The model includes unobserved heterogeneity in preferences and in the job-offer arrival rate, so likelihood evaluation requires numerical integration. Bloemen uses smooth simulated maximum likelihood with 30 replications. The empirical implementation estimates three variants: a base model without desired-hours information, an “optimal desired hours” model linking desired to optimal hours with measurement error, and an “hours satisfaction” model that allows workers to be satisfied even when observed hours differ somewhat from optimal hours. 

Policy simulations then change either benefit levels or the hours-offer distribution and recompute unemployment hazards and accepted-hours distributions. This is not reduced-form policy evaluation; it is fully structural counterfactual analysis conditional on the estimated search and offer processes. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled directly over income and hours. Household composition enters utility through family size and marital status, and age also shifts preferences. The estimated utility parameters imply an optimal-hours schedule and an hours-dependent reservation wage function. These reservation wages are lowest at the individual’s optimal hours and increase as offered hours move away from that optimum. 

The crucial substantive point is that preferences are not inferred from observed hours alone. The paper shows that, without desired-hours data, the estimated preferences are too flat and can imply implausibly high optimal hours, essentially because the model is overusing preferences to fit the observed hours distribution. Once desired-hours data are introduced, the preference profile becomes much sharper and more plausible. 

This is not a preference-respecting welfare paper. Preferences are behavioral primitives used to determine job acceptance and the unemployment hazard, not normative objects for interpersonal comparison. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is the most important section for your agenda.

The paper models opportunities explicitly as job offers. Workers do not choose arbitrary hours from a common feasible set. They receive wage-hours offers drawn from a joint offer distribution, and hours are fixed by the employer within the offer. This is an explicit opportunity-set logic, though the opportunities are summarized only by wages and hours rather than by richer job characteristics. 

Hours restrictions are therefore first-class objects. The discrete hours-offer distribution captures employer-side constraints and institutional bunching in working hours. The paper’s main empirical argument is that observed working hours are the joint result of this offer distribution and worker preferences. Desired-hours data are introduced precisely to separate these two forces. 

Relative to your research direction, this is highly relevant but still narrower than RURO or latent-jobs work. Opportunities are multidimensional only in a limited sense: wage and hours. There are no occupations, amenities, sectors, or job-quality characteristics in the offer distribution. But the paper is strongly on the side of modeling constraints explicitly rather than absorbing them into taste parameters. 

# Welfare / normative object

None. The paper does not develop a welfare metric such as compensating variation, equivalent income, or a social welfare function. Its policy evaluation is behavioral and comparative-static rather than normative in the welfare-economics sense. 

Its relevance for welfare analysis is indirect but important. It improves the behavioral foundation of any later welfare exercise by showing that hours outcomes and unemployment durations cannot be interpreted as free choices if wage-hours opportunities are constrained. 

# Main findings

First, the paper shows that using desired-hours information materially changes the interpretation of the model. The base model fits observed hours best, but only because it is effectively overparameterized: it uses observed hours to recover both preferences and offer probabilities. Once desired-hours information is imposed, the fitted preference profile becomes much more informative about true preferred hours. The discussion on pages 167–168 states this explicitly. 

Second, the base model implies overly flat preferences over hours. Its reservation wage schedule as a function of hours is comparatively flat, with the minimum around 48–60 hours per week, implying underemployment on average. This conflicts with the survey evidence, where overemployment is much more common than underemployment. Figure 2 on page 161 and the surrounding discussion make this contrast especially clear. 

Third, both models that use desired-hours data imply more plausible optimal-hours patterns. The “optimal desired hours” and “hours satisfaction” variants place the minimum reservation wage around 36–40 hours per week and imply stronger identification of preferences over working hours. The “hours satisfaction” model also better captures the concentration of desired hours around 40 hours and the prevalence of overemployment. Table 8 on page 165 and the discussion on pages 165–168 support this. 

Fourth, the estimated unemployment-duration elasticity with respect to benefits is about 0.35 in the base and optimal-desired-hours models and about 0.50 in the hours-satisfaction model. Table 7 on page 162 reports these elasticities. Thus, once workers are allowed to accept a broader range of off-optimal hours, the unemployment hazard becomes more sensitive to benefits. 

Fifth, a 5% cut in unemployment benefits only modestly affects the distribution of accepted hours, but it induces a small shift toward part-time work. Table 10 on pages 169–170 shows the share at 40 hours falling from 68.1% to 66.8%, with small increases in several lower-hours categories. Bloemen interprets this as a consequence of lower unemployment income making lower-income part-time jobs more acceptable. 

Sixth, changing the hours-offer distribution can raise the unemployment hazard. When the standard workweek is shifted from 40 to 36 hours, the paper reports a 2.2% increase in the hazard; when the entire offer distribution is set equal to the empirical desired-hours distribution, the hazard rises by 1.3%. The simulation results are discussed on pages 170–172 and reported in Table 11. 

Seventh, the hours-satisfaction model improves the fit of desired hours but still fails a formal goodness-of-fit test for observed hours, while the base model passes the observed-hours fit test. The paper interprets this as the consequence of the base model’s overparameterization and of the richer models’ harder task of fitting both observed and desired hours simultaneously. This appears in the discussion around the Heckman chi-square test on pages 166–168. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that the job offer space is low-dimensional. Jobs differ only in wage and hours. There are no sectoral, occupational, task, amenity, or job-quality dimensions, so the model is still much thinner than a full latent-jobs or RURO framework. 

A second limitation is that the budget constraint is assumed linear. Bloemen notes this explicitly as a simplification. For policy analysis involving taxes and transfers, this is restrictive relative to modern structural labor supply microsimulation. 

A third limitation is stationarity. The residual analysis indicates neglected negative duration dependence. The model does not fully explain longer unemployment spells, even with unobserved heterogeneity. Figure 3 on page 163 and the discussion on pages 163–164 make this clear. 

A fourth limitation is sample scope. The analysis is for men in the Netherlands in the late 1980s. That limits external validity, especially for populations where part-time work, care constraints, or joint household labor supply are central. 

A fifth limitation, relative to your agenda, is that the model remains purely positive. It does not translate the preferences-versus-opportunities distinction into a welfare metric or fairness criterion. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing an argument that observed labor supply outcomes reflect both preferences and constrained opportunities, and that observed hours alone do not identify the two. It is particularly useful if you want to motivate why subjective desired-hours information, or some other direct signal of preferences, is valuable. 

## possible use for model design

It provides a strong template for modeling labor market opportunities as wage-hours offers rather than as a universal hours set. Even if your own project moves toward richer jobs with occupations or amenities, the paper offers a clean benchmark architecture for dynamic job acceptance under hours restrictions. 

## possible use for identification

This is one of the paper’s strongest uses for your agenda. It shows precisely how an additional data source on desired hours can help separate preferences from the offer distribution. That is directly relevant if your project seeks to separate preferences from opportunities with stronger empirical discipline. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

Indirect only. The paper does not provide the welfare metric itself, but it improves the behavioral side on which any welfare analysis would rely. In your framework, it could help justify why a welfare comparison should not treat accepted hours as fully chosen hours. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

Potentially useful as a methodological module. One could imagine estimating analogous wage-hours offer distributions across countries and asking whether apparent cross-country preference differences partly reflect different opportunity structures rather than different tastes. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How much of the cross-country variation in labor supply elasticities is due to differences in wage-hours offer distributions rather than differences in preferences?

Can desired-hours data, or other subjective information, be used in a RURO or latent-jobs model to separately identify preference parameters and opportunity distributions over richer job characteristics?

How would welfare conclusions change if accepted jobs with nonpreferred hours were treated as constrained rather than fully chosen outcomes?

Can one extend this search-with-hours-restrictions framework to nonlinear tax-benefit systems and joint household decisions without losing identification of preferences versus opportunities? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest simplification is that the model treats jobs as wage-hours packages only. That is already a major improvement over unconstrained hours choice, but it leaves out the richer nonpecuniary dimensions that likely matter for both acceptance and search. A future paper could challenge the model by expanding the opportunity set to multidimensional latent jobs and by carrying the preferences-versus-opportunities distinction into welfare analysis rather than stopping at behavioral acceptance. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

The relation is indirect but important. Bargain et al. (2013) is a structural labor supply and welfare-comparison paper, whereas Bloemen (2008) is a structural job-search and hours-restrictions paper. Relative to Bargain et al., Bloemen is much stronger on the explicit modeling of constrained wage-hours opportunities and much weaker on welfare measurement. For your corpus, the papers are complementary: Bloemen helps with the positive identification of preferences versus constraints, while Bargain et al. helps with the welfare layer once behavior has been modeled. 

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is directly relevant to the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction. It is one of the clearest papers in your set showing that observed hours do not reveal preferences unless the hours-offer distribution is separately modeled. By introducing desired-hours data, Bloemen tries to identify preferred hours separately from employer-constrained offered hours. It does not solve the broader multidimensional opportunities problem, but it is a strong step in exactly the direction you care about. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The core reservation-utility equation is
[
\bar u(q)=u(b+m,0;e)+\frac{\lambda(v)}{r+\delta}\sum_{l=1}^L p_l \int_{y(h_l,\bar u(q);e)}^\infty [u(wh_l+m,h_l;e)-\bar u(q)]f(w),dw,
]
which is the formal expression for the value of search and the acceptance threshold. It is the paper’s key theoretical object. 

The hours-dependent reservation wage (y(h_l,\bar u(q);e)) is defined implicitly by
[
u(y(h_l,\bar u(q);e)h_l+m,h_l;e)=\bar u(q).
]
This is the most important formula for your agenda because it shows directly how the acceptability of a job depends jointly on hours and wages. 

The unemployment hazard is
[
\vartheta(q)=\lambda(v)\sum_{l=1}^L p_l ,\bar F!\big(y(h_l,\bar u(q);e)\big),
]
which makes unemployment exit depend on both the offer distribution and preferences. 

The chart on page 161, Figure 2, is also highly useful: it plots reservation wages against hours and shows how the base model produces a comparatively flat schedule, whereas the models using desired-hours information generate a clear minimum around 36–40 hours. That visual is one of the paper’s most concise demonstrations of the identification gain from desired-hours data.
