# Full citation

Aaberge, Rolf, John K. Dagsvik, and Steinar Strøm. 1995. “Labor Supply Responses and Welfare Effects of Tax Reforms.” *The Scandinavian Journal of Economics* 97(4): 635–659. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper develops and estimates a microeconometric labor supply model with heterogeneous job opportunities and uses it to evaluate how replacing Norway’s 1979 progressive tax system with either proportional taxation or the 1992 tax rules affects labor supply, income distribution, and household welfare. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it is an early and important attempt to move welfare analysis of tax reform away from representative-agent or simple labor-supply models toward a microeconometric framework with observed and unobserved heterogeneity, non-convex budget sets, joint household decisions, and explicit quantity constraints in job opportunities. 

For your potential JMP, it is especially relevant because it already contains the central distinction you care about: labor supply behavior is modeled as choice among feasible “job packages,” not just as free choice over hours. The paper is therefore a precursor to later opportunity-sensitive labor supply work and directly relevant for the preferences-versus-opportunities question. 

# Core research question

How much distortion did Norway’s highly progressive 1979 income tax system impose on labor supply, and what are the labor-supply, distributional, and welfare consequences of replacing it either with the actual 1992 tax system or with a revenue-neutral proportional tax on wage income? 

# Economic setting and context

The institutional setting is Norway at the end of the 1970s, before the major reductions in marginal tax rates that occurred during the 1980s and early 1990s. The paper emphasizes that in 1979 marginal tax rates were very high and strongly progressive, with later reforms gradually flattening the system, especially between 1991 and 1992. 

The analysis compares three regimes: the 1979 baseline, a hypothetical revenue-neutral proportional tax on wage income, and the actual 1992 tax system. The policy question is therefore not abstract optimal taxation, but the efficiency and welfare consequences of concrete or near-concrete tax reforms in a Scandinavian welfare-state setting. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The paper uses a static microeconometric labor supply model in which labor supply is a discrete choice among feasible “job packages.” These job packages are characterized by hours, wages, and unobserved non-pecuniary job attributes. The model is designed for married couples making joint decisions. 

This is not a standard continuous labor-supply model with only consumption and leisure as choice variables. Instead, the household chooses among feasible opportunities, and the model allows different jobs to differ in hours, wages, and non-pecuniary characteristics. The framework also allows for quantity constraints: different types of jobs are not equally available to all agents. 

The paper is both positive and welfare-analytic. Positively, it estimates labor supply responses. Normatively, it evaluates tax reforms through equivalent variation at the household level and then studies the distribution of welfare gains and losses. It is not a social-welfare-function paper in the later formal sense, but it clearly goes beyond purely positive labor-supply estimation. 

# Key objects

The main behavioral objects are household labor supply, participation, hours conditional on participation, gross household income, disposable income, and exact aggregate labor-supply elasticities. The paper distinguishes participation effects from hours effects and reports these by gender and income group. 

The core structural objects are the deterministic utility function (v(C,h_M,h_F)), random job-specific taste shifters, and the opportunity densities over hours and wages. For couples, the model also contains opportunity-set interaction terms linking spouses’ feasible opportunities. 

The main welfare object is household equivalent variation (EV), defined as the amount of non-taxable income that must be added under the initial tax system to make the household indifferent between the initial and alternative tax regimes. The paper studies the distribution of EV, not only its mean. 

# Data

The estimation uses Norwegian labor market and tax-return data from 1979 for married couples. The paper does not provide, in the excerpted discussion, a detailed sample-size table in the way later papers often do, but it is clear that the estimation is based on married-couple microdata from 1979. 

The key observed variables include spouses’ hours of work, wages, household consumption/disposable income, age, education, experience, and the number of children below and above age six. Tax and deduction rules from 1979 are explicitly incorporated into the household budget constraint, and the 1992 rules are used for counterfactual simulation. 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural and relies on the random-utility representation together with parametric restrictions on the opportunity densities. The model separates the deterministic utility component from the opportunity component, with observed choice probabilities depending on both the utility index and the aggregate representation of feasible opportunities. 

A key point is that the opportunity densities are not left unrestricted. The paper states that some additional assumptions on offered hours are necessary for full identification. In practice, offered hours are modeled as uniform with peaks at part-time and full-time for women and at full-time for men, while offered wages follow lognormal densities. These assumptions are part of the identification strategy, not just an empirical convenience. 

Relative to your agenda, the important point is that identification is not just of preference parameters; it is also of the opportunity structure. This is one of the paper’s main methodological contributions. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The model is estimated by a procedure suggested by McFadden (1978), which the authors describe as yielding results close to full maximum likelihood. The opportunity densities are jointly estimated with the utility-function parameters. 

The deterministic utility function is specified flexibly in consumption and the spouses’ leisure, with age, children, and an interaction between spouses’ leisure entering the utility index. The wage opportunity densities are modeled as lognormal with means depending on education and experience. The opportunity densities of hours include structural peaks at part-time and full-time. 

For policy simulation, the paper uses the estimated model in partial equilibrium, keeping the distribution of feasible opportunities fixed across tax reforms. Equivalent variation is then simulated from the indirect random utilities under alternative tax systems. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are heterogeneous through both observed and unobserved components. Observed heterogeneity enters the deterministic utility through ages, children, and the interaction between spouses’ leisure. Unobserved heterogeneity enters through job-specific random taste shifters. 

The paper explicitly criticizes simpler models that allow preferences only over consumption and leisure and ignore other job characteristics. Instead, it treats labor supply as embedded in a broader ranking of job packages. In this sense, preferences are over jobs, not only over hours. 

The paper also emphasizes that welfare metrics such as EV become random variables in the presence of unobserved heterogeneity. This is an important conceptual move: the welfare effects of tax reform are heterogeneous because tastes and opportunities are heterogeneous. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is one of the most important sections for your agenda.

The paper models opportunities explicitly. The household does not choose freely from a universal hours set. Instead, it chooses among feasible market and non-market opportunities, and the model allows jobs with different hours and wages to differ in their availability. The opportunity set therefore enters the model structurally. 

The authors are very clear that this is meant to capture quantity constraints: different types of jobs are not equally available to every agent, and agents differ by qualification while jobs differ by qualification required. This is not an afterthought or a fixed-cost add-on. It is part of the basic behavioral framework. 

The empirical implementation remains reduced-form. Opportunities are summarized by densities over hours and wages, with peaks at common work schedules. Jobs are not characterized by a richer multidimensional vector of observed non-pecuniary attributes, occupations, or tasks. Still, for a 1995 paper, this is a strong and explicit treatment of constrained opportunity sets. 

# Welfare / normative object

The welfare object is household equivalent variation. The paper follows the view that EV summarizes the household’s net welfare gain or loss from a reform, incorporating both increased consumption and changes in leisure. 

The paper does not define a formal social welfare function over households. Instead, it reports the mean of EV and also distributional summaries of EV across households. It explicitly notes that aggregating welfare gains and losses across households is normatively delicate and that conventional approaches often implicitly assume utilitarian aggregation. 

The paper is therefore positive with welfare applications, rather than a full normative theory paper. Its normative language is practical and Hicksian rather than axiomatic. 

# Main findings

First, replacing the 1979 tax system with a revenue-neutral proportional tax on wage income generates very large labor supply responses, especially for married women and especially in the lower tail of the income distribution. Table 4 on page 20 reports female participation rising from 74.2 to 84.3 percent and female annual hours from 1,043 to 1,642; the corresponding male figures also increase materially. 

Second, proportional taxation raises both gross and disposable household income substantially. Table 4 reports mean gross household income rising from NOK 136,279 to NOK 208,449 and disposable income from NOK 95,656 to NOK 167,825. The mean EV is NOK 25,956, equal to 63.8 percent of tax revenue under the 1979 rules. 

Third, proportional taxation reduces inequality in disposable household income, despite being less progressive mechanically than the 1979 system. Table 6 on page 22 shows the Gini for disposable income falling from 0.161 under the 1979 rules to 0.152 under proportional taxes. The mechanism is behavioral: low-income households increase labor supply much more than high-income households. 

Fourth, this income-distribution result does not translate into an egalitarian welfare result. The paper stresses that rich households benefit more in welfare terms because they receive large disposable-income gains without large increases in effort, whereas poor households must work much more for their gains. 

Fifth, the actual 1992 reform produces more modest labor-supply and welfare effects than proportional taxation. Table 4 shows smaller increases in participation and hours, and the mean EV is only NOK 1,588 while mean tax revenue rises by about NOK 8,186 per household. 

Sixth, the pattern of labor supply elasticities is sharply declining in income and much stronger for women than for men. The paper emphasizes this as the core reason why flat or proportional taxation can have such large bottom-end labor-supply effects. 

# Main limitations

The first major limitation is the partial-equilibrium setting. The distribution of feasible opportunities is kept fixed across reforms, so wage rates and job availability do not adjust endogenously. The authors explicitly note that this amounts to assuming that supply creates its own demand. 

A second limitation is scope. The model is estimated only for married couples in Norway using 1979 data. External validity to singles, later labor markets, or other institutional settings is limited. 

A third limitation is that the opportunity structure, though explicit, is still reduced-form. It is summarized by hours and wage densities with unobserved non-pecuniary attributes, not by a richer job-offer process with observed occupations, tasks, or employer-side behavior. 

A fourth limitation is welfare interpretation. The paper uses household EV and its distribution, but it does not solve the deeper normative problem of how to aggregate heterogeneous household welfare effects. It notes this issue, but does not resolve it. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing a project that argues labor supply should be modeled as constrained job choice rather than free choice of hours. It shows that this perspective already existed in the mid-1990s and was motivated by exactly the kind of concern you have: quantity constraints and heterogeneous job availability. 

## possible use for model design

It provides a direct precursor to RURO-style modeling. The central move—choice among feasible job packages with explicit opportunity densities—can be used as a bridge from standard discrete-hours labor supply toward richer latent-job frameworks. 

## possible use for identification

The paper is especially useful for identification because it shows that assumptions on the opportunity distribution are necessary to separate utility parameters from opportunity parameters. That is an important lesson for any project that seeks to distinguish preferences from opportunities empirically. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

It is useful as an early example of how behavioral tax reform analysis can generate different conclusions depending on whether one looks at disposable income or welfare. That distinction is central for your JMP if you want to move beyond income-based comparisons. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

The paper is not cross-country, but it supports a cross-country agenda indirectly. It suggests that institutional comparisons will be misleading if they ignore how tax systems interact with heterogeneous opportunity sets and bottom-end labor supply responses. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How much of the welfare gain from tax reform attributed here to reduced distortions would survive in a model where opportunity sets adjust endogenously rather than remaining fixed?

Would the income-inequality reduction from flatter wage taxation still hold in a richer model with explicit labor demand and endogenous job availability?

How much of the estimated female labor supply responsiveness in the bottom deciles reflects preferences versus the structure of feasible part-time and full-time job opportunities?

Can one combine this paper’s opportunity-sensitive behavioral model with a welfare metric that is more explicit about interpersonal comparability and heterogeneous preferences? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest simplification is the fixed-opportunity partial-equilibrium assumption. The paper correctly treats labor supply as constrained job choice, but then holds the distribution of feasible jobs constant across reforms. That is a strong simplification because exactly the households whose supply expands most under reform are those for whom job availability and employer response may matter most. A future paper could challenge the results by allowing the opportunity set itself to respond to policy. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

The relation is indirect but important. Bargain et al. (2013) studies welfare rankings under heterogeneous preferences in a cross-country consumption-leisure setting, whereas this 1995 paper studies tax-reform welfare effects in one country using household EV. But methodologically this paper is an upstream precursor: it already insists that heterogeneous opportunities and job constraints matter, and it already shows that income-based and welfare-based conclusions can diverge. Relative to Bargain et al. (2013), it is stronger on explicit constrained job choice and weaker on explicit normative treatment of heterogeneous preferences in welfare comparison. 

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper clearly helps separate preferences from opportunities. It does not treat labor supply as free hours choice under a common feasible set. Instead, households choose among feasible job packages, and the opportunity densities are separate structural objects. At the same time, because opportunities are modeled in reduced form and held fixed in counterfactuals, the paper stops short of a full opportunity-equilibrium framework. Still, it is strongly on the “do not conflate preferences and opportunities” side of the literature. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The most important conceptual formula is the decomposition
[
V_i(h,w)=\psi(h,w)e_i(h,w),
]
with
[
\psi(h,w)=v(f(wh,I),h),
]
which separates the structural utility term from the random opportunity-specific component. This is the key formal expression behind the paper’s separation of preferences and feasible job opportunities. 

The choice probability for a job with hours and wage ((h,w)) is proportional to
[
\psi(h,w),g_0,g(h,w),
]
so the probability of observed labor supply depends jointly on utility and the opportunity density. That is the single most important formula for your agenda. 

The paper’s welfare metric is defined by
[
V_i(0,f_1)=V_i(EV,f_0),
]
which gives equivalent variation as the amount of money that, under the initial tax system, makes the household indifferent to the alternative tax system.
