# Full citation

Aaberge, Rolf, and Ugo Colombino. 2012. “Accounting for family background when designing optimal income taxes: a microeconometric simulation analysis.” *Journal of Population Economics* 25: 741–761. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper develops an extended Equality of Opportunity criterion based on family background and uses a structural household labour-supply microsimulation model to identify second-best Italian income tax-transfer rules that are optimal under that criterion. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it moves from evaluating existing tax systems to designing optimal tax-transfer rules under an explicit opportunity-sensitive normative criterion. It is not only about labour-supply responses or redistribution in the usual EO sense; it asks how family background should matter for tax design when taxes may depend only on income. 

For your JMP, it is particularly relevant because it sits close to your interests in welfare, heterogeneous preferences, labour supply, and the distinction between effort and circumstances. At the same time, it is methodologically different from an opportunity-set or RURO agenda: “opportunity” here is operationalized primarily through parental education types in the welfare criterion, while labour-market opportunities enter separately in the behavioural model. 

# Core research question

What income tax-transfer rule is optimal, in a second-best sense, when the objective is equality of opportunity defined with respect to family background, and when labour supply is generated by a microeconometric model with singles, couples, and quantity constraints? 

# Economic setting and context

The empirical setting is Italy, with the benchmark institutional environment given by the 1993 Italian tax rule. The exercise is explicitly second-best: the tax-transfer rule is allowed to depend only on income, not directly on observed circumstances such as parental education, even though those circumstances define the EOp types. 

The broader context is the debate between Equality of Outcome and Equality of Opportunity. The paper argues that redistribution may be more politically and ethically acceptable when targeted at disadvantages due to circumstances beyond the individual’s control, rather than at outcomes associated with effort. Family background is the circumstance emphasized in this application. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model is a static structural household labour-supply microsimulation model combined with an optimal-tax problem under alternative social evaluation criteria. On the behavioural side, it is a microeconometric labour-supply model for single females, single males, and couples, with simultaneous spousal decisions and quantity constraints on the distribution of hours. On the normative side, it compares EO and EOp social welfare criteria and searches computationally for the tax-transfer rule that maximizes them. 

The choice object in the behavioural model is labour supply under alternative tax rules; the paper refers to the model described in the supplementary material and emphasizes that it handles couples and singles together, with quantity constraints and heterogeneous labour-supply responses. The feasible set is therefore not a frictionless continuous hours choice set. However, the paper is not a RURO/latent-jobs paper in its main framing; its central normative distinction is between effort and circumstances, not between preferences and job-offer distributions. 

The framework is both positive and normative. Positive because labour-supply responses are simulated from an estimated structural model; normative because those responses are fed into EO and EOp objective functions to identify optimal second-best tax-transfer rules. 

# Key objects

The main normative objects are the EO social welfare functions
[
W_k=\int_0^1 p_k(t)F^{-1}(t),dt
]
and the extended EOp social welfare functions
[
\widetilde W_k=\int_0^1 p_k(t)\min_j F_j^{-1}(t),dt,
]
where (F_j) is the income distribution for type (j), defined by parental education, and (p_k(t)) determines inequality aversion. The associated EO and EOp inequality measures are (C_k) and (\widetilde C_k). 

The central classification object is the type partition by father’s education: less than 5 years, 5–8 years, and more than 8 years. These types are interpreted as circumstances beyond individual control. 

The main policy objects are two-parameter affine tax-transfer rules (x=c+(1-t)y) and three-parameter rules with a transfer (c), a first-bracket tax rate (t_1), and a second-bracket tax rate (t_2). The paper also studies the case where universal lump-sum taxes are ruled out. 

# Data

The paper uses the Bank of Italy 1993 Survey of Household Income and Wealth (SHIW 1993). The sample contains single females, single males, and couples between about 19 and 54 years old. The exact model and sample construction are delegated partly to the electronic supplementary material. 

The data support the estimation and simulation of labour-supply behaviour under the 1993 Italian tax rule. The paper constructs equivalent income at the individual level by dividing household disposable income by the square root of household size. These individual equivalent incomes are then grouped by parental-education type to build the type-specific distributions used in the EOp criterion. 

The paper is not a harmonized cross-country dataset exercise. It is a single-country structural simulation using Italian survey microdata. 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural and simulation-based. The paper does not identify optimal tax parameters from observed reform episodes; instead, it estimates labour-supply behaviour and then searches over tax-rule parameter spaces to maximize EO or EOp welfare criteria under constant revenue. 

The welfare side relies on the maintained assumption that parental education is a valid proxy for circumstances beyond individual control and that rank within the type-specific income distribution can stand in for effort, following Roemer’s framework. The extended EOp criterion then evaluates tax systems through the distribution of the worst-off type at each quantile. 

A crucial identification assumption for your interests is that the behavioural heterogeneity captured by the microeconometric model is informative enough to determine how different types respond to alternative tax rules. The authors explicitly argue that heterogeneous labour-supply responses are decisive for the shape of the optimal rule. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper uses a previously estimated microeconometric model of household labour supply for Italy. It simulates choices of singles and couples under alternative tax-transfer rules while imposing constant total net tax revenue and non-negative disposable household incomes. 

The simulation proceeds in five steps: apply a tax rule to gross incomes, simulate labour supply, compute individual equivalent incomes, construct type-specific income distributions, and evaluate the resulting allocations through the EO or EOp welfare function. Optimization is then performed computationally over the relevant parameter space. 

The paper first analyzes affine two-parameter rules to remain comparable with Roemer et al. (2003), then moves to three-parameter rules, and finally studies optimal rules when universal lump-sum taxes are not feasible. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are not the conceptual centerpiece of the paper, even though the behavioural model is structural. The paper relies on heterogeneous labour-supply behaviour estimated from singles and couples, but it does not center its normative argument on heterogeneous preferences per se. Instead, the key normative split is between circumstances and effort. 

Relative to your agenda, this is important: the paper is not trying to preserve heterogeneous preferences in welfare comparison. It aggregates on the basis of equivalent income distributions across types, not on the basis of individuals’ own preference orderings. Thus it is opportunity/circumstances-sensitive in a Roemer sense, but not preference-respecting in a Fleurbaey-Maniquet or Bargain et al. sense. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

The paper does model constraints in the behavioural block. It explicitly states that the labour-supply model accounts for joint household decisions and quantity constraints on the distribution of hours. This means behaviour is not generated from a fully unconstrained hours-choice framework. 

However, “opportunity” in the welfare criterion is not represented as job opportunity sets or latent job distributions. It is represented through family-background types, i.e. parental education as a circumstance affecting outcomes beyond individual control. Thus the paper is opportunity-sensitive in the Roemer sense, not in the RURO/job-offer-set sense. 

For your research direction, this is both a strength and a limitation. It is a strength because it shows how circumstance-sensitive tax design can be made operational. It is a limitation because it does not directly model the opportunity set as the feasible job menu faced by the individual. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative. Its main welfare object is the extended Equality of Opportunity criterion, which combines the pure EOp focus on between-type disadvantage with an explicit concern for inequality within the worst-off distribution. The pure EOp criterion is nested as the (k=\infty) case. 

It contrasts this with the more standard Equality of Outcome criterion based on rank-dependent social welfare functions over the overall equivalent-income distribution. The empirical question is then whether EOp leads to less redistributive tax rules than EO. 

This is therefore not a paper about compensating variation, equivalent income in the Fleurbaey sense, or a preference-respecting well-being metric. It is a second-best optimal-tax paper with a Roemer-inspired normative objective. 

# Main findings

First, under the two-parameter affine tax rules, the EOp-optimal policy is very sensitive to the degree of concern for within-type inequality. For (k\ge 3), the optimal rule is a pure universal lump-sum tax; for (k\le 2), the optimal rule instead requires a high marginal tax rate combined with a positive lump-sum transfer. 

Second, the paper finds that concern for equality of opportunity does not mechanically imply high marginal tax rates. Under the pure EOp criterion, or with only moderate concern for within-type inequality, the labour-supply responsiveness of the most disadvantaged type can make a pure lump-sum tax welfare-improving. 

Third, in the three-parameter tax class, the optimal rule changes substantially. For the EOp-Gini case, the optimal rule becomes close to the actual system except for a sizable universal positive transfer. More generally, the ability to differentiate tax rates across brackets reduces the pressure toward extreme affine rules. 

Fourth, when the authors compare EO and EOp, they find that EO is not more redistributive than EOp in this application. On the contrary, EO often selects the universal lump-sum tax, while the extended EOp criterion can require positive transfers and strictly positive marginal tax rates. This is one of the paper’s central substantive conclusions. 

Fifth, when universal lump-sum taxes are ruled out, the optimal rules remain distinct across criteria. Under pure EOp and utilitarian EO, the optimal rule taxes only the first bracket; under more inequality-averse EOp criteria, optimal rules are more progressive and may include positive transfers. 

Sixth, the paper explicitly attributes these results to heterogeneous labour-supply responses across types and across the income distribution. This is not a purely normative result; it depends materially on the behavioural heterogeneity estimated in the microeconometric model. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that the paper’s notion of opportunity is narrow relative to your agenda. Circumstances are proxied only by father’s education, not by richer measures of family background, school quality, social networks, job opportunities, or labour-demand constraints. 

A second limitation is that the welfare object is based on equivalent income only. The authors themselves note that policy prescriptions might change if the value of leisure were included explicitly in individual welfare measurement. 

A third limitation is that the analysis remains second-best within restricted parametric classes of tax-transfer rules. The optimal rule is optimal only within those classes, not globally. 

A fourth limitation, relative to your research interests, is that the paper does not separate preferences from opportunities in the sense of job opportunity sets. Behaviour is constrained in the labour-supply model, but the normative criterion is circumstances-based rather than feasible-set-based. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing a project that wants to argue that optimal tax design depends on the normative interpretation of inequality, not only on elasticities and revenue constraints. It provides a clean contrast between EO and EOp within the same behavioural environment. 

## possible use for model design

It is useful for showing how a structural labour-supply model can be embedded in a second-best tax-design exercise with explicit normative heterogeneity. The treatment of singles and couples, and the computational search over tax rules, are directly relevant design elements. 

## possible use for identification

The paper is useful because it makes clear that the shape of optimal taxation is highly sensitive to heterogeneous labour-supply responses across groups. That supports the use of microeconometric simulation rather than representative-agent shortcuts. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

This is where the paper is strongest for your agenda. It gives a concrete example of a welfare criterion that tries to distinguish inequality due to circumstances from inequality due to effort. Even if you do not adopt Roemer’s framework, it is a major reference for normatively structured tax design. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

Indirectly useful. Although it is Italy-only, it implies that the normative ranking of tax rules may depend sharply on the institutional and behavioural environment. That is a strong caution against transporting tax-design conclusions across countries without re-estimating the behavioural model. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How would the optimal tax rule change if “circumstances” were defined by estimated job opportunity sets rather than by father’s education alone? 

Would the apparent preference of EO for a universal lump-sum tax survive if welfare were measured using income-and-leisure rather than equivalent income alone? 

Can one integrate Roemer-style circumstance sensitivity with a preference-sensitive or opportunity-set-sensitive welfare metric in a structural labour-supply model? 

How much of the paper’s result that EOp can be more redistributive than EO is driven by the particular distribution of labour-supply elasticities across family-background types in Italy 1993? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest simplifying move is the identification of opportunity with parental-education types at the normative stage. That is powerful and operational, but narrow. A future paper could challenge this by replacing type-based “circumstances” with a richer empirical representation of actual opportunity sets, including labour-market constraints, educational quality, and perhaps social-network or regional job-access differences. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

The relation is relevant but indirect. Bargain et al. (2013) studies welfare rankings under heterogeneous labour-supply preferences and alternative welfare metrics, whereas this paper studies optimal income taxation under EO and EOp criteria. Both connect structural labour-supply modelling to normative evaluation, but they locate the normative issue in different places. Bargain et al. emphasizes preference heterogeneity in welfare comparison; Aaberge and Colombino emphasize circumstances versus effort and the design of tax rules. For your purposes, the contrast is useful: the two papers illuminate two different routes from labour-supply estimation to normative analysis. 

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper helps with the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction, but only partially. It treats inequality due to “circumstances” as normatively distinct from inequality due to effort, and it uses a behavioural model with quantity constraints. But it does not model opportunities as explicit job opportunity sets in the welfare criterion, and it does not preserve heterogeneous preferences as the relevant normative object. So it is closer to a circumstance-based fairness framework than to the opportunity-set and preference-separation agenda you are pursuing.
