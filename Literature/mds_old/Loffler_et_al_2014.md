# Full citation

Löffler, Max, Andreas Peichl, and Sebastian Siegloch. 2014. *Structural Labor Supply Models and Wage Exogeneity*. SOEPpapers on Multidisciplinary Panel Data Research, No. 675. Berlin: DIW Berlin / SOEP. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper shows that estimated elasticities from structural discrete-choice labor supply models are not mainly driven by the choice of utility functional form or heterogeneity specification, but are highly sensitive to how wages are treated, especially to the standard exogeneity assumption between wages and preferences. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it opens the “black box” of structural labor supply estimation and asks which modeling assumptions actually drive the elasticities that are then used in welfare analysis and tax design. Its answer is sharp: the dominant issue is wage treatment, not the utility specification that usually receives most of the attention. 

For your potential JMP, this is directly relevant because it warns that estimated labor supply preferences may be partly artifacts of how wages are imputed and how exogeneity is imposed. If your project relies on structural labor supply estimation, especially for welfare or policy analysis, this paper is a methodological checkpoint: it says that before interpreting “preferences,” one must first understand what the wage process is forcing into the preference estimates. 

# Core research question

Which assumptions in state-of-the-art structural discrete-choice labor supply models materially affect estimated labor supply elasticities, and in particular, how important is the usual assumption that wages are exogenous to labor supply preferences and choices? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is primarily methodological, but it studies the issue in empirically relevant tax-benefit environments. The main dataset is Germany, using SOEP data and the German tax-benefit system; robustness checks are conducted using US CPS data and TAXSIM. The motivating context is the large policy importance of labor supply elasticities for welfare analysis and optimal taxation, together with the puzzle that micro elasticities are often much lower than macro ones. 

The paper places itself in the literature on the micro–macro elasticity gap. It notes that recent work often attributes this gap to adjustment costs and frictions, but argues that part of the gap may also be due to how microeconometric structural models are specified, especially with respect to wages. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The paper studies static structural labor supply models of the discrete-choice random-utility type. The agent chooses among a finite set of hours alternatives or jobs, with utility depending on disposable income, leisure, observed characteristics, preferences, labor market conditions, and an unobserved taste term. The baseline choice probabilities are multinomial-logit type, and richer mixed-logit structures arise once unobserved heterogeneity is introduced. 

The key choice object is a labor supply state. For singles, the choice set is seven hours levels: non-participation or 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, or 60 hours per week. Couple households are modeled with joint hours choices; the paper states that fully flexible couples face 72 alternatives. Consumption is static disposable income generated by the tax-benefit function, and leisure is time endowment minus hours worked. 

The paper is purely positive and methodological. It is not a welfare-ranking or optimal-tax paper in itself, although it repeatedly emphasizes that its results matter for those downstream applications because labor supply elasticities are inputs into welfare analysis and optimal taxation. 

# Key objects

The main objects are preference parameters over consumption and leisure, the specification of the utility function, observed and unobserved heterogeneity, fixed costs or hours restrictions, welfare stigma parameters, and above all the wage process. The paper also introduces labor market condition parameters ( \gamma_n ), which can be interpreted as availability or search/friction terms in the general model representation, though these are not the main empirical focus. 

A central methodological object is the wage imputation procedure. The paper distinguishes whether wages are predicted only for non-workers or for the full sample, whether wage prediction errors are ignored, corrected by a single random draw, or integrated out, and whether wages and preferences are estimated jointly. This is the dominant object in the paper’s argument. 

In the second part of the paper, the key additional objects are the covariance terms between the wage equation residual and preferences for consumption and leisure, plus hours-dependent wage effects such as part-time and overtime wage penalties/premia. These covariance terms are what allow the paper to relax the usual wage exogeneity assumption. 

# Data

The main data source is the German Socio-Economic Panel. The paper uses the 2008 SOEP wave, containing labor market information for 2007, and restricts attention to the working-age population while excluding cases such as self-employment, civil servants, military service, and certain complex households for the structural estimation. The paper reports around 24,000 individuals in about 11,000 households in the overall SOEP sample before estimation subsampling. 

The German estimation sample is split into five labor supply groups: 779 single men, 1,065 single women, 688 couples with flexible male labor supply only, 1,042 couples with flexible female labor supply only, and 3,099 couples with two flexible partners. Disposable income for alternatives is computed using IZAΨMOD, or closely approximated by a high-fit polynomial representation of the German tax-benefit system. 

For robustness, the paper also uses IPUMS-CPS data for the US, specifically the March CPS 2007, and computes taxes with TAXSIM. The paper does not present this as a harmonized cross-country structural application; rather, the US sample is used to test whether the methodological sensitivity patterns also appear outside Germany. 

# Identification logic

Identification in the standard structural model comes from variation in hours, wages, non-labor income, household characteristics, and especially the nonlinear tax-benefit function that maps gross earnings into disposable income. Because consumption is a function of hours and wages, and taxes are nonlinear, the model uses tax-induced variation in net incentives to identify labor supply preferences. 

But the paper’s central identification point is that standard models also rely on a highly restrictive extra assumption: wages are independent of preferences and labor market conditions conditional on observables. The paper writes this explicitly as zero conditional correlation between wages and the preference or labor market condition parameters. Its main contribution is to argue that this exogeneity restriction is often taken for granted and can strongly bias estimated elasticities. 

In the flexible joint-estimation model, identification comes from jointly estimating wages and preferences under a multivariate normal structure for wage residuals and preference heterogeneity. For workers, the actual wage residual is observed; for non-workers, the model integrates over the wage equation error distribution. This allows the paper to estimate both hours-dependent wages and covariance between wage residuals and preference heterogeneity. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The first empirical stage is a very large controlled sensitivity analysis. The authors estimate 3,456 plausible structural labor supply model specifications, which across the five labor supply groups produces 17,280 maximum likelihood estimations before dropping non-converged cases; the retained estimation sample contains 16,730 results. These models vary along utility functional form, observed and unobserved heterogeneity, welfare stigma, fixed costs/hours restrictions, wage imputation method, and wage-error treatment. 

The outcome of interest is both statistical fit, measured mainly by AIC, and simulated labor supply elasticities, obtained by increasing own wages and recomputing labor supply. The paper reports both extensive and intensive elasticities, as well as total responses, and examines robustness to 1% versus 10% wage changes and to mean, median, and aggregate elasticity summaries. 

The second empirical stage proposes a joint full-information maximum-likelihood approach, estimated for single women, in which wages and preferences are estimated simultaneously. This model allows hourly wages to depend on hours of work and allows covariance between the wage equation residual and preferences for consumption and leisure. It uses Halton draws to approximate the mixed-logit likelihood. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled over consumption and leisure in standard discrete-choice fashion, with alternative specifications including translog, quadratic, and Box–Cox functional forms. Observed heterogeneity enters through interactions of preferences with characteristics such as age, children, handicap, and similar variables. Unobserved heterogeneity is introduced using random coefficients or correlated random components in the richer models. 

The paper’s key point is that estimated preferences are not robustly interpretable if wages are treated too mechanically. It argues that many standard models effectively impose independence between wages and preferences, thereby attributing too much of observed behavior to preferences after stripping away wage variation in a restrictive way. In its preferred model, the paper finds significant covariance between wage residuals and preferences for both consumption and leisure. 

This is not a preference-respecting welfare paper. Preferences are behavioral objects, and the contribution is econometric: to show how elasticities and estimated preferences depend on wage assumptions. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is especially important for your agenda because the paper is simultaneously relevant and limited.

The paper does not model opportunities explicitly as latent jobs, RURO opportunity sets, or demand-side job distributions. The general theoretical setup allows for labor market condition terms ( \gamma_n ) and choice-availability terms ( g(j) ), and the survey of the literature discusses fixed costs, hours restrictions, and the Aaberge–Dagsvik style interpretation of restrictions as job availability. But the paper’s own empirical core is not an opportunity-set analysis. 

Instead, the main omitted-variable problem the paper tackles is wage structure, not opportunity structure. In other words, it is concerned that standard structural labor supply models may confound wages and preferences, not that they confound opportunities and preferences in the stronger RURO sense. Some estimated models in the sensitivity analysis include fixed costs or part-time restrictions, but these are auxiliary structural features, not first-class opportunity objects. 

Relative to your research direction, this matters a great deal. The paper helps separate preferences from wages, and to a lesser extent from hours restrictions, but it does not separate preferences from individual opportunity sets in a rigorous latent-job sense. So it is highly relevant for identification and specification, but only indirectly relevant for an opportunities-versus-preferences project. 

# Welfare / normative object

None. The paper does not construct equivalent income, compensating variation, or a social welfare function. It is positive and methodological. 

Its normative relevance is indirect but substantial. Because labor supply elasticities are inputs into tax policy design, errors in estimating them can distort conclusions about optimal tax rates and welfare effects. The paper illustrates this directly with a Diamond–Saez style optimal top tax calculation. 

# Main findings

First, most common modeling choices do not systematically drive estimated labor supply elasticities. In particular, the paper finds that functional form choices and the inclusion of observed or unobserved heterogeneity affect statistical fit but do not generate large systematic changes in elasticities. This is one of the central messages of the large-scale sensitivity exercise. 

Second, the treatment of wages is decisive. The paper finds that choices such as predicting wages for the full sample versus only for non-workers, and the way wage prediction errors are handled, can drastically change elasticities. It explicitly states that some common wage-imputation choices may more than double estimated labor supply elasticities. 

Third, the paper finds significant correlation between wages and hours. In the preferred joint-estimation model for single women, part-time work lowers the hourly wage rate by about 7% relative to full-time jobs, while overtime also tends to reduce hourly wages, though less robustly. This supports the view that the fixed-wage assumption across hours choices is too restrictive. 

Fourth, the paper finds strong correlation between wages and preferences. In the preferred specification, the correlation between wages and preferences for consumption is negative and the correlation between wages and preferences for leisure is positive. The paper interprets this as evidence that the standard exogeneity assumption is violated. Table 5 on pages 25–26 reports these correlation estimates and their significance. 

Fifth, loosening the exogeneity assumptions substantially raises labor supply elasticities. The paper states that the preferred model yields total labor supply elasticities for single women of roughly 0.6 instead of about 0.25 in the conventional restrictive model. Table 5 shows aggregated total 10% elasticities rising from 0.201 in the standard model to 0.537 in the most flexible joint model, with mean total elasticities rising from 0.222 to 0.631. 

Sixth, the paper draws a direct policy implication: higher elasticities materially lower the implied optimal top tax rate in a Diamond–Saez calculation. It reports that moving from an elasticity near 0.25 to one near 0.6 lowers the illustrative optimal top marginal tax rate from 72.7% to 52.6%. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is that the paper is primarily methodological rather than a substantive application to one policy setting. It tells you how structural labor supply models behave under different assumptions, but it does not itself provide a richer behavioral model of job choice, opportunities, or welfare. 

A second limitation, relative to your interests, is that opportunities are not modeled as first-class objects. The paper does not estimate latent job sets, opportunity densities, or demand-side availability constraints. It is mainly about wage exogeneity and preference estimation. 

A third limitation is that the most flexible joint-estimation exercise is presented only for single women. This is reasonable computationally, but it means the headline covariance results are not shown symmetrically for all household types. 

A fourth limitation is that the stronger model relies on distributional assumptions, especially multivariate normality in the joint distribution of wage residuals and preference heterogeneity. This is a substantive identifying assumption, not a purely technical detail. 

A fifth limitation is outlet status. This is a working paper in the SOEPpapers series; the front matter explicitly notes that the series has no external referee process and papers appear as works in progress. That does not negate the contribution, but it does mean the paper should be treated as provisional. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is highly useful for framing a JMP that relies on structural labor supply estimation and wants to be explicit about identification and modeling discipline. It gives you a strong methodological warning: before discussing welfare, elasticities, or opportunity sets, make sure your wage process is not smuggling specification error into preferences. 

## possible use for model design

It is useful for model design because it suggests that wages should not be handled as an innocuous first-step object. If your model will estimate preferences structurally, especially with discrete choice, this paper pushes you toward either joint estimation or at least systematic robustness checks on wage imputation and wage-hours dependence. 

## possible use for identification

This is where the paper is strongest. It shows that labor supply elasticities are highly sensitive to wage exogeneity assumptions, and that a flexible joint model can be used to test those assumptions directly. That is a direct lesson for any project that wants to interpret estimated preferences substantively. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

Indirectly very useful. Since elasticities are key inputs into welfare and tax analysis, the paper implies that welfare conclusions may be fragile if they are built on poorly specified wage treatment. It is a warning not to take structurally estimated elasticities at face value. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

The paper gives some support for cross-country robustness, since its qualitative sensitivity findings are reported for both German SOEP and US CPS data. That makes it useful for your cross-country agenda as a reminder that comparable structural models can still be driven by wage treatment choices rather than by genuine institutional or behavioral differences. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How much of the apparent cross-country variation in structural labor supply elasticities is due to different wage-imputation conventions rather than true behavioral differences?

If wages and preferences are jointly estimated in a RURO or latent-job model, do opportunity-sensitive elasticities move even further away from standard micro estimates?

How much of what structural labor supply papers interpret as preference heterogeneity is actually compensation for misspecified wage-hours relationships?

Would welfare comparisons based on structural labor supply models change materially once one relaxes wage exogeneity and jointly estimates wages and preferences? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest challenge is that the paper identifies a major specification problem but stops one level short of a full opportunity-sensitive structural model. It shows that standard labor supply models may confound wages and preferences, but it does not ask whether they also confound wages, preferences, and opportunity sets jointly. A future paper could extend its logic by estimating wages, preferences, and job opportunities simultaneously rather than treating only the wage side as the omitted structure.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper helps prevent one important conflation—between wages and preferences—but it does not fully address the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction. Its main message is that standard structural labor supply models may attribute too much to preferences because wages are treated too restrictively. That is very relevant for your agenda, but it is only a partial solution: the feasible opportunity set still remains largely in the background. So the paper is best seen as a methodological complement to, not a substitute for, a serious opportunities-versus-preferences framework. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The central exogeneity restriction challenged by the paper is:
[
\mathrm{Corr}(\beta_n, w_{nj}\mid x_{nj}) = 0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\mathrm{Corr}(\gamma_n, w_{nj}\mid x_{nj}) = 0,
]
which the paper argues is both common and highly restrictive. This is the single most important formal statement for your JMP because it clarifies what standard structural models are implicitly assuming. 

The paper’s general choice probability is
[
P(U_{ni} > U_{nj}, \forall j\neq i \mid x_n,\beta_n,\gamma_n)
=============================================================

\frac{\exp(v(C_{ni},L_i\mid x_{ni},\beta_n)),g(i\mid x_{ni},\gamma_n)}
{\sum_{s\in J_n}\exp(v(C_{ns},L_s\mid x_{ns},\beta_n)),g(s\mid x_{ns},\gamma_n)},
]
which is useful because it makes explicit that utility and choice availability are conceptually separable in the general framework. 

A concise numerical takeaway from Table 5 is that, for single women, aggregated total 10% own-wage elasticities rise from 0.201 in the conventional model to 0.537 in the most flexible joint-estimation model, while mean total elasticities rise from 0.222 to 0.631. This is the paper’s most policy-relevant quantitative result.

# My quick takeaway

If I only remember one thing from this paper for my JMP, what should it be? That in structural labor supply models, the biggest hidden specification issue may not be the utility function at all, but the wage process: once wages and preferences are allowed to interact rather than being forced apart, estimated labor supply elasticities become much larger and the usual policy conclusions can change sharply.
