# Full citation

Aaberge, R., U. Colombino, and T. Wennemo. 2009. “Evaluating Alternative Representations of the Choice Sets in Models of Labor Supply.” *Journal of Economic Surveys* 23(3): 586–612. DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-6419.2008.00573.x. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper shows, through simulation, that how a discrete labor supply model represents the choice set—fixed versus sampled alternatives, and homogeneous versus heterogeneous availability of hours—matters little for in-sample fit but can matter substantially for out-of-sample policy prediction. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it targets a neglected but fundamental issue in discrete-choice labor supply models: the representation of the feasible set itself. Much of the literature estimates preferences while taking the choice set as mechanically given, yet this paper argues that the way available jobs/hours are represented is part of the constraint side of the problem and can materially affect policy evaluation. 

For your potential JMP, this paper is especially valuable because it is one of the clearest bridges between standard structural labor supply and an opportunity-sensitive perspective. It does not build a full RURO/latent-jobs framework, but it explicitly introduces heterogeneous opportunity density (p(h)) and studies how misrepresenting availability can bias reform simulation. That is directly aligned with your interest in distinguishing preferences from opportunities. 

# Core research question

How do alternative ways of representing the choice set in discrete labor supply models affect estimation and, especially, prediction performance—both when constructing the set of alternatives and when modeling the availability of different hours opportunities? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is methodological rather than a substantive country-comparison study. Its empirical base is a previously estimated model of married/cohabiting female labor supply in Norway, used as the “true” data-generating model in Monte Carlo and large-sample simulation exercises. The broader context is the literature on structural labor supply under nonlinear and nonconvex budget constraints generated by taxes and welfare programs. 

The authors situate the paper within the shift from older Kuhn–Tucker/Hausman-style approaches toward random utility maximization (RUM) models. Their concern is not whether discrete-choice labor supply is useful—it is—but whether the literature has been too casual about how the feasible set is approximated and whether all hours/jobs are treated as equally available. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a static random utility / discrete-choice labor supply model. Individuals choose among job opportunities characterized primarily by hours of work and associated disposable income, with utility written as
[
U(f(wh,I),h,j)=v(f(wh,I),h)+\varepsilon(j),
]
where (w) is the wage, (h) hours, (I) exogenous income, (f) the tax-transfer rule, (j) other job or individual attributes unobserved by the analyst, and (\varepsilon) a random term. 

The key theoretical point is that the choice probability should depend not only on systematic utility but also on the density of available opportunities. In the continuous formulation,
[
\phi(h)=
\frac{\exp(v(f(wh,I),h)),p(h)}
{\int \exp(v(f(wx,I),x)),p(x),dx},
]
so (p(h)) is an explicit opportunity density. This is the central mechanism by which the paper introduces heterogeneous availability of alternatives. 

The framework is purely positive and simulation-based. It is designed to assess prediction performance of alternative representations of the feasible set, especially for policy reform simulation. It is not a welfare-ranking or normative paper. 

# Key objects

The main objects are the utility function (v(f(wh,I),h)), the opportunity density (p(h)), and the choice probability (\phi(h)). The core modeling distinction is between utility/preference parameters and parameters governing the density of opportunities. This separation is conceptually central. 

A second set of key objects concerns the representation of the choice set itself: fixed alternatives versus sampled alternatives; 6 versus 24 alternatives; inclusion or exclusion of a job dummy; inclusion or exclusion of “peaks” dummies meant to capture excess availability of part-time and full-time jobs. The paper studies 16 alternative models generated by combinations of these design choices. 

A third key object is prediction performance. The authors compare models by their relative prediction error for participation, hours, and disposable income, both under the current tax regime and after a hypothetical revenue-constant flat-tax reform. 

# Data

The underlying empirical model is estimated on 1,842 married/cohabiting females from the 1995 Norwegian Survey of Level of Living. Women are restricted to ages 20–62. Other household members’ behavior is treated as exogenous. The model focuses on female labor supply, with household disposable income including spouse income and other nonlabor income. 

The main results of the paper, however, come from simulated data. The authors use the estimated Norwegian model as the “true” model, draw simulated samples from it, estimate alternative models on those samples, and then compare their in-sample and out-of-sample predictions against the “true” model. The benchmark tax regime is the 1994 system, and the main out-of-sample counterfactual is a revenue-constant flat tax. 

# Identification logic

Identification in the paper is not about causal policy effects from raw data; it is about whether different representations of the feasible set recover or approximate the underlying structural environment. The key identifying idea is that in a RUM framework, preferences and constraints can be separated, and the choice set can be explicitly represented through (p(h)), the density of available opportunities. 

A major analytical point is that fixed-alternative methods can be interpreted as aggregation procedures. The paper shows that such aggregation introduces approximation error and, in general, biased estimates unless omitted correction terms happen to cancel out. By contrast, sampling alternatives from an appropriate sampling density and adjusting the choice probability accordingly preserves consistency, following McFadden’s sampling-of-alternatives results. 

So the paper’s identification message is concrete: misrepresenting the feasible set may not destroy in-sample fit, but it can distort the recovered model enough to damage reform prediction. That is the practical identification failure the paper is documenting. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper first estimates a “true” structural labor supply model with heterogeneous opportunity density. The deterministic utility function is specified flexibly in disposable income and leisure, with leisure preferences shifted by age and children. Opportunity density is modeled so that market jobs and, within them, part-time and full-time peaks can be more likely than other hours values. 

The estimated “true” model is then used to generate 30 Monte Carlo samples. On each sample, the authors estimate 16 alternative models varying four features: whether alternatives are fixed or sampled, whether there are 6 or 24 alternatives, whether a job dummy is included, and whether peaks dummies are included. They then compare predicted participation, hours, and disposable income with the “true” values. 

A second exercise uses a large simulated sample to isolate systematic rather than sampling-driven differences. Prediction-error summaries are regressed on indicators for sampled alternatives, number of alternatives, and inclusion of job/peaks dummies. The focus is explicitly on current-regime fit versus post-reform prediction under a flat tax. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled structurally through a random-utility specification over disposable income and leisure. The utility function is flexible and allows heterogeneity through age and children. The paper is explicit that the discrete-choice approach is attractive because it allows relatively flexible utility specifications without the computational restrictions of older continuous approaches. 

At the same time, the paper’s central point is that preference estimation should not absorb what properly belongs to the constraint/opportunity side. In effect, it argues that labor supply models should estimate preferences conditional on a realistic representation of the opportunity set, not conditional on a mechanically uniform hours grid. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is the crucial section for your agenda.

The paper treats opportunities much more explicitly than most of the standard discrete labor supply literature. Instead of assuming that all hours values within a range are equally available to everyone, it allows the density of opportunities (p(h)) to vary across hours and individuals. In the “true” model, offered hours are more concentrated around part-time and full-time peaks, and there is an explicit distinction between market and non-market opportunities. 

More precisely, the opportunity density is modeled with a job dummy and two “peaks” dummies, so that part-time and full-time jobs can be more frequent than other hours bundles. This is not yet a full latent-jobs model with wages, occupations, and job characteristics jointly distributed, but it is clearly an opportunity-set model in the narrower sense of heterogeneous hours availability. 

This makes the paper highly relevant for separating preferences from opportunities. It says directly that not all hour opportunities are equally available to everyone, and it studies the consequences of ignoring that fact. The main limitation is that the opportunity side is one-dimensional in hours. Jobs are not modeled as rich objects with wage-hours-characteristics triples, so the framework remains a partial precursor to RURO rather than a full opportunity-set model. 

# Welfare / normative object

None. The paper does not evaluate welfare in equivalent-income, money-metric, SWF, or fairness terms. It is a positive methodological paper about structural prediction and policy simulation. 

There is, however, a strong policy-evaluation orientation. The authors explicitly argue that the relevant standard for judging labor supply models is not merely their ability to fit observed data, but their ability to predict the outcomes of policy changes. That is a policy-analytic rather than normative criterion. 

# Main findings

First, the way the choice set is represented has little impact on fitting current observed values, but it has a much more important effect on out-of-sample prediction performance. This is the headline result of the paper. 

Second, models based on sampled alternatives generally perform better than models based on fixed alternatives. The paper emphasizes that sampled-alternative procedures retain consistency properties that fixed-alternative aggregation procedures do not generally possess. 

Third, accounting for heterogeneous opportunity availability—through a job dummy and peaks dummies—improves prediction, especially in reform simulations. The evidence is especially clear for out-of-sample predictions of hours worked and disposable income after a flat-tax reform. 

Fourth, the tails of the distribution are hardest to predict when the choice set is simplified too aggressively. Lower and upper deciles show poorer precision, which the authors interpret as evidence that simplification particularly harms prediction in the tails. 

Fifth, in-sample fit is a weak model-selection criterion. Many models reproduce current outcomes reasonably well, but they differ substantially in post-reform performance. The authors state this explicitly in the conclusion. 

# Main limitations

The paper is a simulation study built on one underlying “true” model estimated from Norwegian married females. Its quantitative conclusions are therefore likely to be application-specific rather than universal. The authors themselves state this. 

The opportunity model is richer than in much of the literature, but still limited. Availability varies only over hours categories and participation status, with peaks around part-time and full-time jobs. The model does not represent richer job attributes, wage-offer distributions, occupations, or multidimensional job sets. 

The empirical setup is also narrow in demographic scope: married/cohabiting women only, with spouse behavior treated as exogenous. That is reasonable for the exercise, but it limits external validity. 

Finally, the paper is not a direct empirical test of real-world policy reforms. It evaluates out-of-sample prediction against a model-generated “truth,” not against realized post-reform data. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is excellent for framing an argument that the feasible set is not innocuous in structural labor supply. If your project argues that opportunities must be modeled rather than assumed, this paper provides a precise methodological precedent inside the labor supply literature itself. 

## possible use for model design

It suggests a concrete path from standard discrete-choice labor supply toward opportunity-sensitive models: introduce an explicit density of opportunities, allow heterogeneity in availability across hours, and distinguish preference parameters from opportunity parameters. Even if your eventual model is richer than hours-only, this paper offers a clean intermediate design. 

## possible use for identification

The paper is useful as a warning that in-sample fit does not validate a model’s treatment of opportunities. A model can replicate observed hours and income reasonably well and still be unreliable for policy counterfactuals because it misrepresents the choice set. This is an identification lesson, not just a simulation lesson. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

Indirectly, it is very relevant for welfare work. Any welfare comparison based on a structural labor supply model inherits the model’s representation of the feasible set. If the choice set is misspecified, welfare conclusions after reforms may also be distorted. This is especially relevant for your cross-country or opportunity-sensitive welfare agenda. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

The paper is not itself cross-country, but it is highly relevant for cross-country work because differences in hours availability, job density, and work opportunities are likely to be institutional and country-specific. The paper implies that such differences should not be ignored when comparing preferences, elasticities, or welfare across countries. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How much of the estimated cross-country heterogeneity in labor supply preferences disappears once country-specific opportunity densities over hours or jobs are modeled explicitly? 

Can the one-dimensional opportunity density (p(h)) used here be generalized to a multidimensional job-offer density over wages, hours, and job characteristics in a RURO framework? 

When welfare comparisons are constructed from structural labor supply models, how sensitive are they to the representation of the feasible set rather than to the utility specification alone? 

Do richer opportunity-set models improve real-world out-of-sample policy prediction, not only model-to-model prediction in simulation environments? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest simplification is that opportunities are modeled only through the distribution of hours, with special mass around part-time and full-time jobs. That is an important step beyond uniform availability, but it still leaves wages, occupations, job amenities, and other dimensions of job heterogeneity outside the opportunity set. A future paper could challenge this by moving from heterogeneous hours availability to a full latent-jobs opportunity model. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

This paper is methodologically upstream of Bargain et al. (2013). Bargain et al. (2013) uses discrete-choice labor supply models to recover consumption-leisure preferences and then build welfare rankings across countries, but it also acknowledges that demand-side restrictions are not explicitly modeled. Aaberge et al. (2009) provides a direct demonstration that the representation of the choice set and opportunity availability can matter for policy simulation, which makes it an important methodological caution for later welfare analyses built on standard discrete-choice labor supply models. 

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper directly helps separate preferences from opportunities. It states clearly that one must estimate preferences separately from constraints and that the density of available opportunities should appear explicitly in the choice probability rather than being silently absorbed into utility. In that sense, it is one of the most relevant papers in your corpus for the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction. Its limitation is only that the opportunity side remains relatively narrow, centered on hours availability rather than full job opportunity sets. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The central formula is
[
\phi(h)=
\frac{\exp(v(f(wh,I),h)),p(h)}
{\int \exp(v(f(wx,I),x)),p(x),dx},
]
which shows that the probability of choosing hours (h) depends on both utility and the density of opportunities (p(h)). This is the most important formula in the paper for your agenda. 

A second important result is the sampled-alternatives correction:
[
\phi(h\mid S)=
\frac{\exp(v(f(wh,I),h)-\ln q(h))}
{\sum_{x\in S}\exp(v(f(wx,I),x)-\ln q(x))},
]
which is the basis for consistent estimation when the full choice set is approximated by a sampled subset. 

A short quotation that captures the paper’s core message is that “the way the choice set is represented has little impact on the fitting of observed values, but a more significant and important impact on the out-of-sample prediction performance.”
