# Full citation

Dagsvik, John K., and Zhiyang Jia. 2016. “Labor Supply as a Choice Among Latent Jobs: Unobserved Heterogeneity and Identification.” *Journal of Applied Econometrics* 31(3): 487–506. DOI: 10.1002/jae.2428. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper develops and studies a latent-job structural labor supply model with explicit choice constraints and non-pecuniary job attributes, and its main contribution is to characterize identification under cross-sectional data and different assumptions on wage-offer heterogeneity. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it goes directly at a core weakness of standard labor supply models: they typically treat labor supply as choice over income-leisure bundles or over a finite hours grid, rather than as choice among actual jobs with fixed hours, wages, and latent qualitative attributes. Dagsvik and Jia argue that this misses essential labor-market structure and can blur the distinction between preferences and constraints. 

For your JMP, this is one of the most relevant papers in the corpus. It is very close to your agenda on latent jobs, opportunity sets, and the separation of preferences from opportunities. It is also unusually important because it is not just an application; it is an identification paper. That means it tells you what is and is not recoverable from standard cross-sectional data once the opportunity set is modeled explicitly. 

# Core research question

Under a labor supply model in which workers choose among latent job opportunities with fixed wages, fixed hours, and non-pecuniary attributes, what parts of preferences and opportunity distributions are identified from conventional cross-sectional microdata, and what empirical implications follow in an application to Norwegian couples? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is primarily methodological. Its empirical application uses Norwegian microdata for married/cohabiting couples, but the broader context is the critique of traditional continuous and standard discrete-choice labor supply models. The authors argue that actual labor markets involve job-specific constraints and that workers choose jobs, not abstract hours-income points. 

Institutionally, the paper pays particular attention to restrictions on hours of work and the prevalence of part-time and full-time peaks. In the Norwegian application, this is especially relevant for married women, many of whom are observed in part-time jobs, particularly in public-sector activities such as health care. The simulation exercise later in the paper explicitly interprets a reform as a change in the opportunity distribution of offered hours, not a change in preferences. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model class is a static structural labor supply model with latent jobs. It is a job-choice framework, and substantively it is very close to a RURO / random utility-random opportunities logic even though the paper does not foreground the RURO label. Labor supply is generated by choice among latent job “packages,” each characterized by offered hours, offered wage, and latent non-pecuniary job attributes. 

The agent chooses a job (z). For market jobs, hours (H(z)) and wage (W(z)) are fixed by the job. Utility has multiplicative form
[
U(C,h,z)=v(C,h)\varepsilon(z),
]
where (v(C,h)) is the deterministic utility component and (\varepsilon(z)) is a job-specific taste shifter capturing unobserved non-pecuniary attributes. Consumption is determined by the tax-benefit map (C=f(wh,I)), with non-labor income (I). 

The feasible set is worker-specific and unobserved by the researcher. That is the conceptual core of the paper. The model introduces an opportunity measure over jobs, summarized by job availability (\theta), an hours-offer distribution (g_1(h)), and a wage-offer distribution (g_2(w\mid h)). This makes the framework explicitly about both preferences and opportunity constraints. 

The framework is purely positive in this paper. There is no welfare metric, no equivalent income, and no social welfare function. The paper’s objective is behavioral modeling, identification, and policy simulation of labor supply under changes in taxes or opportunity distributions. 

# Key objects

The central economic object is the latent job opportunity. A job is characterized by fixed hours, a wage offer, and latent non-pecuniary attributes. This means the true choice object is a job package, not a continuous hours decision. 

The main structural objects are the deterministic utility function (v(C,h)), the taste shifters (\varepsilon(z)), the job availability measure (\theta), the opportunity density over offered hours (g_1(h)), and the conditional opportunity density of offered wages (g_2(w\mid h)). In the extension with worker-level wage heterogeneity, the paper also introduces a random effect (\eta) in the offered wage distribution. 

The main estimated/identified objects are therefore preference parameters, opportunity-distribution parameters, wage-offer equations, and labor supply elasticities. The empirical comparison focuses on two model versions: one where wage dispersion comes only from across-job variation, and one where it comes only from inter-individual variation. 

# Data

The empirical application uses Norwegian Labor Survey microdata from 1997. The sample consists of married/cohabiting couples. Table I on page 10 reports 2,254 households where both spouses work, 256 where only the husband works, and 5 where only the wife works. The summary statistics also show very high male participation and much more variation on the female side. 

The main observed variables are spouses’ age, education, potential experience, non-labor income, wage rates, weekly hours of work, and number of children in different age groups. The authors note a key data limitation: only weekly hours are observed, not annual weeks worked or overtime, which creates measurement error and division-bias problems in the observed wage-hours relationship. 

The data are conventional cross-sectional survey data. This matters for the paper’s main argument, because the identification results are developed precisely for the case where only cross-sectional microdata are available. 

# Identification logic

This is the heart of the paper.

The core identification problem is that observed labor supply is jointly determined by preferences and latent opportunity constraints. Standard identification results for multinomial or mixed logit do not directly apply because the model contains both a preference representation and an opportunity measure. The paper is explicit that, in general, the model is not nonparametrically identified from cross-sectional data alone. 

With non-labor income entering only through consumption, the authors show that utility can be recovered only up to an unknown multiplicative structure, not fully. Even when non-labor income shifts consumption while holding hours and wages fixed, the opportunity measure and utility are not separately identified in general. 

A stronger result is obtained when offered wages and offered hours are assumed independent. In that case, the offered-hours distribution can be identified, and utility is identified up to a multiplicative factor depending only on hours. Full identification then requires additional parametric structure, including a generalized Box–Cox specification for utility and extra restrictions on the opportunity distribution. 

The paper further extends the identification analysis to the case where wage offers depend on unobserved individual heterogeneity through a random effect (\eta). Full identification in that setting requires an exclusion variable (X) that shifts wage offers but does not affect preferences. This is conceptually important for your agenda because it shows exactly what additional structure is needed once one wants both latent opportunities and worker-level unobserved wage heterogeneity. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The empirical strategy compares two structurally distinct wage-opportunity specifications under the maintained latent-job framework. Model 1 assumes that observable identical individuals face the same offered-wage distribution across jobs, so wage heterogeneity is across jobs. Model 2 assumes instead that each individual faces one worker-specific wage level, so wage heterogeneity is across individuals. 

Because of measurement error in observed hours and the resulting spurious negative correlation between hours and wages, the authors adopt a three-stage estimation procedure from earlier work when offered wages and hours are independent. First, they estimate participation; second, wage equations controlling for selection; third, the labor supply model by maximum likelihood using predicted wages and integrating over the relevant error terms. 

The utility specification is generalized Box–Cox. For each spouse, feasible annual hours are discretized into eight values: 0, 208, 624, 1040, 1456, 1950, 2340, and 2600. The opportunity distribution over hours is specified as uniform except for peaks at part-time and full-time hours. Page 10 and the surrounding discussion make clear that these peaks are intended to capture institutional restrictions on offered hours. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are modeled structurally through the deterministic utility function (v(C,h)) and the job-specific taste shifters (\varepsilon(z)). The latter capture unobserved non-pecuniary job attributes and latent heterogeneity in tastes across opportunities. This is a richer preference representation than the standard labor-supply model because agents care about jobs, not only income and leisure. 

At the same time, the paper is very careful not to attribute all observed heterogeneity to preferences. That is precisely the reason it introduces the opportunity measure. The identification analysis is built around the fact that observed behavior confounds tastes and opportunities unless the latter are modeled explicitly. 

The paper does not develop normative preference heterogeneity, random coefficients in the modern mixed-logit sense, or preference-respecting welfare evaluation. Its contribution is structural-positive rather than welfare-theoretic. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is the most important section for your agenda.

The paper models opportunities explicitly and centrally. The opportunity set is worker-specific, unobserved, and composed of latent job packages. Jobs differ in offered hours, offered wages, and non-pecuniary attributes. Hours restrictions are therefore not treated as an afterthought or as an ad hoc fixed-cost term; they are built into the opportunity side of the model. 

The opportunity structure is summarized through (\theta g_1(h)g_2(w\mid h)), or through its extension with a random effect in wage offers. The model thus distinguishes between job availability, the distribution of offered hours, and the distribution of offered wages. This is much closer to your preferred latent-jobs perspective than standard discrete-hours labor supply. 

The paper is also explicit that changes in institutional restrictions can be modeled as changes in the opportunity distribution rather than as preference changes. The simulation on pages 15–16 does exactly that: it removes the part-time peak in women’s opportunities and shifts mass toward full-time jobs while holding the total number of jobs fixed. Figure 3 on page 16 shows the corresponding predicted decline in part-time work and rise in full-time work for married women. 

If there is one limitation relative to your broader agenda, it is that jobs are still relatively sparse objects: mainly hours, wages, and latent non-pecuniary attributes. There is no richer observed occupation/task/amenity vector and no explicit equilibrium labor-demand side. But this is still one of the closest papers in your set to a true latent-job opportunity model. 

# Welfare / normative object

None. The paper does not construct a welfare metric, social welfare function, compensating variation, or equivalent income object. It is purely positive, though obviously relevant for downstream welfare and tax analysis because it changes what one means by a labor-supply response. 

Its policy relevance is indirect but substantial. By showing how tax reforms and changes in hours restrictions can be simulated in a model with latent jobs, it changes the behavioral foundation on which welfare or optimal-tax analysis would later rest. 

# Main findings

First, the paper shows that the latent-job model is generally not nonparametrically identified from conventional cross-sectional data. This is the main theoretical finding. Preferences and opportunity constraints cannot in general be separately recovered without additional structure. 

Second, under stronger assumptions such as independence between offered hours and offered wages, plus further parametric restrictions, the model becomes identified. The paper provides formal theorems for these cases, including the extension with worker-level unobserved wage heterogeneity. 

Third, in the Norwegian application, both model variants fit the data reasonably well, but Model 2 fits better than Model 1. Figure 1 on page 13 and the Andrews chi-square tests reported in the text show that the version with purely inter-individual wage heterogeneity outperforms the version with only across-job wage heterogeneity. 

Fourth, estimated gross-wage elasticities are moderate for married women and very small for married men. Table II on page 14 reports, for Model 2, a women’s own-wage elasticity of participation of about 0.333 and an unconditional hours elasticity of about 0.618, while male responses are near zero. 

Fifth, the model implies substantial nonlinearity in labor supply responses. Figure 2 on page 15 shows that expected annual hours of work for married women rise with their wage, but the slope declines at higher wage levels. This is an important reminder that constant elasticity summaries mask strong curvature. 

Sixth, the simulation of a change in women’s opportunity distribution—removing the part-time peak and increasing the full-time peak—shifts women significantly from part-time toward full-time work, with little change for men. This is a direct demonstration that the model can simulate policy-relevant changes in constraints, not only tax changes. 

# Main limitations

A first limitation is the data. The authors explicitly state that their hours measure contains nontrivial measurement error because annual weeks and overtime are not observed. This creates a division-bias problem in the wage-hours relationship and complicates empirical discrimination between wage-offer structures. 

A second limitation is that the opportunity structure, although explicit, remains stylized. Jobs are defined mainly by wages, hours, and latent non-pecuniary attributes. The model does not include a richer observed vector of occupation, task content, or amenities. 

A third limitation is that labor demand is not modeled as an equilibrium object. Policy simulations that alter the opportunity distribution are partial-equilibrium exercises conditional on fixed job availability measures. 

A fourth limitation is that the empirical application is narrow in population scope: married/cohabiting couples in Norway. The identification results are general, but the quantitative findings are application-specific. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This is a core framing paper if your JMP argues that labor supply should be modeled as job choice under latent opportunities rather than as hours choice under a common feasible set. It gives that claim both conceptual force and formal identification content. 

## possible use for model design

It is directly useful for model design. The paper gives you a concrete structural architecture: utility over consumption and hours combined with job-specific taste shifters, plus an explicit opportunity density over hours and wage offers. This is one of the best methodological templates for a latent-jobs labor-supply model. 

## possible use for identification

This is where the paper is indispensable. It shows exactly what cross-sectional data can and cannot identify, and what additional exclusions or parametric restrictions are needed. For your work, that means you should not treat the opportunity side as automatically recoverable from behavior alone. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

Indirectly, it is highly relevant. Any welfare or optimal-tax analysis built on labor supply behavior will inherit the behavioral interpretation of constraints. If the choice set is latent and heterogeneous, welfare analysis that ignores this may misread opportunity deprivation as preference heterogeneity. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

The paper is single-country, but it suggests a strong research path for cross-country comparison: estimate country-specific latent opportunity structures and ask whether apparent preference differences are really differences in offered jobs, hours restrictions, and wage opportunities. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How much of the cross-country variation in estimated labor supply elasticities disappears once each country’s latent job opportunity distribution is estimated explicitly rather than imposed through a common hours grid? 

Can one extend the latent-job framework from wages and hours to a richer opportunity density over wages, hours, occupations, and job-quality characteristics while preserving identification with realistic data? 

How much of what structural labor-supply papers interpret as preference heterogeneity is actually attributable to heterogeneous opportunity densities or hours restrictions? 

Can a preference-sensitive welfare metric be combined with this latent-job model without conflating willingness to work with job availability? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest remaining simplification is that the opportunity side, although explicit, is still relatively low-dimensional and partial-equilibrium. The model convincingly shows that labor supply should be viewed as job choice among latent opportunities, but the jobs are summarized mainly by hours and wages, and the opportunity distribution is not endogenized through labor demand or richer observed job characteristics. A future paper could challenge this by building a higher-dimensional latent-job space and linking it more tightly to market structure. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

The relation is methodological rather than direct. Bargain et al. (2013) uses a structural labor supply framework to derive welfare rankings under heterogeneous consumption-leisure preferences, but it does not model latent job opportunities in the way Dagsvik and Jia do here. Relative to Bargain et al. (2013), this paper is much stronger on explicit opportunity modeling and on the identification of constrained choice, but it does not itself proceed to welfare ranking. For your corpus, the two papers are complementary: Bargain et al. shows what one can do normatively with estimated preferences, while Dagsvik and Jia shows what behavioral structure is needed if one wants to avoid conflating preferences with opportunities. 

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper is central to the opportunities-versus-preferences distinction. Its whole point is that observed labor supply reflects both utility and latent choice constraints, and that the latter should be modeled structurally rather than buried inside preferences or optimization error. It therefore directly supports your agenda. The only respect in which it falls short is that it does not yet connect that positive distinction to a welfare criterion that separately values opportunity sets. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The core utility representation is
[
U(C,h,z)=v(C,h)\varepsilon(z),
]
where (z) indexes a latent job and (\varepsilon(z)) captures unobserved non-pecuniary job attributes. This is the paper’s key formal departure from standard labor supply. 

The main choice density is
[
\phi(h,w\mid I)=
\frac{v(f(hw),I,h),\theta g_1(h)g_2(w\mid h)}
{v(f(0),I,0)+\theta\sum_{r\in D}\int_0^\infty v(f(ry),I,r)g_1(r)g_2(y\mid r),dy},
]
which makes observed labor supply a joint product of preferences and opportunity densities. 

A concise statement of the paper’s main message is on page 16: standard identification results do not apply because the model contains “both preferences and latent choice constraints (opportunity measure).”
