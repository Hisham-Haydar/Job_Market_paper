# Full citation

Aaberge, Rolf, and Ugo Colombino. 2013. “Using a Microeconometric Model of Household Labour Supply to Design Optimal Income Taxes.” *The Scandinavian Journal of Economics* 115(2): 449–475. DOI: 10.1111/sjoe.12015. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper uses a structurally estimated household labor supply model with heterogeneous preferences and opportunity sets to compute optimal piecewise-linear income tax schedules for Norway by direct simulation rather than by plugging elasticities into analytical optimal-tax formulas. 

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because it operationalizes a fully structural route from household labor supply estimation to optimal tax design. Instead of relying on stylized Mirrlees/Saez formulas plus imported elasticity values, it computes optimal taxes inside the same microeconometric environment that generates behavior. 

For your JMP, it is particularly important because it explicitly combines three ingredients that are often separated in the literature: heterogeneous preferences, explicit opportunity sets, and normative tax design. It is also one of the clearest examples where the distinction between preferences and opportunities is not merely discussed but built into the empirical model. 

# Core research question

What income tax schedule maximizes social welfare, subject to a public budget constraint, when labor supply behavior is generated by a flexible microeconometric model of singles and couples with heterogeneous preferences and heterogeneous opportunity sets? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper studies Norway, taking the 1994 tax system as the benchmark institutional environment and searching for alternative optimal personal income tax schedules. The broader context is the empirical optimal-tax literature, which the authors criticize for relying on restrictive analytical formulas and potentially inconsistent empirical inputs. 

The policy setting is narrowly defined as personal income taxation. Other elements of the wider fiscal system, such as VAT, payroll taxes, social assistance, and most transfers, are kept fixed at their 1994 levels. The search is therefore over personal income tax schedules, not over the full tax-benefit system. 

The target population includes working-age singles and couples. The sample excludes the self-employed and permanent disability recipients. The behavioral model is estimated on 1,842 couples, 309 single females, and 312 single males. 

# Model / theoretical framework

The model is a static structural labor supply model of the random utility / multinomial choice type, embedded in a computational optimal-tax framework. On the positive side, households choose among jobs characterized by hours, wages, and job characteristics. On the normative side, a social planner chooses tax parameters to maximize a social welfare function under a revenue constraint. The framework is thus explicitly both positive and normative. 

The key choice object is a job, not just hours of work. For singles, the agent solves a problem over ((w,h,s,j)), where (w) is the wage rate, (h) hours, (s) observed job characteristics, and (j) unobserved job/household characteristics. The budget constraint maps gross income and non-labor income into disposable income via the tax rule. 

The feasible set is an opportunity set (B) containing both market and non-market opportunities. This is critical: the model does not assume that all households face the same universal hours choice set. Instead, jobs are distributed according to an opportunity density (p(h,w,s)), allowing opportunities to differ across individuals. 

The optimal-tax problem is solved over a class of 10-parameter piecewise-linear tax rules with five brackets, including potentially negative marginal tax rates at the bottom and a lump-sum term that can be positive or negative. 

# Key objects

The main behavioral objects are household labor supply choices, wage elasticities, joint household responses, and the opportunity density over jobs. The paper emphasizes that households respond not only in hours but also through job choice more broadly, because jobs differ in wages and non-pecuniary characteristics. 

The central opportunity object is the density (p(h,w,s)) of available jobs. This object governs the relative availability of different jobs and is part of the structural likelihood. It is one of the main ways the paper separates preferences from constraints. 

The main normative objects are the common individual welfare function (V(y,h)), the equivalent-income transformation for couples, and the family of rank-dependent social welfare functions (W_1, W_2, W_3,) and (W_\infty). The tax design object is the piecewise-linear tax schedule parameterized by five marginal tax rates, four bracket limits, and one lump-sum parameter. 

# Data

The behavioral model is estimated using the 1995 Norwegian Survey of Level of Living, which includes detailed tax-record income information. The choice of the 1994 sample is justified by relatively stable macroeconomic conditions. 

The estimation sample contains working-age individuals aged 20–62. The paper excludes self-employed individuals and permanent disability recipients. The sample comprises 1,842 couples, 309 single females, and 312 single males. 

The out-of-sample validation exercise uses 2001 tax rules and data from the 2002 Norwegian Survey of Level of Living to predict 2001 disposable income distributions. On page 12, Table 4 shows that the simulated relative decile distributions are close to the observed ones for couples, single females, and single males, which the authors use as evidence of satisfactory predictive performance. 

# Identification logic

Identification is structural rather than quasi-experimental. Preferences are identified from observed labor supply choices conditional on detailed tax rules, wages, non-labor income, and job opportunity densities. Because the model explicitly incorporates the tax system and the opportunity set, identification comes from variation in the attractiveness and availability of jobs across households. 

A key identification feature is that the choice probability depends jointly on systematic utility and the job opportunity density (p(h,w,s)). This means that the model is not forcing all observed heterogeneity into preferences. It allows differences in available jobs to shift behavior even if preferences are held fixed. 

The optimal-tax stage is identified by simulation. For each candidate tax rule, the model simulates household choices, computes welfare levels, aggregates them through a social welfare function, and checks the revenue constraint. This is only convincing if the behavioral model predicts policy changes reasonably well, which is why the out-of-sample validation exercise is important. 

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper uses a microeconometric structural labor supply model estimated by maximum likelihood, building on Aaberge and Colombino (2011). The model contains 78 parameters that capture heterogeneity in preferences and opportunities among households and individuals. The estimated model is then used as the engine for tax optimization. 

Behavioral implications are summarized via simulated labor supply elasticities. Table 1 on page 10 reports own- and cross-wage elasticities by household type and income decile. The aggregate wage elasticity is about 0.12, but this masks strong heterogeneity across groups and along the income distribution. 

The optimal-tax exercise is implemented by iterative global search over approximately 200,000 tax-transfer rules. For each candidate rule, the paper simulates choices over household-specific opportunity sets, imputes equivalent income, evaluates the chosen bundles with the common welfare function, and aggregates welfare with alternative rank-dependent social welfare functions. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences are heterogeneous and are used to generate actual household behavior. The paper stresses that it does not make sense to aggregate these estimated utility functions directly as if they were interpersonally comparable welfare functions, especially because households differ in preferences and household structure. 

This generates a two-level structure. Actual utility functions govern behavior, but welfare evaluation is performed using a separate common utility function (V) chosen by the social planner. Thus the paper does not use preference-respecting welfare evaluation in the strict sense. Instead, it treats heterogeneous actual preferences as behaviorally relevant but not normatively aggregable. 

The paper also highlights cross-elasticities within couples. On page 10, Table 1 shows that cross-wage elasticities are often non-negligible, reinforcing the importance of joint household modeling rather than separate one-person labor supply equations. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This is one of the strongest papers in your corpus on the opportunity side.

The paper models opportunities explicitly through the opportunity set (B) and the density (p(h,w,s)) of available jobs. This allows households to differ not only in preferences and wages but also in the number and characteristics of available jobs. The model therefore does not assume a universal common hours grid. 

The paper is explicit that it accounts for both observed and unobserved heterogeneity in constraints and can accommodate quantity constraints in labor supply choices. This includes the possibility that some households face restricted job availability. In that sense, opportunities are treated as first-class objects in the structural model. 

At the same time, the opportunity set is still represented in reduced form as a density over jobs characterized by hours, wages, and sector. It is not yet a full RURO model with richer occupation-level job characteristics or a separate process for opportunity formation. Still, relative to the standard discrete-hours labor supply literature, this paper is much closer to an explicit opportunities framework. 

# Welfare / normative object

The paper is explicitly normative. The welfare object is a common individual welfare function (V(y,h)) that evaluates after-tax income and leisure in an interpersonally comparable way. For couples, equivalent income is constructed by dividing household disposable income by (\sqrt{2}), and then the social planner evaluates the chosen bundle with the common welfare function. 

Social aggregation is then done using rank-dependent social welfare functions. The paper considers (W_1) (Bonferroni), (W_2) (Gini), (W_3), and (W_\infty) (utilitarian). On page 16, Table 6 reports the distributional weight profiles, showing that (W_1) gives much larger relative weight to the bottom of the distribution than (W_3) or the utilitarian case. 

This is therefore not a paper about equivalent income in the Fleurbaey-Maniquet responsibility-sensitive sense, nor about direct aggregation of heterogeneous actual utilities. It is a normative tax-design paper based on a common welfare metric and rank-dependent welfare aggregation. 

# Main findings

First, all optimal tax schedules have monotonically increasing marginal tax rates. On page 19, Table 7 shows that the marginal rates rise across brackets under all social welfare criteria. 

Second, the optimal bottom marginal tax rate is negative, while the optimal top marginal tax rate is 100 percent. The negative bottom rate makes the schedule resemble in-work support mechanisms such as the Earned Income Tax Credit or Working Families Tax Credit, while the top 100 percent rate applies only to a very small fraction of taxpayers at the top of the distribution. 

Third, all optimal rules imply a lower average tax rate than the current 1994 rule while keeping total net tax revenue constant. The authors interpret this as the result of stronger labor supply under the optimized tax schedules. 

Fourth, compared with the current rule, the optimal schedules lower marginal rates on low and/or average incomes and raise them on very high incomes. This goes against the standard reform pattern of reducing top marginal tax rates while lowering average tax rates. 

Fifth, more egalitarian welfare criteria imply more progressive tax schedules. Bonferroni is more progressive than Gini, which is more progressive than (W_3), while the utilitarian rule is least progressive among the four cases. 

Sixth, the strongest labor supply responses come from lower-income households and especially from married women. On page 21, Table 10 shows very large total-hours responses in the bottom deciles, with much smaller responses near the top. 

Seventh, all optimal rules generate a strong majority of winners relative to the 1994 system. On page 22, Table 11 shows that each optimal rule would “win the referendum” against the current rule, although some subgroups, especially higher-income single women, may lose. 

# Main limitations

The main limitation is that the results are application-specific. The paper is explicit that the exercise is Norway-specific and depends on the estimated model and the chosen class of tax rules. It is not a general analytical characterization of optimal taxation. 

A second limitation is that the social planner uses a common welfare function rather than the actual heterogeneous utility functions. This solves interpersonal comparability, but it also means the welfare analysis is not preference-respecting in a heterogeneous-preferences sense. 

A third limitation is that the tax search is confined to a flexible but still restricted class of piecewise-linear rules. The optimal rule is therefore conditional on that tax-rule family, not globally optimal over all possible schedules. 

A fourth limitation is that the top marginal tax result may depend on missing behavioral margins. The authors themselves note that the 100 percent top rate might fall if the model fully captured top-income responses such as intercountry mobility or taxable-income response. 

A fifth limitation, relative to your agenda, is that opportunity sets are explicit but still reduced-form. The model does not offer a richer multidimensional latent-job structure with occupation/task/job-quality characteristics, and it does not fully model the process generating opportunities. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing a project that argues for moving beyond formula-based optimal tax exercises. It provides a strong example of how behavioral heterogeneity and opportunity heterogeneity can be carried directly into tax design. 

## possible use for model design

It is highly relevant for model design because the unit of choice is a job, not simply hours, and because the model explicitly includes singles and couples. If your project wants to combine labor supply estimation with opportunity-sensitive welfare or policy analysis, this is a major benchmark. 

## possible use for identification

The paper is useful for identification because it shows how opportunity densities and preference parameters can be estimated jointly and then validated through out-of-sample prediction. That is a stronger empirical discipline than fitting current labor supply alone. 

## possible use for welfare measurement

This paper is useful partly as a model and partly as a foil. It shows one coherent solution to the problem of welfare evaluation under heterogeneous preferences, namely the use of a common welfare function. If your JMP aims instead to preserve heterogeneity in welfare evaluation, this paper helps clarify exactly what is gained and lost under the common-function approach. 

## possible use for cross-country comparison

The paper is single-country, but methodologically it suggests that cross-country welfare or tax comparisons should not ignore country-specific opportunity sets. If opportunities differ across institutional environments, then the kind of structural exercise done here would need to be re-estimated country by country rather than transported mechanically. 

# Research questions this paper inspires

How would optimal tax schedules change if opportunity sets were allowed to vary across countries rather than being estimated only for one institutional environment? 

Can one combine this opportunity-sensitive microeconometric tax-design approach with a welfare metric that preserves heterogeneous preferences rather than imposing a common welfare function? 

How much of the optimal negative bottom marginal tax rate is driven by low-income labor supply elasticities, and how much by opportunity constraints in the bottom segment of the labor market? 

Would the top optimal marginal tax rate remain near 100 percent if one enriched the model with taxable-income responses, migration, and richer top-job opportunities? 

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest simplification is that the paper combines a rich structural model of behavior with a relatively reduced-form normative comparison device. Actual household choices are generated by heterogeneous preferences and heterogeneous opportunity sets, but welfare is evaluated with a common utility function that suppresses preference heterogeneity at the normative stage. A future paper could challenge this by integrating a more opportunity-sensitive and preference-sensitive welfare criterion into the same structural design. 

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

This paper is closely related to Bargain et al. (2013) in its combination of structural labor supply estimation and normative analysis, but the two papers differ sharply in what they do with heterogeneity. Bargain et al. (2013) uses heterogeneous estimated preferences to study individual welfare rankings across countries. Aaberge and Colombino instead use heterogeneous preferences to generate behavior, but then replace them with a common welfare function in order to perform interpersonal welfare comparisons for optimal tax design. Relative to Bargain et al. (2013), this paper is stronger on explicit opportunity modeling and weaker on preserving heterogeneous preferences as normatively relevant objects. 

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper clearly helps separate preferences from opportunities. Preferences and opportunity sets enter distinct parts of the structural model, and job availability is represented explicitly through an opportunity density rather than being folded into tastes. However, at the welfare stage the paper does not preserve heterogeneous preferences; it replaces them with a common evaluative function. So the paper is strongest on the positive separation of preferences and opportunities, but less aligned with a fully preference-sensitive normative agenda. 

# Useful quotations / formulas

The core behavioral choice density is
[
\varphi(h,w,s)=
\frac{v[f(wh,I),h,s],p(h,w,s)}
{\iiint v[f(xy,I),y,z],p(y,x,z),dxdydz},
]
which shows directly that choice depends on both utility and the density of available opportunities. This is the central formula for your opportunities-versus-preferences agenda. 

The common welfare function used by the planner is
[
\log V(y,h)=\gamma_2\left(\frac{y^{\gamma_1}-1}{\gamma_1}\right)
+\gamma_4\left(\frac{L^{\gamma_3}-1}{\gamma_3}\right),
]
with equivalent income defined for couples by dividing household disposable income by (\sqrt{2}). 

A short quotation that captures the paper’s methodological contribution is that the authors “do not start from theoretical results that dictate the conditions for optimal tax rules” but instead “use a microeconometric model of labour supply in order to identify, by simulation, the tax rule that maximizes a social welfare function.”

# My quick takeaway

If I only remember one thing from this paper for my JMP, it should be this: once labor supply is modeled as choice over heterogeneous job opportunities rather than as free hours choice, empirical optimal tax design can look very different from the standard analytical formulas—more supportive at the bottom, more progressive at the very top, and much more tightly tied to the actual structure of opportunities.
