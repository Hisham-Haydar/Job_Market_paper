# Full citation

Bargain, Olivier, and Andreas Peichl. 2016. “Own-wage labor supply elasticities: variation across time and estimation methods.” *IZA Journal of Labor Economics* 5:10. DOI: 10.1186/s40172-016-0050-z. 

# One-sentence contribution

The paper surveys and meta-analyzes static labor supply elasticity estimates for Europe and the US and argues that variation across studies is driven primarily by time period rather than by estimation method.

# Why this paper matters

This paper matters because labor supply elasticities are core inputs in tax-benefit reform simulation and optimal-tax analysis, yet the literature contains very wide variation in reported values. The paper’s main contribution is not a new structural labor supply estimate for one country, but a disciplined synthesis of how elasticities differ across demographic groups, countries, time periods, and estimation approaches. 

For your agenda, it is especially useful as a methodological map. It clarifies what the elasticity literature can and cannot tell you, why estimated responses differ, and why one should be cautious in importing elasticity values from one setting into another. It also helps position papers like Bargain et al. (2013, 2014): those papers use structural labor supply models in welfare or optimal-tax settings, whereas this paper steps back and evaluates the credibility and comparability of the elasticity inputs themselves.

# Core research question

How much of the large variation in own-wage labor supply elasticities reported in the literature is due to genuine differences over time, and how much is due to differences in estimation methods such as continuous Hausman-type models, discrete-choice structural models, and quasi-experimental approaches? 

# Economic setting and context

The paper is not an application to a single country or policy episode. Its setting is the empirical literature on static labor supply in Western Europe and the United States, especially research used for tax-benefit policy analysis. The authors frame the problem around “steady-state” labor supply elasticities relevant for ex ante reform simulation and optimal tax calibration. 

The institutional context is therefore broad: tax and benefit systems matter because many of the underlying studies identify labor supply responses from nonlinear budget constraints, reforms, or policy discontinuities. The paper is concerned with how those estimates should be interpreted and selected for policy work rather than with estimating one common structural model on one harmonized dataset. 

# Model / theoretical framework

This is primarily a review and meta-analysis paper, not a paper centered on one new structural model. Its analytical framework is the literature on static labor supply elasticities. It classifies and compares three main empirical traditions: continuous labor supply models of the Hausman type, structural discrete-choice labor supply models, and quasi-experimental / natural-experiment approaches. It also discusses a smaller set of long-run grouping or IV-style approaches. 

The paper is therefore positive rather than normative in its direct objective, although it is motivated by the normative and policy importance of elasticities in welfare and optimal-tax analysis. There is no welfare ranking, social welfare function, or equivalent-income object estimated in this paper itself. The central object is the elasticity estimate and the sources of its variation across studies. 

If one asks what the “agent chooses,” the answer depends on the underlying studies under review: hours of work, labor-force participation, or both. The paper itself does not specify one unique feasible set. Instead, it compares methods that variously represent labor supply through continuous hours, discrete hours menus, or reform-based reduced-form responses. 

# Key objects

The key objects are own-wage labor supply elasticities and income elasticities from static frameworks. The paper distinguishes total-hour wage elasticities, participation elasticities, intensive-margin responses, and extensive-margin responses, and emphasizes that these should not be conflated. It also discusses uncompensated and compensated wage elasticities and notes that, because income effects are often small, the two are frequently close in practice.

A second key object is the study-level variation in these elasticities. The authors compile 282 elasticity estimates from 92 studies, including 156 wage elasticities for individuals in couples, 70 wage elasticities for single individuals and lone parents, and 56 income elasticities. They then relate these estimates to demographic group, country, period of observation, and estimation method. 

A third key object is the meta-regression coefficient on time. In the common-support sample for married women, the paper reports that an additional year lowers the wage elasticity by about 0.013, which they interpret as a substantial decline over time. 

# Data

The paper’s direct dataset is a literature-based meta-sample rather than microdata on households. It compiles elasticity estimates from 92 studies for Western Europe and the USA. The underlying studies use many different microdata sources, including survey panels, labor force surveys, household budget surveys, CPS, PSID, SIPP, and administrative data, but those are the sources of the original studies, not newly pooled microdata for this paper.

The authors state that they compare 282 elasticity estimates. The underlying evidence covers married women, married men, childless single women and men, and single mothers/lone parents. The period covered stretches broadly from the late 1960s to the 2000s, and the main meta-regressions use both an all-years sample and a common-support window in which both Hausman-type and discrete-choice estimates coexist.

# Identification logic

The paper itself does not identify a new behavioral parameter from primary microdata. Instead, it evaluates identification strategies used in the literature and then studies how resulting estimates differ across study characteristics. The identification discussion is therefore methodological and comparative rather than one empirical design. 

For continuous Hausman-type models, the paper stresses identification from cross-sectional variation in after-tax wages and virtual income and highlights the core endogeneity problem: unobservables affecting work preferences may also affect wages and non-labor income. It also emphasizes sensitivity to functional form, tangency conditions, global Slutsky restrictions, and measurement error such as division bias. 

For structural discrete-choice models, the paper says identification comes mainly from nonlinearities, non-convexities, and discontinuities in tax-benefit budget sets, sometimes supplemented by time or regional variation in tax rules. For natural experiments, identification comes from reforms or discontinuities, but the paper notes limitations such as control-group validity and restricted external validity. 

So the paper’s own empirical logic is meta-analytic: if time or method matters systematically, study-level elasticity estimates should covary with observation year and model type. The authors find stronger evidence for the former than the latter.

# Estimation / empirical strategy

The paper combines three elements. First, it surveys the empirical literature on static labor supply elasticities. Second, it classifies estimates by demographic group, country, estimation method, and period. Third, it runs simple meta-regressions of reported elasticity values on study characteristics such as year of data, method class, use of desired hours, and selected modeling features.

The meta-analysis focuses especially on married women and, in some specifications, on married women together with single mothers. The key comparison is between the data year and an indicator for discrete-choice versus Hausman-type methods. The paper reports that time effects remain significant whereas method effects are broadly insignificant once time is controlled for. 

# Treatment of preferences

Preferences enter this paper indirectly, through the underlying labor supply studies it reviews. The paper explicitly notes that recent studies suggest that preference heterogeneity across countries explains only a small share of the overall variation in elasticity estimates. It therefore shifts attention away from “deep” preference differences and toward time period and estimation method as practical determinants of observed differences across studies.

At a conceptual level, the paper discusses labor supply models as resting on consumption-leisure preferences. In the review of methods, it points out that continuous and discrete-choice approaches differ in how they parameterize those preferences and in the restrictions they impose. But the paper does not estimate heterogeneous preferences itself and does not develop a normative treatment of preference heterogeneity. 

# Treatment of opportunities / constraints

This section is central for your agenda, even though the paper is not organized around it.

The paper does not model opportunities explicitly. It is about the estimation of labor supply elasticities, not about latent job offers, job opportunity sets, or RURO-type structures. However, it repeatedly notes that labor supply estimates are affected by institutional and demand-side features. In its discussion of methods, it emphasizes that discrete-choice models can incorporate non-participation, fixed costs of work, and full tax-benefit non-convexities more naturally than Hausman-type models. It also notes that reduced-form reform evidence often has limited external validity and that childless singles may be harder to study partly because non-participation may more often reflect demand-side constraints rather than voluntary choice.

So the paper clearly recognizes that observed labor supply responses are not a pure reflection of preferences, but it does not turn that recognition into an explicit opportunity-set framework. Relative to your research interests, this is an important omission: the paper is methodologically sophisticated on elasticity estimation, yet still operates within a labor-supply paradigm where opportunity heterogeneity is discussed as a complication, not modeled as a first-class object.

# Welfare / normative object

None in the direct sense. The paper does not construct an individual welfare metric, social welfare ranking, or fairness criterion. Its relevance for welfare economics is indirect: it argues that labor supply elasticities are crucial inputs for policy simulation and optimal taxation, so poor choices of elasticity values can distort welfare and policy conclusions.

The paper is therefore best described as purely positive with strong welfare-policy implications. It is about the empirical credibility and transportability of key behavioral parameters used downstream in welfare or optimal-tax analysis. 

# Main findings

The first main finding is that the literature contains very wide variation in labor supply elasticity estimates, but the broad stylized hierarchy remains: own-wage elasticities are largest for married women, smaller for men, and often substantial for single mothers, while evidence for childless singles is thinner. The demographic distributions summarized in Figure 1 show mean wage elasticities around 0.43 for married women, 0.12 for men, 0.23 for childless single women, and 0.59 for single mothers. 

The second main finding is that a strong time decline appears in the elasticity literature, especially for married women and also for single mothers. The graphical evidence shows a concentration of high estimates in earlier decades and much lower estimates since the late 1990s; the paper states that this pattern appears in both Europe and the US.

The third main finding is that the time effect survives meta-regression. In the common-support sample for married women, an extra year lowers the wage elasticity by roughly 0.013, and this result is robust to including a dummy for discrete-choice models. The paper interprets this as support for a “shrinking elasticity” hypothesis. 

The fourth main finding is that the estimation method, by itself, does not explain much once time is controlled for. The paper says it finds no compelling evidence that the choice of estimation method explains variation in elasticity estimates; method effects seen in raw comparisons largely reflect the correlation between older periods and Hausman-type methods.

The fifth main finding is that income elasticities are generally small, often negative, and not a major source of difference between compensated and uncompensated wage elasticities.

# Main limitations

The main limitation is that this is a meta-analysis over heterogeneous studies rather than a unified re-estimation on harmonized microdata. The paper is transparent about the fact that it is pooling estimates produced under very different data sources, demographic selections, model specifications, and reporting conventions. That limits how sharply one can interpret the meta-regression coefficients.

A second limitation is that the paper does not identify causal effects of time or method. It explicitly notes that, like all meta-regressions, its analysis is not identifying causal effects because omitted variables may remain. 

A third limitation, relative to your research interests, is the absence of explicit opportunity modeling. The paper acknowledges demand-side constraints and institutional factors, but it remains within the standard elasticity literature rather than moving toward RURO, latent jobs, or opportunity-set estimation.

A fourth limitation is that the paper is not directly about welfare measurement even though it is motivated by welfare and policy applications. It helps you choose better behavioral inputs; it does not solve the normative problem of how to evaluate well-being when preferences or opportunities differ. 

# Relevance for my JMP

## possible use for framing

This paper is very useful for framing any project that uses labor supply elasticities as inputs. It provides a disciplined reason not to treat an elasticity from one classic paper as a universal constant. Instead, it supports the position that elasticity values are historically contingent and demographic-group specific.

## possible use for model design

If your JMP involves a structural labor supply model, this paper is helpful as a methodological guide to the strengths and weaknesses of continuous Hausman-type models, discrete-choice models, and quasi-experimental approaches. In particular, it gives a concise rationale for preferring discrete-choice structures in tax-benefit applications when non-participation and nonlinear budget sets matter. 

## possible use for identification

This paper is useful for identifying what kinds of variation are doing the work in different labor supply traditions. For a project that tries to separate preferences from opportunities, that is important because it shows where standard identification relies on tax-benefit nonlinearities and where demand-side or opportunity constraints may still contaminate interpretation.

## possible use for welfare measurement

Indirectly, it helps welfare measurement by disciplining the behavioral parameters that feed into optimal-tax or microsimulation exercises. The lesson is that welfare conclusions can be very sensitive to the elasticity input, and that historically outdated values may overstate contemporary responsiveness.

## possible use for cross-country comparison

This paper is especially useful if your project compares countries. It suggests that once one holds year and method in view, genuine cross-country preference heterogeneity explains only a limited share of observed variation in elasticities. That is a strong caution against reading cross-country differences in the literature too literally when the underlying estimates come from different decades and methods.

# Research questions this paper inspires

How much of the apparent historical decline in labor supply elasticities reflects changes in preferences, and how much reflects changes in labor-market opportunities, childcare institutions, and participation constraints? 

Can one build a harmonized cross-country RURO or latent-job framework that explains why elasticity estimates vary less across countries than across decades?

Do modern discrete-choice labor supply estimates remain stable once explicit job availability or rationing is modeled, especially for childless singles whose non-participation may reflect demand-side constraints? 

How should welfare and optimal-tax analysis be adjusted when historically large elasticity values from the 1970s–1980s are no longer credible for current policy environments?

# Challenge to this paper

The strongest omission is that the paper explains variation in elasticity estimates mainly through time and methods while leaving the underlying opportunity structure largely implicit. That is understandable given its objective, but it means the paper cannot tell whether “time trends” are really changes in preferences, labor-market attachment, childcare institutions, selection into demographic groups, or unmodeled opportunity constraints. A future paper could challenge the elasticity literature more fundamentally by embedding these estimates in an explicit model of job opportunities and constrained choice.

# Relation to Bargain et al. (2013)

The relation is indirect but important. Bargain et al. (2013) uses structural labor supply estimation to recover heterogeneous preferences and then construct alternative welfare rankings across countries. This 2016 paper is more methodological: it asks how much confidence one should have in labor supply elasticities themselves and what explains their variation across studies. For your corpus, the two papers work together: the 2013 paper shows how structural labor supply estimates can enter welfare analysis, while the 2016 paper cautions that those estimates are not fixed primitives and vary substantially with period and empirical design.

# Relation to opportunities vs preferences

This paper does not separate preferences from opportunities in a direct structural sense. What it does do is show that variation in estimated labor supply responses cannot be read simply as variation in deep preferences or even as variation in methods alone. Because the paper repeatedly points to demand-side constraints, institutional change, and different identification environments, it actually strengthens your agenda: it implies that a serious future literature should treat opportunities explicitly rather than leaving them buried inside elasticity estimates.

# Useful quotations / formulas

The paper defines the uncompensated wage elasticity as
[
\varepsilon^u=\frac{dh}{dw}\frac{w}{h},
]
the income elasticity as
[
\varepsilon^y=\frac{dh}{dy}\frac{y}{h},
]
and the compensated elasticity through the Slutsky relation
[
\varepsilon^c=\varepsilon^u-\frac{wh}{y}\varepsilon^y.
]
These are the central elasticities used throughout the survey. 

A short formulation of the main conclusion is that the paper finds “no compelling evidence” that estimation method explains the variation in elasticity estimates, while time changes do.

# My quick takeaway

If I only remember one thing from this paper for my JMP, it should be this: labor supply elasticities are not portable constants, and much of the variation in the literature reflects when the estimate was produced rather than which estimator was used—so any serious structural or welfare project should use elasticity evidence that is temporally and institutionally matched to the policy environment it studies.
