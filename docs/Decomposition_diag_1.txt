DECOMP-DIAG-1: can B move households across the wage distribution?
Short answer: yes. The reported Delta_I measures the model's opportunity effect; the node support does not cap it. Phi_B is small because the model's estimated differences in wage offers between households are small, not because the nodes run out.

What I ran: a read-only diagnostic on the DECOMP-2 engine (b52761b4). Nothing under outputs/welfare/preseminar_pab_v1 changed. There was no re-estimation, no pricing, and only coalitions that were already recorded (EMPTY, B, PA, PAB).

Checks:

Same simulation: on the same seed and random-number stream, I(S) matches the recorded values to ≤ 8e-17 for all four coalitions, both samples, both scales.
Same B operator: my rebuilt own and reference wage-offer locations reproduce the engine's V(B) − V(EMPTY) to 2e-14.
The script and CSVs are in the scratchpad (decomp_diag1.py, decomp_diag1_out/). Only group-level figures are written.

How B works here: B replaces the offer location built from education and potential experience with the sample reference. The occupation term (set per node), sigma, the wages and the priced consumption stay fixed. So the most B can move anyone's offer location is about ±0.10 log points.

How the bounds are built:

Starting point: attainment under the recorded PA coalition. Starting from PAB would apply B twice; in the smoke test that version raised inequality.
Wage change: each worker's attained wage is replaced by the reference-density quantile at their rank. Consumption then changes by retention × Δearnings, with hours fixed and no re-choice.
Retention: "local" is each household's own consumption-on-earnings slope across its already-priced nodes (median 0.60 for singles, 0.64 for couples).
R1 uses the household's rank within its own offer density. It asks what fully equalising offer locations would do if new nodes could be created. Its Delta lands at 0.9–1.15× the actual Delta_I; the one outlier, couples equivalised at retention = 1, is 0.01×. So the model's reweighting already captures the location effect.
R2 uses the rank in the pooled attained-wage distribution and maps it onto the pooled reference mixture. This compresses the whole wage distribution, including the common offer spread (sigma) and selection. It gives 2–8× Delta_I, but that is equalising wage luck, which B by definition does not touch.
The consumption floor was hit on 55 of about 3.8M household-replications, which is negligible.
5. Verdict
The reported Delta_I measures the model's opportunity effect. The node support does not set it as a lower bound.

Support is not binding. Each household's nodes cover 95–98% of the reference offer mass.
B does what its label says. It moves attained wages across education groups and closes most of the education gap.
The oracle agrees. R1, which equalises offer locations without any support limit, reproduces the actual Delta_I to within about ±15% (one couples variant is the exception above).
Phi_B is small because the estimated offer heterogeneity B removes is small. Education and experience coefficients shift offer locations by at most about 0.10 log points, against sigma ≈ 0.37. On top of that, about 60% of an earnings change passes through to consumption.

Two caveats should travel with the number:

"Earning opportunities" means offer location only. Wage spread (sigma) and selection stay in the residual, and R2 suggests they are worth 2–8× more.
Couples support is thin. Mean ESS is about 17 and 23% of couples are below 10 effective nodes. That leaves per-household wage attainment noisy, though it does not cap it.
