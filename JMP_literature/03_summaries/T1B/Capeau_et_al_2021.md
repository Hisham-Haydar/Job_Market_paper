## 1. One-paragraph relevance to my JMP
This paper is the most general statement of how a Fleurbaey-style **nested-opportunity-set (NOS)** welfare *level* — the class that contains my money-metric / equivalent-income object — is constructed and identified in a discrete-choice model with unrestricted unobserved preference heterogeneity. Its NOS construction (Equations 1, 10–13) is the formal scaffold my $W^1$–$W^6$ family sits inside: each of my measures is a NOS measure with a particular reference, and the paper's MMU and equivalent-income (EI) special cases (its Equation 13 and Appendix A.1) are exactly the kind of reference-indexed money metric I compute. It speaks to the **preference** channel most directly — it is built to be agnostic about the utility function $v$ and the heterogeneity distribution — and to my **welfare object** generally; it does **not** speak to the **access** or **ability** channels, because it has no opportunity mechanism and treats the choice set as universal. Its most useful disanalogy for me is methodological: it identifies welfare distributions *nonparametrically from choice/transition probabilities* with relative-price variation, whereas my baseline is a parametric RURO estimate certified by synthetic recovery; the contrast sharpens my identification note and my defence against a "your welfare is just your functional form" referee.

## 2. Data and setting
- **Country/years:** Germany, SOEP 2018 wave (explicit-in-source).
- **Dataset:** German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP), with a German tax-benefit calculator provided by Peter Haan (acknowledged) used to compute disposable income at each alternative.
- **Sample unit:** individuals — specifically **single females** available to the labour market (under 60). Couples are *not* modelled; the joint-decision unit is absent.
- **Sample size:** final estimation subsample of **1,922 single females** (explicit). Of these, 1,492 have an observed hourly gross wage; 430 wages are missing and imputed by a Heckman-type selection model (descriptive table, Appendix C.1).
- **Key variables:** weekly hours worked, gross hourly wage (restricted to 4–90 EUR), yearly asset income (capped below 12,000 EUR), age, years of education, number of children by age band, East Germany dummy. Disposable income is computed at three hour points (0/20/40 h/week) by the tax-benefit calculator.
- **Budget-set construction:** three discrete labour-supply alternatives — non-working (NW, <5 h/wk), part-time (PT, 5–32 h/wk), full-time (FT, ≥32 h/wk) — each carrying a tax-benefit-calculated disposable income; the NW disposable income is floored at the social-assistance guarantee.
- **Transport to my France pooled 2015–2017 EUROMOD cross-section:** *partial.* The architecture transports — discrete labour-supply alternatives priced by a tax-benefit microsimulator, money-metric welfare on the resulting numeraire — and is the same family Bargain et al. (2013) and my own build instantiate. What I do **not** have / what differs: (i) the paper uses **only three alternatives**, where I use 101 (singles) / 901 (couples) sampled latent jobs; (ii) it is single-females-only, where I have single males, single females, and **couples as a joint unit**; (iii) its identification engine wants **relative-price variation** and, for the joint level-difference results, **panel/transition data**, neither of which my pooled repeated cross-section supplies in the form the paper's point-identification theorems require (I have no panel and currently no external instrument). The 2018 single wave is, like my data, effectively cross-sectional for the level results.

## 3. Model and objects (object-by-object map to mine)
- **Their choice set vs my latent-jobs set:** *Different.* Their $C$ is a fixed, common, three-element menu $\{NW,PT,FT\}$ available to everyone (explicit). My set is a household-specific sampled latent-jobs set with an estimated opportunity density governing availability. Their set carries **no** availability/feasibility variation across people — this is the central structural difference and the reason the paper cannot, by construction, separate access from preference.
- **Their deterministic utility vs my preference utility $v$:** Their $U_c^\omega(y-p_c)$ is left **fully nonparametric** (continuous, strictly increasing in the numeraire; Assumption 1) with arbitrary unobserved type $\omega$; they deliberately do **not** parameterise or recover it. My $v$ is a parametric Box–Cox block over consumption and leisure with demographic shifters. So their utility object is strictly more general than mine but is never estimated as an object.
- **Opportunity / availability mechanism analogous to my $g$:** **None** (explicit-in-source: the choice set is treated as freely available; the only "opportunity sets" in the paper are the *virtual* nested sets $B_\lambda$ that *define the welfare metric*, not descriptions of what jobs a person can get). There is therefore no hours channel, no wage-technology (ability) channel, no market/participation channel, and no occupation channel in their model. The NOS $B_\lambda$ family is a normative reference device, not my $g$. **(derived-by-analogy caution: do not map $B_\lambda$ to my opportunity density $g$ — they are different kinds of object.)**
- **Their budget map vs my EUROMOD disposable income:** Analogous. Both price each discrete alternative through a tax-benefit microsimulator to a disposable-income numeraire; theirs is a German calculator, mine is EUROMOD `ils_dispy` on a 2016-real basis.
- **Does any job attribute enter BOTH utility and the opportunity mechanism?** N/A — there is no opportunity mechanism, so the double-entry flag cannot arise. (This is precisely the design risk the paper sidesteps by not modelling opportunities at all.)

## 4. Estimation method
- **Likelihood/estimator:** No structural likelihood. The objects of interest are **choice probabilities** $P_i(\mathbf p,y)$ and **transition probabilities** $P_{i,j}(\mathbf p,\mathbf p',y)$, which they treat as conditional expectation functions ("average structural functions") estimable by nonparametric regression (kernel / series). In the empirical illustration they use a **semiparametric** estimator (described as a sieve approximation): for PT and FT each a flexible binary logit with cubic polynomials in the disposable incomes of all three alternatives plus a linear demographic index; NW is the complement. Estimation is by **nonlinear least squares with a penalty function** enforcing the shape restrictions and non-negativity of the NW probability (Appendix C.2).
- **Choice-set construction:** fixed 3-point grid (NW/PT/FT). **Not** sampled alternatives.
- **Proposal / sampling density:** N/A — there is no sampling of alternatives, hence no proposal distribution.
- **Prior/proposal correction:** **N/A — there is no $-\log\pi$ correction in this paper.** This is a hard difference from my RURO pipeline, where the sampling-of-alternatives correction is mandatory. The paper's machinery is not a sampled-alternatives estimator and so does not need one.
- **Normalisation/scale:** They normalise exogenous income to the full-time disposable income, $y=d_{FT}$, which makes all alternative "prices" non-negative ($p_{NW}=d_{FT}-d_{NW}$, $p_{PT}=d_{FT}-d_{PT}$, $p_{FT}=0$). Reference prices for the MMU are set to the **sample median** of the disposable-income gap between FT and each alternative (Section 6.1).
- **Numerical method / starts / multistart:** NLS with a grid-based penalty; 90% confidence intervals via a bootstrap with **200 resamples** (Appendix C.3). No multistart procedure described. [verify whether any was used.]
- **What pins preferences vs the opportunity mechanism:** Nothing needs to — the method **never separates** the two. It identifies welfare distributions as functionals of choice probabilities *without* recovering preferences or any availability object. There is no preference/opportunity separation problem here because there is no opportunity object.
- **Verdict — reusable for my RURO/JAX pipeline?** **No, not as an estimator.** It is a different econometric philosophy (nonparametric identification of welfare functionals vs parametric structural estimation). **Yes, as a benchmark and as two specific imports:** (i) the NOS definition that frames my whole $W$ family, and (ii) the distributional-incidence machinery (levels × differences) as a template for a robustness/incidence exhibit, *if and only if* I can supply the transition probabilities it requires (I cannot point-identify them from my cross-section; I would inherit the bounds of §5.1 of the paper). The named step it could enter my pipeline at is the *welfare-incidence reporting* layer, not estimation.

## 4b. Proposal / sampling-of-alternatives correction
**Not applicable / not present.** The paper does not sample alternatives and carries no log-prior per alternative; there is no McFadden-style correction and no individualised-vs-common proposal distinction to extract. The only individual-level conditioning is that choice probabilities are functions of the individual's own $(\mathbf p,y)$ and demographics, and that disposable incomes (hence the implied "prices") are individual-specific. For my purposes the relevant contrast is explicit: **my importance-sampling welfare integrator needs $-\log\pi$ and a proposal-individualisation audit; this paper needs neither because it integrates over heterogeneity implicitly through observed choice frequencies rather than over sampled job nodes.** (explicit-in-source that there is no sampling; the contrast to my integrator is derived-by-analogy.)

## 5. Opportunity mechanism  [MOST IMPORTANT]
**There is no explicit opportunity mechanism in this paper.** Stated plainly: the choice set $\{NW,PT,FT\}$ is treated as freely and universally available; all cross-person variation in observed choices is attributed to prices, income, demographics, and unobserved **preferences**. The paper itself does not model demand-side rationing, offer probabilities, reservation wages, or feasible-set heterogeneity.

Mapping to my three sub-objects:
- **access** (hours / participation / region / year / occupation offers): **absent.** Hours alternatives exist as a menu but their *availability* is not modelled; everyone can choose any of the three. Region (East Germany) and demographics enter only as conditioning covariates in the choice-probability regression, not as an availability density.
- **ability** (wage technology — returns to education/experience, residual productivity dispersion): **absent as a structural object.** Wages are data (observed or Heckman-imputed) used to price alternatives; there is no estimated Mincer-type wage return and no productivity-dispersion parameter $\sigma$. Wage *quartiles* are used descriptively to group results, and the paper reads off a wage→welfare gradient, but this is post-hoc grouping, not an ability layer.
- **occupation:** **absent.** No occupation/`loc4`-type object; correspondingly **no sector/industry conflation risk arises** — the paper simply has no occupation or industry construct to confuse. (No flag needed.)

The only "opportunity sets" present are the **virtual nested opportunity sets** $B_\lambda$ (Equations 4–9) that *define* the NOS welfare metric. These are normative reference objects common to all individuals, not descriptions of feasible jobs. **Functional form:** $B_\lambda=\{(y',c): y'\le y^\lambda_c\}$ with $y^\lambda$ continuous and monotone in $\lambda$ (Equations 4–8), equivalently characterised by virtual prices $\tilde p_c(\lambda)=y-y^\lambda_c$ (Equation 9). For the MMU special case the upper bounds shift by a common amount across options, $y^\lambda_c=\lambda-p^{ref}_c$ (Equation 13).

**Cost of this omission to my access/ability/preference decomposition (derived-by-analogy):** because the paper has no $g$, it cannot separate opportunity-driven from preference-driven inequality — it can only attribute non-price variation to (unobserved) preferences. This is exactly the limitation my opportunity layer is built to remove, and the paper is therefore best cited as *the welfare-measurement scaffold* and as *evidence that the measurement layer can be made preference-agnostic*, **not** as a source for the opportunity mechanism. The paper's own empirical reading ("besides wages, preferences play an important role") is, in my vocabulary, a statement about the **ability**-correlate (wage) gradient plus a residual it labels preference — it cannot isolate **access** at all.

## 6. Welfare object — and its place on my $W^1$–$W^6$ map
- **Does it compute welfare?** Yes — this is the paper's purpose.
- **Type:** A general **NOS welfare *level*** (Equation 10/12), with the **money-metric utility (MMU)** as the leading worked example (Equation 13) and the **equivalent income (EI)** and **wage-rate (WR)** metrics treated in Appendix A.1. Welfare *differences* are handled via the **compensating variation (CV)** (Section 4.2.2, Theorem 3) and **equivalent variation (EV)** (Appendix A.2).
- **Universal vs constrained feasible set:** the welfare *level* is defined against a **universal common family** of virtual nested sets $B_\lambda$ — *not* against a household-specific feasible set. This is the deepest difference from my object: my welfare reads attained utility $V_i$ over the household's **own** feasible latent-jobs set (so unequal access lowers welfare through attainment), whereas their NOS level evaluates a chosen bundle against a *common* reference family with no notion of a person-specific accessible set.
- **Reference price / preference / bundle / set:** MMU uses researcher-fixed **reference prices** $p^{ref}$ (in the application, sample medians of FT-minus-alternative disposable income); EI fixes a **reference option**; WR fixes a reference **ray**/wage. The reference *preference* is always the individual's **own** preference (the metric is preference-respecting by construction) — there is no swap to a common reference preference.
- **Discrete-choice subtleties:** handled rigorously. The pivotal **Lemma 1** translates "$W^\omega\ge w$" into "$k$ is optimal at virtual prices $\tilde p(w)$", reducing welfare distributions to choice/transition probabilities. They derive marginal, conditional (on observed choice), and joint distributions (Theorem 1, Corollary 1). Welfare is a **random variable** from the econometrician's viewpoint (unobserved $\omega$), interpretable either as econometrician uncertainty or as within-type population inequality (Section 4 opening). Selection on the chosen alternative is handled explicitly via conditional distributions. **Hicksian vs Marshallian:** they work from **uncompensated (Marshallian)** choice/transition probabilities (following Bhattacharya 2015, de Palma–Kilani 2011), not Hicksian. **Ex-ante vs ex-post:** the welfare *level* is the metric value of an evaluated bundle/choice — this is closest to an **ex-post**, realised-choice valuation (it can be taken conditional on the observed optimal choice). It is **not** an ex-ante inclusive-value (log-sum) object; see §6b. Integration over unobserved heterogeneity is done implicitly by reading choice frequencies, not by simulation.
- **Locate on my $W^1$–$W^6$ family:** **The paper does NOT contain my $W^1$–$W^6$ family.** It contains the *parent class* (NOS measures) of which my six measures are instances, plus three named instances (MMU, EI, WR) and two difference objects (CV, EV). The correspondences I can assert:
  - My money-metric / equivalent-income measures are **NOS measures in their sense** — explicit-in-source that MMU and EI are NOS instances.
  - My Ind-$y$/Ind-$A$ classification (compensation–responsibility spectrum) is **not present** in this paper; it comes from the Fleurbaey / Fleurbaey–Maniquet fairness literature the paper *cites* (Fleurbaey 2009; Fleurbaey & Maniquet 2017, 2018), not from results derived here. **Do not attribute the $W^1$–$W^6$ characterisation to this source.**
  - Their MMU at reference-equal-actual prices returns welfare = actual income $y$ (Corollary 2) — a degenerate-reference property analogous to my $W^3$ laissez-faire measure returning $\Omega\approx 0$ on the own set. (derived-by-analogy; the *mechanism* differs because mine is an ex-ante inclusive value, theirs an ex-post level.)
- **Verdict:** **Adaptable, not directly usable.** The NOS scaffold and the MMU/EI definitions are directly citable primitives for my welfare layer. The *level-distribution identification results* are not directly usable on my data (they need relative-price variation / transitions I lack) and rest on a universal-set construction my ex-ante constrained object deliberately departs from.

## 6b. Inclusive value and money-metric inversion
- **Inclusive value (log-sum / expected maximum)?** **No.** The paper does **not** use an inclusive-value/log-sum as the welfare core. Welfare is the NOS level of an evaluated bundle (Equation 10–12), and its distribution is obtained from choice/transition probabilities — not from an expected-maximum aggregation. This is a clean contrast to my **ex-ante** object, which *is* the log-sum inclusive value $V_i=\log\sum_j\exp(\cdot)$.
- **Money-metric inversion?** Yes in spirit, but analytic, not a numerical own-utility solve. The MMU is defined implicitly through the expenditure-function representation (Equation 13 and its implicit form), but the paper never needs to numerically invert an own-utility map per household because **Lemma 1 converts the inversion into a choice-probability evaluation at virtual prices**. My pipeline instead performs an explicit **one-dimensional bracketing root-solve** of the household's own-utility map. So: same money-metric concept, different operationalisation (their virtual-price functional vs my numerical inversion).
- **Expectation over the shocks — analytic or simulated?** **Neither, in my sense.** There are no extreme-value shock draws and no analytic log-sum; the heterogeneity is integrated **implicitly** by reading observed choice frequencies (the choice probability *is* the integral over $\omega$). This differs from my analytic-in-shocks log-sum and from a simulation approach alike. Relate to my integrator only as a contrast: I integrate over sampled job nodes with importance weights and take the shock expectation in closed form; they integrate over the unobserved type by nonparametric regression on observed choices.

## 7. Inequality / decomposition content
- **Inequality index:** They use **first-order stochastic dominance** as the primary comparison criterion across groups and between baseline/reform, and report **Atkinson-Kolm-Sen** social welfare for inequality-aversion parameters $\varepsilon\in\{0,0.5,5\}$ (Appendix C.5, Table 4). No Gini/Theil/MLD is reported. Welfare is reported as full **distributions** (CDFs) by wage quartile and chosen alternative.
- **Decomposition rule:** **None of the kind I need.** There is **no source-composition decomposition** (no Shapley, no Shorrocks, no factor-component, no subgroup/Owen decomposition into access/ability/preference or even opportunity/preference). The closest object is the **distributional incidence** analysis: the joint distribution of baseline welfare *level* and welfare *difference* (CV), summarised as iso-percentile contours and a 3×3 winners/losers table (Table 1), answering "are the gainers from a reform disproportionately the initially well-off?" That is an incidence question, not a variance/inequality decomposition.
- **Counterfactual construction:** the counterfactual is a **price/tax change** (progressive schedule → 42% flat tax + basic income, revenue-neutral on the full SOEP), and welfare differences are CV/EV between the two price vectors. Nothing is "equalised", "neutralised", or "zeroed out" in the source-decomposition sense; the exercise holds preferences fixed across the reform (perfect $\omega$-correlation before/after) and changes prices.
- **Order/path-independence/exhaustiveness:** N/A — there is no multi-factor decomposition to which Shapley exhaustiveness or path-independence would apply. They do note that their general welfare-difference object avoids the path-dependence problem that afflicts composing CVs across successive price changes (their general metric-difference is well defined where a sum of CVs is not).
- **Verdict — reusable for my three-way Shapley–Shorrocks split?** **No, not as a decomposition.** This paper supplies **measurement** (welfare levels and differences and their joint distribution) but not a **source decomposition**. To get to my three-channel split I would have to add the entire Shapley–Shorrocks layer the paper does not contain. What *is* reusable: (i) the **joint level × difference** machinery as a *robustness/incidence exhibit* (who-gains-where), and (ii) the use of **first-order dominance** and **Atkinson SWF** as inequality-comparison tools across measures. If I cite it on decomposition at all, I must say it is **not even a two-way decomposition** — it is incidence analysis — and that the three-channel extension is entirely mine.

## 8. Identification and the separation of preferences from opportunities  [STRICT]
- **What identifies the welfare distribution:** **relative price (and exogenous-income) variation** in cross-sectional data identifies the choice probabilities; evaluating those probabilities at **virtual prices** (via Lemma 1) identifies the marginal/conditional welfare-*level* distributions. The **joint** level-difference distributions and CV/EV distributions require **transition probabilities**, which are point-identified only from **panel data (≥2 periods)**; from cross-sections they are merely **set-identified** via Boole-Fréchet inequalities plus a stochastic-revealed-preference shape restriction (Proposition 3), yielding bounds.
- **Tastes vs constraints:** **Not separated, and not separable in this framework.** The paper's design choice is to make the welfare distribution *robust to* unknown preferences by never recovering them — but the flip side is that it attributes all non-price systematic variation to preference heterogeneity and cannot isolate an opportunity/availability component. **Ability vs access within the opportunity side: not addressed at all** (no opportunity side exists). The wage gradient they observe is, in my vocabulary, an ability-correlate, but they do not estimate an ability technology.
- **Source of identification (named):** (i) **functional-form-free** monotonicity in the numeraire (Assumption 1) + exogeneity of budget sets (Assumption 2) + RUM (Assumption 3); (ii) **relative-price variation** in the data; (iii) for joint objects, **panel/transition data** or, failing that, **Boole-Fréchet + stochastic-RP bounds**. No exclusion restriction or external opportunity shifter (local unemployment, vacancies) is used; the wage imputation does use children as exclusion restrictions in a Heckman selection step, but that is for missing wages, not for welfare identification.
- **Transport to my France pooled cross-section:** **Largely does not transport for the point-identification results.** I have a **repeated cross-section, no panel, no external instrument**. The paper's *level* results need rich relative-price variation across individuals (my disposable-income variation exists but my welfare object is constructed differently — ex-ante over a constrained set, not from virtual-price evaluations of nonparametric choice probabilities). The paper's *joint level-difference* results need transitions I cannot point-identify; I would inherit its **bounds**, not its point identification. **My identification backbone is different in kind:** parametric RURO certified by **synthetic recovery**, not nonparametric identification from price variation.
- **Use against the "your decomposition is mechanical" referee:** This paper is the strongest available citation for the claim that **money-metric / NOS welfare *levels* are a well-defined, identifiable object even under unrestricted preference heterogeneity** — i.e. that the welfare-measurement layer is not an artefact of a chosen utility functional. I should cite it for that, while being explicit that it achieves robustness *by not modelling opportunities at all*, which is exactly the trade I refuse to make. Do not soften: the paper buys preference-agnosticism at the price of having no access/ability separation, and my contribution is to pay the parametric price to recover that separation.
- **Synthetic-recovery / parametric-identification note:** the paper relies on **nonparametric identification**, not on synthetic-recovery arguments; this is orthogonal to my certification standard and should be flagged as a difference, not borrowed.

## 9. Key results and magnitudes
All figures explicit-in-source unless flagged.
- Under MMU at actual reference prices, the welfare distribution is a **step function (deterministic welfare) for ≈25%** of the single-female subsample; this concentrates among low-wage individuals who choose FT and high-wage individuals who choose NW.
- **Wage→welfare gradient:** the welfare distribution of **4th-quartile (high-wage) FT** workers first-order dominates PT, which dominates NW; for **1st-quartile (low-wage)** individuals the ordering reverses (with exceptions at the bottom). Aggregating out the chosen alternative, the **highest-wage quartile first-order dominates** the other three, while the lower three quartiles are **intermingled** — read by the authors as evidence that unobserved preferences carry real weight in welfare levels.
- **Reform (flat 42% + basic income):** the reform welfare distribution **first-order dominates** the baseline for the single-female subsample (partly attributable to non-budget-neutrality on this subsample). Despite dominance, **≈15% of single females lose**; losers are concentrated among the **initially well-off**. Among the bottom two-thirds by baseline welfare, **≈98% gain**. About **half gain up to ~200 EUR/month**.
- **Winners/losers table (Table 1, % of population):** losers total **14.6%**; of the loser mass, **≈90%** sits in the initially best-off third; loser rate is **13.1%** in the top baseline-welfare band (2000–4000 EUR) vs **0.9%** (1500–2000) and **0.6%** (800–1500). Moderate gains (0–200 EUR) total 49.4%; large gains (200–1000 EUR) total 35.9%.
- **Social welfare (Atkinson, Table 4, EUR):** baseline 1,815 / 1,783 / 1,530 for $\varepsilon=0/0.5/5$; reform 1,925 / 1,897 / 1,673 — reform higher at every aversion level (consistent with first-order dominance).
- **Benchmarking value for me:** these are *reform-incidence* magnitudes, not opportunity-share magnitudes, so they do **not** benchmark my opportunity share. They *do* give a plausibility anchor for **across-measure / across-group welfare spread** driven by the wage (ability-correlate) dimension, and a template for the order of magnitude of "share of losers concentrated in the top".

## 10. Estimators, theorems, or formal results
For each: statement (near-verbatim LaTeX), assumptions, technique, reusability verdict.

- **NOS welfare measure (Eq. 10 / 12).** $W^\omega(y-p_k,k)=\sup_\lambda\{\lambda \mid U_k^\omega(y-p_k)\ge \max_{(y',c)\in B_\lambda}U_c^\omega(y')\}=\max_\lambda\{\lambda\mid U_k^\omega(y-p_k)\ge \max_c U_c^\omega(y-\tilde p_c(\lambda))\}$. *Assumptions:* A1 (continuity + strict monotonicity in numeraire, with regularity R1/R2), nested family conditions (4)–(7). *Technique:* the sup is attained (closed, bounded, non-empty argument set). *Reusability:* **Yes** — this is the formal definition I cite as the parent class of my $W$ family.
- **Lemma 1 (the pivot).** $\{\omega\mid w\le W^\omega(y-p_k,k)\}=\{\omega\mid U_k^\omega(y-p_k)\ge \max_c U_c^\omega(y-\tilde p_c(w))\}$. *Assumptions:* A1–A2. *Technique:* monotonicity + nestedness give the price-event equivalence. *Reusability:* **Maybe** — conceptually it justifies expressing welfare events as choice events; I do not use it operationally (I invert numerically), but it is the right citation for "welfare-as-choice-at-virtual-prices".
- **Theorem 1 / Corollary 1 (level distributions).** Marginal: $\Pr_\omega[w\le W^\omega(y-p_k,k)]=P_k\big((p_k,\tilde p_{-k}(w)),y\big)\,\mathbb I[p_k\le \tilde p_k(w)]$; optimal-bundle marginal $=\sum_k P_k(\min(\mathbf p,\tilde{\mathbf p}(w)),y)\,\mathbb I[\cdot]$ (Eq. 17–18). *Assumptions:* A1–A3. *Technique:* Lemma 1 + definition of choice probabilities. *Reusability:* **No** for estimation (needs nonparametric choice probabilities at virtual prices, a different engine); **Yes** as a benchmark of what is identifiable.
- **Corollary 2 (MMU degeneracy at reference=actual).** MMU in the optimal bundle equals actual income $y$ when reference prices equal actual prices. *Reusability:* **Yes** as an analogue/sanity reference for my $W^3$ own-set zero-equivalent-income property (flag the ex-ante vs ex-post difference).
- **Theorems 3–4 / Corollaries 3–4 (CV and joint MMU-CV).** CV distribution and joint MMU×CV distribution as transition-probability functionals at actual/post-reform/virtual prices (Eqs. 23, 28). *Assumptions:* A1–A3 + perfect $\omega$-correlation across the price change. *Technique:* Lemma 2 (CV-as-choice-event) + transition probabilities. *Reusability:* **Maybe** — usable for a welfare-*difference* / incidence exhibit only if I can supply/bound transition probabilities; I would be in the set-identified (bounds) regime.
- **Proposition 2 (social welfare from choice probabilities).** $F_W(w\mid \mathbf p,y)=1-\sum_k P_k(\min(\mathbf p,\tilde{\mathbf p}(w)),y)\,\mathbb I[p_k\le\tilde p_k(w)]$, plugged into an additively separable Bergson–Samuelson SWF (Eq. 34). *Reusability:* **Maybe** — the SWF-from-distributions idea transports; the specific choice-probability route does not.
- **Proposition 3 (cross-sectional bounds).** Boole-Fréchet + stochastic-RP bounds on transition probabilities from choice probabilities. *Reusability:* **Maybe/No** — relevant only if I ever try the level-difference object on my cross-section; tells me I would get bounds, not points.
- **Proposition 4 (shape restrictions).** $\partial P_i/\partial p_i\le 0$, $\partial P_i/\partial p_j\ge 0$. *Reusability:* **No** (a property of their nonparametric choice-probability estimator, not of my parametric model).
- **Appendix A.1 (EI and WR metrics).** EI: $U_0^\omega(EI^\omega)=U_k^\omega(y-p_k)$ against a reference option 0 (Eq. 38). WR: implicit in Eq. 46. *Reusability:* **Yes** — EI is the precise formal object behind my equivalent-income framing; cite for the discrete-setting EI definition.

## 11. Robustness and specification sensitivity
What they vary / what is fragile (explicit-in-source where noted):
- **Choice-set size:** fixed at 3 alternatives; the authors note this makes the welfare distribution **coarse** and flag richer alternative sets as future work (concluding remarks). For me: a caution that very small grids coarsen welfare; my 101/901 grids are far richer, but the ESS thinness in my singles is the analogous fragility.
- **Cross-section vs panel:** the central sensitivity — joint level-difference objects degrade from point- to **set-identification** without panel transitions; they report aggregate bounds **at most ~4 percentage points apart** and proceed with the **upper bound** (Appendix C.4). ## 16. TL;DR for retrieval
Capéau–De Sadeleer–Maes–Decoster (2021, CESifo WP 9071) is the general **nonparametric identification** of Fleurbaey-style **NOS welfare levels** (the class containing my money-metric / equivalent-income object, with MMU/EI/WR as worked cases and CV/EV as the difference objects) in discrete-choice RUMs with unrestricted preference heterogeneity, identified from choice (levels) and transition (differences) probabilities, with a German SOEP single-female labour-supply application. It informs my **welfare** and **preference** layers as the formal scaffold and as evidence that the measurement layer can be preference-agnostic, but it has **no opportunity mechanism** (no access, no ability technology, no occupation) and **no source decomposition** — only reform-incidence analysis on a **universal-reference, ex-post-flavoured** welfare level, so it neither supports my access/ability channels nor contains my $W^1$–$W^6$ family, and its point identification largely does not transport to my panel-free France cross-section.
