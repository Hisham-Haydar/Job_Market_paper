## 1. One-paragraph relevance to my JMP
This is the most directly relevant general-theory reference for my welfare layer's discrete-choice money-metric core, because it establishes — under essentially only "numeraire is a normal good" — when the *distribution* of EV/CV across heterogeneous agents is identified from choice probabilities, and (centrally for me) when it is *not*. It speaks to my **ability** and **access** channels only indirectly, through one mechanism: a job's wage enters disposable income additively (a "price/income" object in this paper's language), whereas hours, occupation availability, and market/participation are *characteristics* of alternatives that do not enter as additive components of net income. The paper's Section 3.1 result — that welfare distributions for **characteristic changes** are generically only set-identified (point-identified only under weak separability) — is a direct, explicit-in-source caution for any welfare claim I make about equalising the **access** side, as opposed to the **ability**/pay side. Its log-sum critique (Sections 1, 5) is a citable justification for why I do not rely on a naive log-sum welfare shortcut, though my own ex-ante inclusive-value object is a *parametric, structural* log-sum and is therefore exactly the kind of object this paper warns is identified "solely from functional form" — a tension I must state honestly, not paper over. The paper computes **no** access/ability/preference decomposition and contains **no** $W^1$–$W^6$ family; it is a single-agent (or unitary-consumer) private-welfare-of-a-price-change apparatus.

## 2. Data and setting
- **Country/year/dataset:** US (Southern California) cross-section of **1,182 individuals**, fishing-mode microdata previously analysed in Herriges and Kling (1999); variable description in Kling and Thomson (1996) (explicit, p. 591). Publicly available.
- **Sample unit:** individual consumers (a cross-section of consumers making one discrete choice each).
- **Choice set:** 4 fishing modes — beach, pier, charter boat, private boat (explicit, p. 591).
- **Key variables:** individual monthly income ($), chosen mode, the price of each alternative faced by the individual (primarily transport cost to the site), and an exogenous catch rate (avg fish per hour, from an external source) per option (explicit, pp. 591–592, Table 1).
- **Budget-set construction:** utility is $U_j(Y-P_j,\eta)$; price enters additively through the budget constraint as $Y-P_j$ (explicit, p. 575). There is no tax-benefit budget; "price" is a transport cost, not a wage-derived disposable income.
- **Transport to my setting:** the *theoretical* apparatus transports (it is dataset-agnostic, requiring only choice probabilities estimable on a cross-section, which I have); the *empirical illustration* does **not** transport — it is a leisure-choice toy application, not labour supply, not EUROMOD, not couples. Features the paper has that I do **not**: cross-sectional price variation that is *independent across exclusive alternatives* (the engine of point identification here) — in my latent-jobs setting wage variation is not a freely-varying exclusive-alternative price vector. Features the paper does **not** rely on that I also lack: panel, administrative match, external instrument, vacancy/offer data. The paper explicitly notes its illustration assumes independence between budget sets and preferences (explicit, p. 592).

## 3. Model and objects (map object-by-object to mine)
- **Their choice set vs my latent-jobs set:** their $J$ (or $J+1$) exclusive alternatives are *not* a latent-jobs set; they are a small fixed menu (4 fishing modes) with no opportunity/availability mechanism. **Difference:** no analogue of my sampled-alternatives construction, no proposal density.
- **Their deterministic utility vs my preference utility $v$:** they impose *no* functional form — $U_j(Y-P_j,\eta)$ with $\eta$ of unknown dimension and unknown distribution, continuous and strictly increasing in the numeraire (Assumption 1, p. 575). This is *more general* than my Box–Cox $v$; correspondingly they identify welfare *distributions* without identifying preferences. My $v$ is parametric and estimated.
- **Opportunity / availability mechanism analogous to my $g$:** **none.** There is a common, universal choice set; every consumer faces all alternatives, differing only in the prices/characteristics they face. There is no hours channel, no wage-as-ability technology, no market/participation channel, no occupation availability. This is the single most important structural gap for my purposes: the paper has *no* opportunity layer to map onto my access/ability split.
- **Their budget map vs my EUROMOD disposable income:** their $Y-P_j$ is a raw income-minus-price; mine is EUROMOD `ils_dispy`-derived disposable income at a job package. Price additivity in their utility plays the role my consumption argument plays, but they obtain it from a transport-cost budget, not a tax-benefit simulator.
- **Does any attribute enter BOTH utility and an opportunity mechanism?** N/A — there is no opportunity mechanism, so the double-entry flag cannot arise. However, the paper draws the relevant *conceptual* line I care about: **prices** enter utility additively (via the budget constraint) and yield point-identified welfare; **characteristics** ($x$, e.g. catch rate, bus frequency, teacher-student ratio) do *not* enter as additive income components, and welfare changes in them are generically only set-identified (Section 3.1, Theorem 2, p. 582). Mapping by analogy (derived-by-analogy, **not** explicit-in-source): my **wage/ability** dimension behaves like their *price/income* object (additive in the budget), whereas my **access** dimension — hours availability, occupation offers, participation — behaves like their *characteristic* object. If that analogy holds, the paper predicts the access side of my welfare is the harder-to-identify side. This is an analogy I am drawing, not a claim the paper makes about labour supply.

## 4. Estimation method
- **Likelihood / estimator:** the paper's theoretical results require only *structural choice probabilities* $q_j(\mathbf p,y)$ (defined in eq. (1), p. 575) and are agnostic about how they are estimated. In the illustration, choice probabilities are estimated by a **partially linear series/sieve** specification — a cubic in (income, own price) plus controls and cross-terms (eq. (29), p. 592) — interpreted as a nonparametric approximation; for modest samples a parametric approximation is suggested (explicit, p. 589, p. 592).
- **Choice-set construction:** fixed grid of the 4 real alternatives; **no sampled alternatives**, no proposal density.
- **Prior/proposal correction:** **N/A / not present.** There is no sampling-of-alternatives correction because the full (small) choice set is used. This is a structural absence relative to my pipeline, not a design choice the paper defends.
- **Normalisation / scale:** none of the discrete-choice scale normalisations my parametric model needs arise, because welfare is read off choice-probability *functionals*, not off estimated utility parameters. The paper stresses that no scale, no heterogeneity dimension, and no utility functional form need be pinned down (Section 6, pp. 598–599).
- **What pins preferences separately from opportunities:** **N/A** — there is no opportunity mechanism to separate from preferences; and the paper's entire point is that it *avoids* identifying the preference distribution at all.
- **Verdict — reusable for my RURO/JAX pipeline?** **Partly / as a benchmark, not as the engine.** The closed-form EV/CV-from-choice-probability formulas (Theorem 1, Corollaries 1–2) are reusable as a *nonparametric cross-check* on my structural welfare numbers for the wage/price-like channel — the named step is: estimate my structural choice probabilities, then evaluate eq. (8)/(9) or the elimination corollary against my structural EV/CV for a wage change, to see whether my parametric log-sum welfare is an artefact of functional form. They are **not** reusable as my primary estimator because (a) they need independent cross-sectional price variation across exclusive alternatives that my latent-jobs design does not supply, and (b) they have no opportunity layer.

## 5. Opportunity mechanism  [MOST IMPORTANT — be exhaustive; split by channel]
**There is no explicit opportunity mechanism. State this plainly:** the paper uses a *common, universal* choice set in which every consumer can choose every alternative and differs only in the prices and characteristics attached to alternatives. There is:
- **no access channel** — no hours availability, no participation/employment-probability restriction, no occupation-offer process, no region/year availability shifters;
- **no ability channel** — no wage technology, no returns to education/experience, no residual productivity dispersion $\sigma$;
- **no reservation-wage or offer-probability restriction** generating a household-specific feasible set.

The only feasibility-relevant operation the paper studies is **elimination/introduction of an alternative** (Section 2.2, pp. 579–581): removing alternative $J+1$ from a *common* set for *everyone*. This is a welfare evaluation of a set-contraction, not a model of heterogeneous feasible sets — it changes the set identically for all agents, and is formally treated as raising that alternative's price to infinity (formal justification, Corollary 1, p. 605). It is therefore **not** an analogue of my access channel, which is precisely cross-household variation in feasible sets.

**Map to my three sub-objects:**
- **access** (hours/market/region/year/occupation offers): **absent.** The closest object — alternative elimination — is common across agents, so it carries no cross-sectional access heterogeneity.
- **ability** (wage technology, returns to schooling/experience, $\sigma$): **absent.** Wage-like variation enters only as an exogenous price/income difference, not as an estimated productivity technology.
- **occupation as availability vs something else:** **N/A.** No occupation object exists; **no sector/industry conflation occurs** because neither concept appears.

**Functional form of the (absent) mechanism:** N/A. **Cost of the omission for my decomposition:** because the paper has no opportunity layer, it cannot, on its own, identify or bound the **access** or **ability** components of my decomposition; it can only inform the *evaluation* (welfare-conversion) step for the price/income-like (wage/ability) channel, and warn — via its characteristic-change set-identification result — that the access-like channel may not be point-identified from choice probabilities without further restrictions.

## 6. Welfare object — and its place on my $W^1$–$W^6$ map
- **Does it compute welfare?** Yes — **money-metric**: the Hicksian **compensating variation (CV)** and **equivalent variation (EV)** of an economic change, defined per heterogeneity type $\eta$ as the income compensation equating pre- and post-change indirect utilities (eqs. (2)–(3), p. 576), and recovered as **marginal distributions across the population** (Theorem 1, p. 577).
- **Equivalent income?** Not in the King/Fleurbaey equivalent-income sense I use. It is CV/EV of a *price/quality/menu change*, i.e. money-metric welfare of a **change**, not a reference-indexed equivalent income of a *state*. This is a real conceptual difference from my object.
- **Universal vs constrained feasible set:** **universal/common** set. Welfare is the private value of a change to a consumer who faces the whole menu; there is no constrained feasible set varying by circumstance. My object is explicitly the welfare of a *constrained feasible set* — the opposite stance.
- **Reference:** the reference is the *status-quo situation before the change* (pre-change prices/characteristics/menu), per type $\eta$; there is no menu of normative references. CV references the post-change utility, EV the pre-change utility (standard, eqs. (2)–(3)).
- **Discrete-choice subtleties:** handled by the closed-form choice-probability functionals rather than log-sum aggregation: Marshallian consumer surplus is shown **path-dependent** (hence ill-defined) for simultaneous multiple price changes, while Hicksian CV/EV remain well-defined (Proposition 1, p. 576; Appendix path-dependence demonstration, pp. 599–600). Hicksian vs Marshallian: Hicksian is the object. Integration over unobserved heterogeneity: done by expressing welfare CDFs as transformations of $q_j$, which already integrate out $\eta$ (eq. (1)). **Ex-ante vs ex-post:** this is a key mismatch — the paper's welfare is the value of a change to an agent who *makes a choice* under the change; it is neither my ex-ante inclusive-value-over-the-feasible-set object nor exactly an ex-post realised-job object. It is the distribution, across types, of the income compensation each type would require; the "expected maximum / log-sum" inclusive value is **not** its welfare core (and is in fact criticised, see §6b).
- **Locate on my $W^1$–$W^6$ map:** **does not correspond to any specific $W^k$.** The $W^1$–$W^6$ family is defined by Independence-of-pay (Ind-$y$) and Independence-of-set (Ind-$A$) stances on a reference; this paper has a single status-quo reference and no Ind-$y$/Ind-$A$ taxonomy. **Explicitly:** the paper does **not** contain $W^1$–$W^6$ and takes no Ind-$y$/Ind-$A$ stance. The honest mapping is that it supplies the *general identification theory* sitting underneath any money-metric discrete-choice measure, including each $W^k$'s conversion step, but it is not itself a member of the family.
- **Verdict:** **adaptable, not directly usable.** Adaptable as: (i) a nonparametric identification benchmark for the wage/price-like channel of my measures; (ii) the formal authority for "Hicksian well-defined, Marshallian path-dependent" and for the log-sum critique. Incompatible as a drop-in welfare object because it is a change-CV/EV on a universal set, not a constrained-set ex-ante equivalent income, and has no opportunity layer.

## 6b. Inclusive value and money-metric inversion  [extract if the paper uses a log-sum or an EV/CV]
- **Does the paper use the inclusive value (log-sum) as its welfare core?** **No — and it argues against doing so.** The log-sum (Small–Rosen) formula is presented as the *common practice the paper improves upon*, because it relies on strong parametric assumptions: constant marginal utility of income / quasilinearity (no income effects) and a specified heterogeneity distribution (explicit, pp. 573, 593, and footnote 6 p. 593). The empirical illustration shows the log-sum estimates differ substantially from the paper's nonparametric estimates — smaller, and with an income gradient of the *opposite sign*, reversing the progressivity conclusion (explicit, pp. 575, 593–594; Table 2, p. 597).
- **Is welfare obtained by inverting an own-utility map to money (a 1-D solve), or by a closed-form shortcut?** **Closed-form, bypassing own-preference inversion.** The whole contribution is that EV/CV *distributions* are closed-form functionals of choice probabilities (Theorem 1), requiring no inversion of an individual utility map and no knowledge of utility structure. This is the **opposite** of my design, where each $W^k$ is computed by a one-dimensional bracketing root-solve of the household's *own*-utility map and explicitly forbids a closed-form shortcut that bypasses own preferences.
- **Expectation over EV shocks — analytic or simulated?** Neither in my sense: there is no extreme-value-shock specification to integrate analytically; expectations over heterogeneity $\eta$ are handled implicitly inside the choice-probability functionals (eq. (1)). So the paper is "analytic" only in the trivial sense that it never simulates shocks — but it also never takes a closed-form log-sum expectation, because it deliberately avoids the parametric structure that would license one.
- **Relation to my analytic-in-shocks importance-sampling inversion:** the paper is a useful **contrast/benchmark**: my object is a parametric, analytic-in-shocks log-sum inverted by a 1-D solve; this paper is a nonparametric, choice-probability-functional object that needs no inversion. The cross-check value is real (compare my structural wage-change CV/EV to the paper's nonparametric formula), but the paper would classify my object as one of the "parametric, identified-from-functional-form" measures it cautions about — a caution I should cite *against myself* and answer with my synthetic-recovery certification, not ignore.

## 7. Inequality / decomposition content  [three-way where relevant]
- **Inequality index:** **none.** The paper reports welfare *distributions* (CDFs of EV/CV) and means, plus expected deadweight loss, but computes no Gini/MLD/Theil/Atkinson and no inequality index.
- **Decomposition rule:** **none.** No Shapley, Shorrocks, factor-component, subgroup, RIF, or Owen decomposition appears.
- **Counterfactual construction:** the "counterfactuals" are *policy changes* (multiple price changes; elimination/introduction of an alternative; quality/characteristic change; nonexclusive price changes), not equalisation/neutralisation of circumstance components. Nothing is "zeroed out" in the inequality-decomposition sense.
- **Order/path/exhaustiveness properties:** the paper proves **path-dependence of Marshallian CS** (a negative result) and **uniqueness/well-definedness of Hicksian CV/EV** (Proposition 1) — these are about the welfare measure, not about a decomposition's order-independence.
- **Verdict — reusable for my three-way access/ability/preference Shapley–Shorrocks split?** **No, not as a decomposition.** The paper supplies no decomposition machinery. It is **two-way at most only by analogy** (price/income-like vs characteristic-like), and even that is a *welfare-identification* distinction, not a source decomposition. To use it toward my three-way split I would have to (i) supply the structural opportunity model it lacks, (ii) build the equivalisation counterfactuals it does not contain, and (iii) layer a Shapley–Shorrocks rule on top — none of which is in this paper. Its only contribution to my decomposition section is a caution about which channel (access-like / characteristic) is point-identified vs only bounded.

## 8. Identification and the separation of preferences from opportunities  [STRICT]
- **What identifies welfare here, and from what:** EV/CV **distributions** are identified from **structural choice probabilities** $q_j(\mathbf p,y)$ under **Assumption 1** alone (utility continuous and strictly increasing in the numeraire), with **no** restriction on the dimension or distribution of heterogeneity and **no** utility functional form (explicit, Theorem 1, pp. 577–578; Section 6 summary, pp. 598–599). The identifying variation is **cross-sectional variation in own price and income** (and in the prices of other exclusive alternatives), within the observed support.
- **Tastes vs constraints:** the paper does **not** separate tastes from constraints — it has no constraint/opportunity object, and it deliberately **does not identify the preference distribution at all** (this is sold as an advantage over random-coefficient methods, p. 598). So there is *no* preference/opportunity separation result to transport.
- **Ability vs access within the opportunity side:** **N/A** — no opportunity side. The nearest *identification* statement that bears on my access/ability cut is the **price-vs-characteristic asymmetry**: welfare from additive-in-income **price** changes is point-identified (Theorem 1), but welfare from **characteristic** changes is generically **not** point-identified from choice probabilities (Theorem 2, p. 582), and is only **set-identified / bounded** (Propositions 2–3, Appendix) unless **weak separability** of heterogeneity is imposed, which restores point identification (Proposition 2, p. 584). By the analogy in §3 (derived-by-analogy, **not** explicit-in-source), my **ability/pay** channel is price/income-like (the favourable case) and my **access** channel is characteristic-like (the unfavourable case).
- **A second, sharper identification warning directly usable in my defence:** for **nonexclusive** alternatives, *multiple* simultaneous price changes give welfare distributions that are **not point-identified** from choice probabilities, proved via an explicit **multicollinearity/global-singularity** argument (the regressor vector spans a lower-dimensional subspace, so distinct parameter vectors produce identical choice probabilities yet different welfare; Theorem 3 and counterexample, pp. 586–588, citing Rothenberg (1971) and Böhning (1992)). This is a concrete template for the "your decomposition is mechanical / not identified" referee: it shows that even with correctly specified choice probabilities, welfare objects can fail to be identified when the relevant design matrix is collinear. My job-package attributes (consumption, hours, wage moving together within a job) are exactly the kind of co-moving regressors that can induce such collinearity, so I should pre-empt this.
- **Transport to my France pooled cross-section (no panel, currently no external instrument):** the *favourable* result (price/income-channel point identification) transports **only** to the extent that I have independent cross-sectional variation in the wage/income dimension across alternatives — which in a latent-jobs design I largely do **not**, because wages are not freely-varying exclusive-alternative prices. The *unfavourable* results (characteristic-change set-identification; nonexclusive multicollinearity) transport as **cautions** that bite on my access channel and on co-moving job attributes. Net: the paper strengthens my identification note by giving a precise, citable account of *when* money-metric discrete-choice welfare is and is not identified nonparametrically — and most of my opportunity content falls on the "not nonparametrically identified without further structure" side, which is exactly why my baseline is **parametric and certified by synthetic recovery, not by in-sample fit**. I should cite this paper as the reason that certification standard is necessary, not as a result that rescues nonparametric identification for my case.

## 9. Key results and magnitudes
All magnitudes are from the fishing-mode illustration (US, 1,182 individuals), at mean monthly income $4,000 unless noted (Table 2, p. 597); units are dollars per month of welfare loss (CV); "logsum" = Small–Rosen comparison.
- **Multiple price change** (beach & pier price fall 75th→25th pct; private-boat price rise 25th→75th pct): mean CV (cubic) **$42.57**, bootstrap CI [40.0, 43.1]; logsum **$19.21**. The paper's estimate is ~2× the logsum and **increases** with income, whereas the logsum **decreases** with income (explicit, pp. 593–594; Fig. 1).
- **Eliminate private boat:** mean CV (cubic) **$536.37**, CI [292.31, 705.08]; logsum **$1,212.4** (i.e. logsum ~2× larger here) (Table 2).
- **Eliminate charter boat:** mean CV (cubic) **$588.16**, CI [582.4, 591.8]; logsum **$280.29** (cubic ~2× the logsum) (Table 2). Elimination losses ≈ 13% of mean monthly income; signs of the income gradient are *opposite* between methods (explicit, pp. 594–596).
- **Decrease catch-rate of beach** (quality/characteristic change, 4th→1st quartile, 0.53→0.06): upper-bound mean welfare loss **$7.05**, CI [4.55, 11.91]; logsum **$4.68** (Table 2). This is a *bounds* result (lower bound zero; upper bound ≈ $7.50 over most of the income range), reflecting set-identification of characteristic changes (explicit, p. 596; Fig. 4). The small magnitude is interpreted as: the chosen-mode experience, not the catch quantity, is what is valued (p. 598).
- **Headline qualitative finding for me:** parametric log-sum and nonparametric estimates can differ in **magnitude (~2×)** *and in the sign of the income gradient*, reversing the **progressivity** conclusion (explicit, pp. 575, 593–596). **Benchmark use:** this is the order of magnitude of error I should expect my parametric log-sum welfare could carry if its functional form is wrong — motivation for my IS-vs-direct and synthetic-recovery gates, not a number comparable to any of my opportunity shares (the application is unrelated to labour supply).

## 10. Estimators, theorems, or formal results
For each: near-verbatim statement (LaTeX), assumptions, technique, reusability verdict.

**Assumption 1** (p. 575). *For each $\eta$ and each $j=1,\dots,J$, $U_j(\cdot,\eta)$ is continuous and strictly increasing.* — Technique: this is the sole substantive restriction (non-satiation in numeraire); heterogeneity dimension/distribution unrestricted. Reusable: **yes**, my Box–Cox $v$ satisfies monotonicity in consumption, so the well-definedness results apply to my measures in principle.

**Proposition 1** (p. 576). *Under Assumption 1, for a price change $\mathbf p_0\to\mathbf p_1$ the individual CV and EV are uniquely defined.* — Technique (3–5 bullets): RHS of the defining equation is continuous in the compensation $S$ (continuity of $U$); strictly decreasing in $S$ (strict monotonicity); intermediate value theorem gives existence; strict monotonicity gives uniqueness; Marshallian CS shown path-dependent separately. Reusable: **yes** — authority that my equivalent-income solve has a unique root given monotone $v$.

**Theorem 1** (pp. 577–578). *Under Assumption 1, for an arbitrary multiple price change satisfying the ordering (5), the marginal CDFs of individual EV and CV are closed-form functionals of the structural choice probabilities $q_k$* (eqs. (8)–(9)). — Technique: order alternatives by size of price change; slice the population by which alternative is chosen post-change; use monotonicity to convert utility-comparison events into choice-probability evaluations at shifted price/income arguments; separate $a\ge0$/$a<0$ branches to accommodate an outside good at price 0. Reusable: **maybe / as cross-check** — usable to nonparametrically benchmark my structural wage-change CV/EV, *if* I can construct the requisite price/income variation; not my primary welfare engine.

**Corollaries 1–2** (pp. 605, 579–581). *Welfare of eliminating alternative $J+1$ equals the limit of the price-change EV/CV as that alternative's price $\to\infty$; with no cross-price effects the formulas simplify (eqs. (14)–(17)); mean EV equals the integral of the eliminated alternative's choice probability from its price to $\infty$ (eq. (17)).* — Technique: dominated-convergence argument as $p'\to\infty$ (DCT, p. 605); change of variable. Reusable: **limited** — set-elimination is common-set, not heterogeneous-feasible-set; informs how I might value a *common* menu contraction but not my cross-household access variation. The "integrate choice prob to infinity = mean EV of a new good" justification (eq. (17)) is a citable clarification of an ad hoc practice.

**Theorem 2** (p. 582). *Under Assumption 2, the marginal distribution of the CV from a characteristic change is **not identified** from structural choice probabilities (proof by counterexample, Appendix).* — Technique: construct two heterogeneity distributions $G_1,G_2$ with equal means → identical choice probabilities (eq. (41)) but different $\Pr(S=0)$ (eq. (43)). Reusable: **yes, defensively** — the core caution for my **access**-side welfare claims (characteristic-like channel).

**Proposition 2** (p. 584). *Under weak separability $U_1(y-p,x,\eta)\equiv V_1(h_1(y-p,x),\eta)$ and Condition S, the CV distribution for a characteristic change is point-identified.* — Technique: the separable index collapses (price, characteristic) into a scalar $h_1$, restoring a price-change-type argument; index restriction is a special case. Reusable: **maybe** — tells me what additional structural restriction (a separable index in the access dimension) would restore point identification, i.e. exactly the role my parametric $g$ plays; a way to frame my parametric assumption as the separability that buys identification.

**Theorem 3** (p. 587). *For nonexclusive alternatives, single-price-change EV/CV is a closed-form functional of choice probabilities, but multiple simultaneous price changes are **not point-identified**.* — Technique: exact multicollinearity (regressors span a 3-dim subspace), global singularity of $E(W\otimes XX')$, Rothenberg (1971) non-identification, plus a constructive thought experiment. Reusable: **yes, defensively** — template for collinearity-based non-identification when job attributes co-move.

**Endogeneity result** (Section 4.1, pp. 589–591). *For a price increase, the income-conditioned **EV** distribution is unaffected by income endogeneity (CV is); for a price fall, EV/CV reverse. Hence meaningful welfare analysis is possible without an instrument/control function, using EV at realised income, provided price is exogenous conditional on income and covariates.* — Technique: the income-conditioned structural choice probability $q^c$ equals the observed conditional choice probability when $P\perp\eta\mid Y$, so the EV expression needs no counterfactual income shift (eqs. (27)–(28), p. 590). Reusable: **maybe, with strong caveats** — I have no panel and currently no instrument, so a result that some welfare object survives income endogeneity is attractive; but it is about *price/income* exogeneity for a *price change*, and my analogue would be wage exogeneity conditional on covariates — a strong, separate assumption I should not silently inherit.

## 11. Robustness and specification sensitivity
- **What they vary:** the choice-probability approximation — cubic vs quadratic vs linear spline (explicit, p. 593): the two substantive features (cubic ≈ 2× logsum; opposite income gradient) **survive** these changes. Logsum specifications with and without income as a covariate are both compared (p. 596).
- **What breaks / what is fragile:** the **quality/characteristic-change** welfare is only **bounded**, not point-identified (the one case where the bootstrap CI contains the logsum estimate, p. 598); identification of *tails* of welfare distributions requires observing demand at prices where aggregate demand hits zero — typically unobserved, so only a **lower bound** is available there (explicit, p. 574, p. 591). For new-good/elimination integrals, the upper integration limit is set to the highest observed price → estimates are **lower bounds** (p. 594).
- **Informative for my stress-tests:** (i) my parametric log-sum welfare should be stress-tested against a flexible alternative because functional form can move both magnitude *and* sign of the income gradient — this maps to my IS-vs-direct cross-check and the draw-growth stability gate; (ii) **effective support** matters — the paper's tail/bounds problems are the nonparametric analogue of my **effective-sample-size** concern (thin IS weights for singles), both being "the data do not cover the region the welfare integral needs"; (iii) the catch-rate (characteristic) case being the only one where methods agree warns me that my *access*-side numbers may be the least robust and should carry the widest reported uncertainty.

- That **welfare distributions for changes in non-price *characteristics* are generically only set-identified** from choice probabilities, with **weak separability** restoring point identification (Theorem 2; Proposition 2) — my citation for why the access/characteristic side is harder and why a structural restriction is needed.
- That **welfare in the nonexclusive multiple-change case is non-identified via exact multicollinearity** (Theorem 3) — my citation for a collinearity-based non-identification risk when job attributes co-move.
- That the **log-sum (Small–Rosen) welfare formula rests on quasilinearity/constant marginal utility of income and a specified heterogeneity distribution**, and that relaxing these can change welfare magnitudes ~2× and reverse the income gradient / progressivity sign (Sections 1, 5; Table 2; Fig. 1) — my motivation for not trusting a naive log-sum and for certifying my structural object.
- That **for a price rise the income-conditioned EV is robust to income endogeneity** even absent instruments (Section 4.1) — citable with the caveats in §10.

## 16. TL;DR for retrieval
