## 1. One-paragraph relevance to my JMP
This is a direct structural ancestor of my RURO / latent-jobs choice probability and is the canonical statement (with the companion Dagsvik–Strøm 1995 manuscript) of the model my engine implements. Explicit-in-source: it factorises the choice probability into a deterministic utility term $\psi(\cdot)$ and an **opportunity density** $g(h,w)$ over hours-and-wage job packages, with separate channels for hours availability, wage offers, and the market-vs-non-market margin — the same channel structure my $g$ uses. Its wage-offer density (returns to schooling and experience) is the **ability** sub-block in my vocabulary; its hours-availability and market-participation densities are the **access** sub-block; its $v(C,h)$ is my **preference** block. It computes household-level money-metric welfare (equivalent variation) from indirect random utility, the closest 1995-era analogue to my equivalent-income object, and reports its distribution and Gini — so it informs both my welfare layer and the plausibility of welfare-vs-income divergence. It does **not** perform an opportunity/preference (or three-way) inequality decomposition, so it is an estimation-and-welfare ancestor, not a decomposition precedent.

## 2. Data and setting
- **Country / year:** Norway, 1979 (the estimation year and pre-reform base); reforms compared are a revenue-neutral proportional wage-income tax, the 1992 tax rules, and lump-sum taxes.
- **Dataset:** Norwegian labour-market and tax-return data for 1979 (the source describes "Norwegian labor market and tax return data from 1979 for married couples"; finer dataset provenance is in Dagsvik & Strøm 1995, not reproduced here) [verify exact data source].
- **Sample unit:** married couples (the estimated model); single-person households are used for expository derivation and for the welfare formalism. Sample size: **not reported in this paper** (estimation details deferred to Dagsvik & Strøm 1995) — not-established here.
- **Key variables:** consumption $C$ (set equal to disposable income), annual hours $h$ by gender, gross/after-tax income via the tax function $f(wh,I)$, non-labour income $I$, age $A_k$, children under 6 ($CU6$) and over 6 ($CO6$), years of schooling $S_k$, experience $\mathrm{Exp}_k=A_k-S_k-6$.
- **Budget-set construction:** $C=f(wh,I)$ with $f$ derived from 1979 (and 1992) tax-and-benefit rules; deductions accounted for; explicitly **non-convex** budget sets allowed (non-monotone effective marginal taxes, joint taxation of spouses, benefit rules).
- **Transport to my France pooled 2015–2017 EUROMOD cross-section:** partial. Shared: married-couple joint unit; consumption = disposable income; tax-benefit budget map (my EUROMOD plays the role of their $f$); a discrete job-package choice set with an opportunity density. **Features they have that I do not / differ:** a single cross-section (1979) rather than pooled years; no harmonised multi-country microsimulation; their hours-availability density is specified directly from institutional peaks (full-time/part-time), whereas my access sub-block carries region/urbanisation/year/occupation shifters. **Features I do not have anywhere:** no panel, no administrative match, no external opportunity instrument, no vacancy/offer data — and this paper does **not** supply any of those either (it is also a single cross-section with no external instrument).

## 3. Model and objects (object-by-object map)
- **Choice set = my latent-jobs set?** Yes, in kind. Their alternatives are "job packages" indexed by $(h,w)$ plus an unobserved opportunity index $j\in B_i(h,w)$; $B_i(0,0)$ is the set of non-market opportunities. This is the latent-jobs primitive my model inherits. Difference: they work with a continuum/large count of latent jobs collapsed via the opportunity density, not a fixed sampled grid of 101/901 alternatives; my engine samples alternatives and corrects with $-\log\pi$ (see §4b).
- **Deterministic utility = my preference utility $v$?** Yes. Eq. (2): $U_i = v(f(wh,I),h)\,\varepsilon_{ij}(h,w)$, with $v$ deterministic, positive, quasi-concave in $(C,h)$, increasing in $C$, decreasing in $h$. The empirical $v$ (eq. 16) is a Box–Cox-type form in consumption and in normalised leisure $L_k=1-h_k/8760$, with age polynomials and children terms shifting leisure tastes. This is structurally my Box–Cox preference block (consumption + leisure + demographic taste-shifters). Difference: their multiplicative random utility $v\cdot\varepsilon$ vs my additive-in-logs index; equivalent once logged.
- **Explicit opportunity / availability mechanism analogous to my $g$?** Yes, and it is the paper's defining feature. Eq. (9)–(11) define a conditional **opportunity density** $g(h,w)$ ("mean of the fraction of feasible jobs with hours $h$ and wage rate $w$") and a scalar $g_0$ (mean fraction of feasible opportunities that are job opportunities, i.e. the market-vs-non-market margin). It separates:
  - **hours** availability — $g_{1k}(h_k)$, eqs. (17)–(18);
  - **wage (ability)** availability — lognormal wage-offer density with Mincer mean, eq. (19);
  - **market / participation** — $g_0$ and the gendered $g_{0k}$, $g_{0FM}$ terms (eqs. 11, 14–15).
  - **occupation** — **N/A**; there is no occupation/sector channel. Job heterogeneity beyond $(h,w)$ enters only through the unobserved index $j$ and the taste shifter $\varepsilon_{ij}$, not an estimated occupation-availability term.
- **Budget map = my EUROMOD disposable income?** Functionally yes: $C=f(wh,I)$ from tax-benefit rules; "Household consumption is assumed to be equal to disposable income" — the same consumption=disposable-income lock my build uses.
- **Does any job attribute enter BOTH utility and the opportunity mechanism?** **Flag:** *hours and wage enter both.* $(h,w)$ are arguments of $v$ (through $C=f(wh,I)$ and $h$) **and** are the arguments of the opportunity density $g(h,w)$. Explicit-in-source, the authors state that this is exactly why an identifying assumption on the hours-offer density is needed: "Unless this or analogous assumptions are made, it is not possible to separate some of the structural coefficients ... of the utility function from the parameters of the opportunity densities." So the dual appearance is acknowledged and the separation is justified on **functional-form / institutional** grounds (regulated hours peaks), not via an exclusion restriction or external shifter. This is the model's central identification fragility and is directly relevant to my own preference-vs-opportunity separation defence (§8).

## 4. Estimation method
- **Likelihood / estimator:** the choice probabilities (eqs. 9, 12, 14) are multinomial-logit-type expressions in $\psi(\cdot)\,g(\cdot)$; estimation uses **a procedure suggested by McFadden (1978)** that "yields results that are close to the full maximum likelihood method" — i.e. a sampling-of-alternatives ML approximation. The utility parameters and the opportunity-density parameters are **jointly** estimated. Full estimation detail is deferred to Dagsvik & Strøm (1995).
- **Choice-set construction:** conceptually a large/continuum latent-job set summarised by the opportunity density; the hours dimension is treated with peaks at full-time (and part-time for women) over an otherwise uniform offered-hours density. Grid size / number of sampled alternatives: **not reported here** — not-established in this source.
- **Proposal / sampling density:** the McFadden (1978) reference implies a sampling-of-alternatives scheme, but the explicit proposal density and its correction term are **not written out in this paper** (deferred to Dagsvik & Strøm 1995). See §4b.
- **Prior/proposal correction:** **not explicit-in-source.** This paper does not display a $-\log(\text{prior})$ term subtracted from a choice index; the opportunity density $g$ itself plays the role of the alternative-weighting object, but whether a McFadden-style sampling correction is applied is not stated here. Mark $-\log\pi$ presence: **[verify in Dagsvik & Strøm 1995].**
- **Normalisation / scale:** the Type-I-extreme-value / Fréchet structure is fixed by eq. (7), $P(\varepsilon_{ij}\le y)=\exp(-1/y)$ (a unit-Fréchet taste shifter), tied to an IIA property; opportunity-density hours parameters $d_F,d_M$ are normalised so the densities sum to unity. No additional scale normalisation is described in-source.
- **Numerical method / starting values / multistart:** not reported here — not-established.
- **What pins preferences apart from the opportunity mechanism:** the institutional assumption that **offered hours and wages are independently distributed**, plus the hours-offer peak structure (full-time/part-time), is what the authors invoke to separate structural utility coefficients from opportunity-density parameters (explicit-in-source).
- **Verdict — reusable for my RURO/JAX pipeline?** **Yes, as the conceptual template** (the $\psi\cdot g$ factorisation, the hours/wage/market channel split, the multiplicative-EV closed form). **No, as a turnkey estimator** — the actual likelihood, sampling scheme, and any proposal correction live in Dagsvik & Strøm (1995), not here, so this paper cannot be cited for the sampling-of-alternatives correction step my engine needs.

## 4b. Proposal / sampling-of-alternatives correction
- **Explicit-in-source:** estimation "is based on a procedure suggested by McFadden (1978)" yielding results close to full ML. McFadden (1978) is the residential-location sampling-of-alternatives paper, so this is a McFadden-style scheme — but the paper does **not** display the correction.
- **Individualised vs common proposal:** the **wage**-offer density is individualised via schooling and experience (eq. 19), so the wage channel of any proposal is covariate-dependent; the **hours**-offer density (eqs. 17–18) is common up to gendered full-time/part-time peaks, i.e. not household-individualised. This matches my proposal-individualisation pattern (wage individualised; hours/employment common) at the channel level — derived-by-analogy, since they do not frame these as a *proposal* but as the *structural* opportunity density.
- **Per-alternative log-prior:** not shown in-source. **[verify].**
- **Relation to my importance-sampling welfare integrator:** their welfare simulation (§V) draws the residual shocks $\theta_i(h,w)$ from the unit-Fréchet (eq. 21) and maximises over $(h,w)$, i.e. a **simulated-argmax** welfare computation, *not* an analytic-in-shocks importance-sampling inversion. This is a substantive contrast with my analytic log-sum core (see §6b).

## 5. Opportunity mechanism  [MOST IMPORTANT — by channel]
Explicit-in-source. Feasibility of jobs is modelled as a **density over latent job alternatives**, not as offer probabilities or a reservation-wage rule. Each agent faces an individual feasible set $B_i(h,w)$ of jobs with hours $h>0$, wage $w>0$, and $B_i(0,0)$ of non-market opportunities. Let $n_i(h,w)$ be the count of jobs at $(h,w)$; $\tilde g(h,w)$ is the mean fraction, normalised to the conditional opportunity density $g(h,w)$ (eq. 10) and a market-margin scalar $g_0$ (eq. 11). The choice probability (eq. 9) is

$$\varphi(h,w)=\frac{\psi(h,w)\,g_0\,g(h,w)}{\psi(0,0)+g_0\sum_{x>0}\sum_{y>0}\psi(x,y)\,g(x,y)},$$

with the non-participation probability $\varphi(0,0)$ given by eq. (12). Mapping to my three sub-objects:

- **access** (hours / market / region / year / occupation offers):
  - *hours availability* — eqs. (17)–(18): $\log g_{1F}(h_F)=d_F+\alpha_{19}D_1(h_F)+\alpha_{20}D_2(h_F)$ (full-time and part-time peak dummies) and $\log g_{1M}(h_M)=d_M+\alpha_{18}D_1(h_M)$ (full-time peak only). Offered hours are uniform apart from these peaks; the uniform case is stated to coincide with the textbook free-choice-of-hours model.
  - *market / participation* — $g_0$, $g_{0F}$, $g_{0M}$, and the couple term $g_{0FM}$ (eqs. 11, 14–15), with an interaction $\alpha_{17}$ allowing the spouses' opportunity sets to be **correlated** (justified by a shared local labour market). Estimated $\alpha_{17}=-1.841$ ($t=4.6$), i.e. the sets are not independent.
  - *region / year / occupation* — **N/A.** No regional, temporal (single cross-section), or occupation availability shifters. This is a channel my access block has and theirs does not.
  - *covariate dependence of access* — the **female** market-opportunity term depends on **education** ($\log(g_{0FM}/g_{0M})=\alpha_{14}+\alpha_{15}s_F$, with $\alpha_{15}=0.208$, $t=3.0$); no analogous male education effect was found/retained. So access is circumstance-dependent only through female schooling.
- **ability** (the wage technology): eq. (19), $E\log W_k=\beta_0+\beta_1 s_k+\beta_2\mathrm{Exp}_k+\beta_3(\mathrm{Exp}_k)^2\times10^{-2}$, a lognormal wage-offer density with a Mincer mean in schooling and experience, estimated separately by gender (Table 2: education coefficient $\approx0.045$ M / $0.047$ F; residual standard error $0.101$ M / $0.172$ F). This is exactly my ability sub-block (returns to education and experience plus residual productivity dispersion $\sigma$).
- **occupation:** **N/A** — no occupation-as-availability object; no sector/industry conflation to flag (the paper simply has no such channel). Job qualitative heterogeneity is carried entirely by the unobserved $j$ and $\varepsilon_{ij}$.
- **Functional form:** multiplicative random utility $U=v\cdot\varepsilon$ with unit-Fréchet $\varepsilon$ (eq. 7) generating the logit-type probability; opportunity density log-linear in hours-peak dummies (hours), lognormal (wage), and scalar/interaction terms (market margin).
- **Cost of any omission for my decomposition:** they **do** have an explicit opportunity mechanism, so the "universal-set" cost does not apply. The relevant gap for me is the absence of region/year/occupation access shifters and the single-cross-section design, which means this paper cannot demonstrate identification of access *variation across circumstances* of the kind my decomposition leans on.

## 6. Welfare object — and its place on my $W^1$–$W^6$ map
- **Does it compute welfare?** Yes. The welfare object is **equivalent variation (EV)** at the **household** level (§II, §V). Explicit-in-source: EV is "the amount of money that has to be added to the household's disposable income under the initial tax rules in order to make the household indifferent between the initial and the alternative tax system."
- **Money-metric / equivalent income?** Money-metric, EV form (a compensating/equivalent-variation money metric, not a King-style equivalent income indexed to a reference price vector). They explicitly cite Auerbach (1985), Hausman (1984), King (1987) on money metrics and Kay (1980) for preferring EV.
- **Universal vs constrained feasible set?** Constrained/feasible: EV is derived from the indirect random utility $\tilde V_i(y,f)$ (eq. 20, 22) that maximises over the **feasible** job set $B_i(h,w)$, i.e. it respects the opportunity density. So welfare is over a constrained feasible set, like mine.
- **Reference:** the reference is the **initial tax system** $f_0$ (the 1979 rules); EV solves $\tilde V_i(0,f_1)=\tilde V_i(\mathrm{EV},f_0)$ (eq. 24). The reference is a *policy state*, not a reference price/preference/opportunity-set in the responsibility-sensitive sense. This is the key contrast with my family.
- **Discrete-choice subtleties:** handled by **simulation**. The residual term $e_i(h,w)=g_0 g(h,w)\,\theta_i(h,w)$ with $\theta_i$ i.i.d. unit-Fréchet (eq. 21) is **drawn**, and EV is computed from the simulated indirect utility (eqs. 22, 24); the opportunity density $g_0 g(\cdot)$ is held **fixed** across the reform (partial-equilibrium, "supply creates its own demand"). It is **ex post in the shocks via simulation** but **ex ante over the feasible set** in that it integrates over $B_i$ through the maximisation. They follow the **mean** of the EV distribution (after Haneman 1982) as the summary, and report its full distribution and Gini.
- **Locate on my $W^1$–$W^6$ map:** **the paper does NOT use, name, or construct $W^1$–$W^6$.** Its EV is a tax-reform compensation metric referenced to a policy baseline, not a point on my Ind-$y$/Ind-$A$ compensation–responsibility menu. The closest *derived-by-analogy* correspondence is that EV, like all my measures, reads attained (indirect) utility over the feasible set; but it encodes **no** responsibility stance about pay vs feasible set, so it does not map to any specific $W^k$. Recording it as "$W$-anything" would be an overclaim.
- **Verdict:** **adaptable, not directly usable.** The machinery (indirect random utility over a feasible set → money metric by inversion) is reusable; the *object* (EV against a policy baseline) is not my equivalent-income family and must not be presented as such.

## 6b. Inclusive value and money-metric inversion
- **Inclusive value as welfare core?** **No, not analytically.** Although the model has a logit structure with a closed-form expected maximum available in principle, this paper computes welfare by **simulating** the Fréchet shocks $\theta_i(h,w)$ (eq. 21) and maximising (eq. 22), then inverting for EV (eq. 24). It does **not** take the expectation over shocks analytically; it draws them. This is the explicit contrast with my analytic-in-shocks log-sum.
- **Inversion vs shortcut?** Inversion: EV is obtained by solving $\tilde V_i(0,f_1)=\tilde V_i(\mathrm{EV},f_0)$ — a one-dimensional money solve against the household's own indirect utility — not a closed-form shortcut that bypasses preferences. This matches my one-dimensional root-solve in spirit (but on a simulated rather than analytic utility).
- **Expectation analytic or simulated?** **Simulated** (explicit-in-source: shocks generated via eq. 21 for the policy experiments).
- **Relation to my integrator:** same inversion logic, opposite treatment of the shock expectation (their simulation vs my analytic log-sum + importance sampling). Citable as a precedent for *household-level money-metric inversion from random indirect utility over a feasible set*; **not** citable as analytic-in-shocks.

- **Inequality index:** **Gini coefficient**, reported for gross income, disposable income, and the EV (welfare-gain) distribution (Tables 5A, 6).
- **Decomposition rule:** **N/A — no factor/Shapley/subgroup decomposition is performed.** The paper reports inequality *levels* of separate distributions (gross, disposable, EV) under each tax regime and contrasts them; it does not attribute inequality to components.
- **Counterfactual construction:** counterfactuals are **tax-policy** counterfactuals (1979 → proportional / 1992 / lump-sum), not equalisation of a circumstance. What is held fixed: the opportunity density $g_0 g(\cdot)$ (partial equilibrium). What changes: the budget function $f$. Nothing is "zeroed out" in a decomposition sense.
- **Order/path-independence / exhaustiveness:** **N/A** (no decomposition).
- **Verdict — reusable for my three-way access/ability/preference Shapley–Shorrocks split?** **No, not as a decomposition method.** It is neither two-way nor three-way; it has no source decomposition at all. To reach my three-channel split one would have to add, entirely from outside this paper, the equalisation counterfactuals (equalise access; equalise ability; equalise preference) and a Shapley aggregation — none of which is present. Citable only for the *finding* that welfare (EV) inequality can diverge sharply from income inequality (see §9), which motivates a decomposition, not for how to do one.

## 8. Identification and the separation of preferences from opportunities  [STRICT]
- **What identifies tastes vs constraints:** explicit-in-source, the separation rests on a **functional-form / institutional restriction on the hours-offer density**: offered hours are assumed uniform apart from full-time/part-time peaks (eqs. 17–18), and offered hours and wages are assumed **independently distributed** (justified by centrally regulated Scandinavian working hours). The authors state plainly that without such an assumption the structural utility coefficients cannot be separated from the opportunity-density parameters, and that **full identification of $g_0 g(\cdot)$ and $\psi(\cdot)$ "can only be fully solved when additional assumptions about the distribution of offered hours are made"** (citing Dagsvik & Strøm 1995).
- **Ability vs access within the opportunity side:** identified only by functional-form separation — the wage (ability) density is a parametric lognormal Mincer object (eq. 19), the hours/market (access) densities are the peak/scalar objects (eqs. 17–18, 11). There is **no** exclusion restriction or external shifter distinguishing them; the separation is purely parametric.
- **Source of identification:** **distributional assumptions on unobservables** (unit-Fréchet, IIA) + **functional-form restrictions** on the offer densities. **Not** panel, **not** an external opportunity instrument (local unemployment/vacancies), **not** choice-set variation.
- **Transport to my France pooled cross-section (no panel, no external instrument):** the *mechanism* transports — I likewise lean on functional-form + distributional assumptions, not on an instrument. But this paper is therefore a precedent for **parametric / assumption-based identification**, which is exactly the posture a "your decomposition is mechanical" referee attacks. It does **not** provide an instrument I could borrow, and its own authors flag the separation as only fully solvable under extra offered-hours assumptions. For my identification note this is the honest reading: a respected ancestor identifies the same way I do, *and concedes the fragility*. My synthetic-recovery certification is the appropriate substitute standard (this paper offers no synthetic-recovery argument — not-established here).

## 9. Key results and magnitudes
All explicit-in-source, Norway 1979, married couples (and the gendered components):
- **Labour-supply elasticities (Table 3, "exact aggregate"):** direct unconditional Slutsky elasticity of **0.33 (males)** and **1.59 (females)**; utility-constant (cross-effects netted) **0.22 (males)** and **1.31 (females)**. Elasticities **decline sharply with income** and are much higher for females, especially at middle/upper incomes. Participation elasticities exceed conditional-hours elasticities.
- **Wage-offer density (Table 2):** education coefficient $\approx0.045$ (M) / $0.047$ (F); experience $0.022$ (M) / $0.008$ (F); residual s.e. $0.101$ (M) / $0.172$ (F); $R^2\approx0.23$ both.
- **Proportional-tax reform (revenue-neutral; rate found = 20.1%):** participation rises $\approx$ +10pp (F) / +6pp (M); hours given participation +38% (F) / +31% (M); gross and disposable household income +53% / +75%. Mean EV = **NOK 25,956** (1979 prices) $=63.8\%$ of 1979 tax revenue; **99.4% of households gain**, 0.6% lose.
- **Distribution of gains:** Gini of EV (proportional reform) = **0.303** vs 0.472 for the 1992 reform (Table 5A); among the top-gaining decile mean EV NOK 45,400 (1979 gross income NOK 182,800) vs NOK 1,600 (NOK 84,500) for the least-gaining decile — **gains rise strongly with pre-reform income**.
- **Income inequality (Table 6, Gini):** disposable income Gini **falls** from 0.161 (1979) to 0.152 (proportional), while the **welfare** gains favour the rich — the paper's central tension: lower income inequality, regressive welfare incidence, because the poor have higher labour-supply elasticities and "work much harder," nearly offsetting their consumption gain in utility terms.
- **Lump-sum benchmark (Table 7):** 1979 female labour supply = 48.2% of non-distortive potential, male 72.2%; proportional taxes raise these to 76.0% / 94.5%.
- **Benchmark value for my work:** the **divergence between income-inequality movement and welfare-incidence** is the directly relevant magnitude — it shows a money-metric welfare object can reorder the distributional story relative to disposable income, which is the kind of effect my opportunity-sensitive welfare measures are built to surface. Their EV-Gini levels (0.30–0.47 on *gains*) are not directly comparable to my welfare-*level* Ginis.

## 10. Estimators, theorems, or formal results
For each formal object (statements in LaTeX, near-verbatim from the source):

1. **Multiplicative random utility (eq. 2):** $U_i(f(wh,I),h,j)=v(f(wh,I),h)\,\varepsilon_{ij}(h,w)$, with $v$ deterministic, quasi-concave, increasing in $C$, decreasing in $h$. *Technique:* separate systematic utility from a job-specific random taste shifter known to the agent, random to the econometrician. *Reusability:* **yes** — this is my preference $\times$ opportunity factorisation in multiplicative form.
2. **Unit-Fréchet taste shifter (eq. 7):** $P(\varepsilon_{ij}(h,w)\le y)=\exp(-1/y)$, stated to follow from an IIA property; shifters i.i.d. and independent of the choice sets $\{B_i(h,w)\}$. *Reusability:* **yes/maybe** — equivalent to the EV structure underlying my logit core; I use the additive-Gumbel form, the log of this.
3. **Choice probability with opportunity density (eq. 9):** $\varphi(h,w)=\dfrac{\psi(h,w)\,g_0 g(h,w)}{\psi(0,0)+g_0\sum_{x>0}\sum_{y>0}\psi(x,y)g(x,y)}$, with $g(h,w)$ the conditional opportunity density (eq. 10) and $g_0$ the market-margin scalar (eq. 11); non-participation $\varphi(0,0)$ (eq. 12). *Assumptions:* large mean number of jobs $\sum n_i$; IIA; shifters independent of choice sets. *Reusability:* **yes** — the structural form my engine implements (preference term $\times$ opportunity density, normalised).
4. **Couples extension (eqs. 13–15):** household utility $U_i(C,h_F,h_M,j_F,j_M)=v(C,h_F,h_M)\,\varepsilon_{ij_Fj_M}(\cdot)$; joint choice probability $\varphi(h_F,h_M,w_F,w_M)$ with a couple market term $g_{0FM}$ permitting **correlated** spouse opportunity sets (shared local labour market). *Reusability:* **yes** — direct precedent for my couples-as-joint-unit with a joint opportunity object; my 901-alternative joint grid is the sampled analogue.
5. **Indirect random utility & EV (eqs. 20, 22, 24):** $\tilde V_i(y,f)=\max_{h,w}\big(v(f(wh,I)+y,h)\,e_i(h,w)\big)$ with $e_i(h,w)=g_0 g(h,w)\theta_i(h,w)$, $\theta_i$ unit-Fréchet (eq. 21); EV defined by $\tilde V_i(0,f_1)=\tilde V_i(\mathrm{EV},f_0)$. *Technique:* simulate $\theta_i$, maximise, invert for the money compensation. *Reusability:* **maybe** — the inversion is my one-dimensional money solve; but they simulate the shocks whereas I take the expectation analytically, so the *method* is a partial precedent only.

No numbered theorems are stated in this paper (it is an applied paper; the identification claims are asserted and referred to Dagsvik & Strøm 1995). Not-established: any formal identification theorem here.

## 11. Robustness and specification sensitivity
- **What they vary:** the **tax regime** (proportional / 1992 / lump-sum) — policy robustness, not estimation robustness. The opportunity density is held fixed across regimes (partial equilibrium), explicitly flagged as the "supply creates its own demand" assumption.
- **What breaks / is sensitive:** they note the broader literature's sensitivity to **functional form** (citing Hausman 1985, Blomquist 1983, Blundell et al. 1988) as motivation for a flexible micro-model, and contrast their high female elasticities with lower estimates elsewhere (Blomquist–Hansson-Brusewitz) and similar/higher ones (Arrufat–Zabalza, Hartog–Theeuwes). They do **not** report sensitivity to choice-set size, number of draws, number of starts, alternative opportunity-set definitions, or reference-state choice — those are **not-established** in this source.
- **For my stress-tests:** the transferable warning is (i) **functional-form sensitivity of elasticities** (motivates my robustness on the Box–Cox block) and (ii) the **fixed-opportunity-density (partial-equilibrium) assumption** under counterfactuals — directly relevant to my welfare counterfactuals, which similarly hold the estimated $g$ fixed when constructing reference packages. They give no guidance on effective-sample-size or draw-count, which my importance-sampling integrator must address independently.

## 16. TL;DR for retrieval
Aaberge–Dagsvik–Strøm (1995) is the canonical RURO/latent-jobs labour-supply paper (Norway 1979, married couples) whose $\psi(\cdot)\times g(\cdot)$ choice probability, with separable **hours/market (access)** and **lognormal Mincer wage (ability)** opportunity densities and a Box–Cox-type preference utility, is the direct structural ancestor of my estimation layer; it computes **household-level money-metric EV** from random indirect utility over the feasible set, summarised by mean and Gini, and documents that welfare incidence can be regressive even as disposable-income inequality falls. It informs my **access**, **ability**, and **preference** channels and my welfare-inversion logic, but it has **no source decomposition**, **no $W^1$–$W^6$ family**, **no occupation channel**, computes welfare by **simulating** (not analytically integrating) the shocks, and **concedes** that preference/opportunity separation requires offered-hours assumptions — so it is an estimation-and-welfare precedent and an honesty cite for identification, never a decomposition or responsibility-sensitive-welfare precedent.
