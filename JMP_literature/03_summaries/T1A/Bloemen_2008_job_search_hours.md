## 1. One-paragraph relevance to my JMP
This paper is the cleanest classical statement of the empirical claim my JMP rests on: observed working hours are the joint product of *individual preferences over hours* and a *demand-side hours-offer distribution*, and the two cannot be separated from observed hours alone. That is exactly my **access** channel (hours/market availability inside $g$) versus my **preference** block $v$. Bloemen's central methodological contribution — that an extra, independent data source (subjective *desired hours*) is needed to pin down preferences and thereby identify the offer side — is direct ammunition for my identification note and my defence against the "your access/preference split is mechanical" referee, because it shows the separation is *not* free even within a fully structural model. It speaks to **access** and **preference**; it does **not** speak to my **ability** (wage-technology) channel as a distinct object, nor to any welfare measure on my $W^1$–$W^6$ map.

## 3. Model and objects (map object-by-object to mine)
- **Choice set vs my latent-jobs set:** Partially analogous. A job is a (wage, hours) pair drawn from a **joint wage-hours offer distribution** $f(w,h)=f(w)\,p_l$, with hours discretised into 21 categories of 4 hours (1–84). This is a *sequential search* accept/reject framing, **not** a one-shot multinomial choice over a fixed alternative set: the unemployed individual draws offers over time at arrival rate $\lambda$ and accepts the first offer above a reservation utility. My RURO is a static log-sum over a sampled feasible set. So his "feasible set" is realised through a search process, not enumerated. **Derived-by-analogy:** his offer distribution $f(w,h)$ is the conceptual ancestor of my opportunity density $g$.
- **Deterministic utility vs my preference block $v$:** His utility $u(y,h;\varepsilon)$ is a Hausman (1980) functional form over **net income and hours** (Eq. 5 in source), with random preferences $\varepsilon$ and taste-shifters $X$ (family size, marital status, age dummies). It implies a labour-supply function linear in wage and nonlabor income (Eq. 6). This maps to my **preference** block $v$, but note: his utility is over **income**, not consumption — he explicitly substitutes the available budget for consumption and does **not** model savings. My $v$ is a Box–Cox over consumption and leisure (and children-time). Different functional form, same role.
- **Explicit opportunity / availability mechanism analogous to my $g$?** **Yes, and it is split** — this is the paper's value to me. The opportunity side has three separable pieces:
  - the **hours-offer distribution** $p_l = P(h=h_l)$ (discrete, **common across individuals** in the baseline empirical spec — see §5);
  - the **wage-offer distribution** $f(w)$ (lognormal, with mean shifted by education and age);
  - the **job-offer arrival rate** $\lambda(v)$ and the **layoff rate** $\delta$, both functions of observed characteristics (education, age, region, education-type), i.e. the *market/participation* channel.
- **Does it separate hours / wage(ability) / market / occupation?**
  - **hours** → the offer probabilities $p_l$: maps to my **access** (hours availability).
  - **wage** → the lognormal wage-offer distribution with returns to education and age: this is the closest thing in the paper to my **ability** channel (a wage technology with returns to characteristics) — but **derived-by-analogy**, because Bloemen does not frame it as "ability" or as a responsibility-relevant pay dimension; it is a search-theoretic offer distribution.
  - **market/participation** → arrival rate $\lambda$ + layoff rate $\delta$: maps to my **access** (market participation).
  - **occupation** → **N/A.** There is **no occupation channel.** The paper uses education *type/sector* (technical / economic-administrative / general / services) as a covariate in $\lambda$, $\delta$, and the wage offer, but this is a *covariate*, not an occupation-availability object, and it is education-sector, not job occupation. Do **not** read it as my `loc4`.
- **Budget map vs my EUROMOD disposable income:** His linear $y=wh+m$ (or $b+m$) is far simpler than my EUROMOD `ils_dispy`. Not reusable as a budget map.
- **Does any job attribute enter BOTH utility and the opportunity mechanism?** **Hours enter both** — hours are an argument of utility *and* are governed by the offer distribution $p_l$. This is the entire point of the model and is **the** identification problem the paper confronts (not a casual double-entry). The author's justification is explicitly on **identification grounds**: he resolves it not by exclusion but by adding an external data source (desired hours). Age also enters both utility and the arrival rate, and he notes that without desired-hours data the age effects are identified only "by functional form." Flag this as the structural precedent for my own worry about putting a variable on both sides.

## 4. Estimation method
- **Likelihood and estimator:** Maximum likelihood via **smooth simulated maximum likelihood (SSML)**, in the Börsch-Supan–Hajivassiliou tradition. The likelihood multiplies spell-type contributions (unemployed censored; unemployed-with-observed-job-characteristics; employed wage-hours; employed desired-hours; job-tenure for the layoff rate). Explicit-in-source (his Eqs. 10–14).
- **Choice-set construction:** **fixed discrete grid** of 21 hours classes (some merged for low cell counts), **not** sampled alternatives. The wage is continuous (lognormal) and integrated over analytically/numerically above the reservation wage.
- **Proposal / sampling density:** **N/A in the McFadden sense.** There is no sampling-of-alternatives over a large enumerated set; the offer distribution $f(w,h)$ is itself the structural object being estimated, and offers are simulated (R=30 replications of unobserved heterogeneity and wage draws) to form the likelihood. The wage draws are taken from the conditional-on-acceptance density $f(w)/\bar F(y_l(q))$.
- **Prior/proposal correction — is $\log(\text{prior})$ subtracted from a choice index?** **No — and this is the key boundary for my use of the paper.** There is no additive choice index with a $-\log\pi$ correction term. The structure is a sequential-search reservation-utility model, not a sampled-alternatives discrete-choice likelihood. **Do not cite this paper as a proposal-corrected RURO likelihood.** The simulator (his Eq. 14) is described as an *unbiased* simulator of the likelihood integral, which is a different statement from a sampling-of-alternatives bias correction.
- **Normalisation/scale:** discount rate $r$ fixed so the annual rate is 5%; utility scaled via the Hausman form; variances of random preferences ($\sigma_\varepsilon^2$), arrival-rate heterogeneity ($\sigma_\nu^2$), and their covariance estimated.
- **Numerical method / starting values / multistart:** numerical integration over 2-D unobserved heterogeneity plus, where desired hours enter, over wages; R=30 simulation replications. No multistart procedure is described [verify — not stated].
- **What pins preferences apart from the opportunity mechanism:** in the base model, *only model structure and functional form*; with desired-hours data, the desired-hours equation pins preferences and frees the offer probabilities to be identified from observed hours (see §8).
- **Verdict — reusable for my RURO/JAX pipeline?** **Partial / conceptual, not mechanical.** The *likelihood architecture* (sequential search, reservation utility, duration contributions) is **not** reusable — it is a different estimator class from my static sampled-alternatives log-sum. **Reusable as a design principle:** the discrete hours-offer distribution as a separable access object, and the demonstration that hours-preferences need an external pin. Step to reuse: the *identification logic* (§8), not the code.

## 4b. Proposal / sampling-of-alternatives correction
- **N/A as a sampling-of-alternatives correction.** The paper does not sample from a large enumerated alternative set and does not carry a per-alternative log-prior. The relevant analogue is the *offer distribution* $f(w,h)$, which is **partly individualised**: the **wage**-offer mean depends on education and age (individualised), and the **arrival rate** $\lambda$ depends on education, age, region, education-type (individualised); the **hours**-offer probabilities $p_l$ are **common across individuals** in the empirical specification (Bloemen flags this as "possibly restrictive" and notes hours offers may reflect institutional standard-hours norms). 
- **Relation to my proposal-individualisation concern:** This pattern is a useful precedent / partial mirror of mine — in my proposal, wage and occupation are individualised while hours and employment are common. Bloemen's setup is the same *shape* (wage individualised, hours common) arrived at independently. **Derived-by-analogy** — he does not frame it as importance-sampling individualisation, and the mechanism (offer density vs sampling proposal) differs. Do not present his $p_l$-common assumption as an endorsement of my common-hours proposal; cite it only as independent precedent that hours availability is plausibly common/institutional while wage offers are individual.

## 5. Opportunity mechanism  [MOST IMPORTANT — split by channel]
The paper models availability as a **search process with an offer density and arrival/separation rates**, not a static density over an enumerated set. Be exhaustive by channel:

- **access — hours.** The **hours-offer distribution** $p_l = P(h=h_l)$ over 21 four-hour categories (some merged). **Discrete**, and **common across all individuals** in the estimated specification. Empirically it shows a sharp **peak at 40 hours/week** (offer probability ≈0.55–0.57 across model variants), reflecting institutional standard hours. This is the direct analogue of my hours **access** object — and the paper's evidence that the hours-offer distribution is concentrated at institutional values, distinct from the distribution of desired hours, is exactly the "hours are constrained by available jobs" empirical case I want to cite. Functional form: a free discrete probability vector (subject to low-cell restrictions), **not** parameterised by covariates in the baseline.
- **access — market / participation.** The **job-offer arrival rate** $\lambda(v) = \exp(z_u' \kappa_u + \varepsilon_u\,\sigma_e + \nu)$ [transcription of source's parameterisation; verify exact index notation] depends on education, age, region, education-type, and unobserved heterogeneity $\nu$; the **layoff rate** $\delta = \exp(z_e'\kappa_e)$ depends on observed characteristics. These govern whether/how fast a feasible job materialises — my market/participation **access**. Identified off **unemployment duration** and **job tenure** respectively (data I lack).
- **ability — wage technology.** The **wage-offer distribution** is **lognormal** with log-mean shifted by education and a quadratic in log(age) and log-variance $\tau$. Returns to education and (age-proxied) experience live here. This is the channel that maps to my **ability** sub-block — but **derived-by-analogy only**: Bloemen treats it as a productivity/offer distribution, not as a normatively-distinct "pay" dimension, and there is no residual-productivity-dispersion object framed as in my $\sigma$. Identified off observed accepted wages (above the reservation wage).
- **occupation.** **N/A — no occupation-availability object.** Education *type/sector* is a covariate only; do not map it to `loc4`, and note there is **no industry/NACE object** either. There is no sector/industry conflation to flag because the paper simply does not have a job-occupation or job-industry availability channel.
- **Functional form summary:** offer density $f(w,h)=f(w)\cdot p_l$ with $f(w)$ lognormal and $p_l$ a free discrete vector; arrival and layoff rates exponential in covariates; bivariate-normal unobserved heterogeneity in preferences and arrival rate.
- **Is there an explicit opportunity mechanism?** **Yes**, and a rich one — this is the paper's strength for me. What it lacks relative to my design: it is **single-person (males only)**, has **no occupation channel**, has **no region×year market shifter of the kind I am considering merging**, and identifies the market channel from **duration data** I do not have.

## 6b. Inclusive value and money-metric inversion
- The paper uses a **value of search** $rV(q) = u(b+m,0;\varepsilon) + \frac{\lambda(v)}{r+\delta}\,E_{(w,h)}\max[0, u(wh+m,h;\varepsilon)-rV(q)]$ (his reservation-utility equation, Appendix A / Eq. 3). This is an **expected-maximum** object and is the structural ancestor of an inclusive value, but it is **not a log-sum** (no extreme-value shocks; the expectation is over the offer distribution and is solved numerically as a reservation-utility fixed point).
- **Money-metric inversion?** **No.** Welfare is never converted to a money figure; there is no one-dimensional own-utility-to-money solve. **Do not relate this to my analytic-in-shocks importance-sampling inversion** beyond the loose observation that both involve an expected-max over a feasible set. **Not-established** for any of my welfare machinery.

## 8. Identification and the separation of preferences from opportunities  [STRICT]
This is the paper's principal contribution for me; do not soften it.

- **The core identification problem (explicit-in-source):** with hours entering both utility (preferences for hours) and the offer side (hours-offer probabilities $p_l$), the marginal distribution of observed hours is $f(h_l)=\int \big[p_l \bar F(y_l(q)) / \sum_j p_j \bar F(y_j(q))\big]\,g(q,S)\,dq$ (his Eq. 15). Both the offer probabilities and the preference parameters can move to fit this distribution. **From observed hours alone, preferences-for-hours and hours-offer probabilities are only *parametrically* identified** — i.e. through the nonlinear structure and functional form — and "a variety of combinations" of the two give a reasonable fit. This is precisely my "the access/preference split is identified only by structure" exposure, stated explicitly by a canonical source.
- **The resolution (explicit-in-source):** adding **subjective desired-hours data** as a *fifth* data series "pins down" the preference parameters, which then lets the hours-offer probabilities be identified from observed hours conditional on the rest. The desired-hours equation links reported desired hours to model-optimal hours (two variants: an "optimal desired hours" measurement-error link, and a richer "hours satisfaction" model that uses the questionnaire's satisfied/not-satisfied routing).
- **What identifies each block:** wage-offer parameters ← observed accepted wages; arrival-rate parameters ← unemployment duration; layoff rate ← job tenure; preferences ← (base model) functional form only, (preferred models) desired-hours data; hours offers ← observed hours given the above. The exclusion restriction that **household characteristics (family size, marital status) enter utility but not the arrival rate** also does identifying work.
- **Empirical evidence the identification "bites":** without desired-hours data the estimated reservation-wage-as-a-function-of-hours is nearly flat (no pronounced minimum) and the variance of random preferences $\sigma_\varepsilon$ is largest — i.e. the base model dumps unexplained variation into "preferences." Adding desired hours produces a pronounced reservation-wage minimum and a smaller preference variance. **This is direct empirical support for my supporting sub-question 1** (that models which do not model constrained opportunities overstate preference heterogeneity by absorbing opportunity differences into tastes) — a model lacking the offer-side pin loads variation onto preferences. Cite carefully: his mechanism is the *missing desired-hours pin*, mine is the *missing opportunity density*; the analogy is strong but is **derived-by-analogy**, not the identical claim.
- **Transport to my France pooled cross-section (honest):** **Weak transport of the *solution*, strong transport of the *problem*.** The *problem* (structure-only identification of the access/preference split) is exactly mine. The *solution* (desired-hours data + duration data) I do **not** have: no subjective desired-hours item [verify in your SILC extract], no panel/duration data. My identification therefore rests on **functional form + distributional assumptions + synthetic recovery**, which is *closer to Bloemen's "base model"* than to his preferred desired-hours models — and Bloemen's own result is a warning that the base-model route is where preference variance is least credibly identified. This is a risk to state plainly in my identification note, and it strengthens the case for my region×year continuous opportunity shifters as an external source of identifying variation (the role Bloemen's desired-hours data plays).
- **Synthetic-recovery relevance:** the paper relies on **parametric/structural identification arguments**, not on synthetic-recovery certification. It does not run a recovery experiment of the kind that is my standard of evidence. **Not-established** that his preferred models would pass a synthetic-recovery test; he reports they *fail* a formal goodness-of-fit test at the individual level (see §11).

## 9. Key results and magnitudes
- **Benefit / unemployment-duration elasticity:** elasticity of expected unemployment duration w.r.t. the benefit level ≈ **0.35** in the base and optimal-desired-hours models, and ≈ **0.50** in the hours-satisfaction (preferred) model. (Units: elasticity; population: a representative unemployed male with sample-mean characteristics.)
- **Reservation-wage elasticity w.r.t. benefits:** small, roughly **0.02–0.14** depending on hours level and model variant (e.g. ≈0.08 at 40h in the base model, ≈0.06 in the optimal-desired-hours model, ≈0.14 in the hours-satisfaction model).
- **Hours-offer concentration:** offer probability of the 40-hour category ≈ **0.55–0.57**; the offer distribution is far more concentrated at 40h than the desired-hours distribution.
- **Satisfaction split:** among those reporting, ≈**68.6%** satisfied with current hours, ≈**25.3%** overemployed (want fewer), ≈**6.1%** underemployed (want more). Mean observed hours ≈40.6/week; mean desired hours ≈39.0/week.
- **Policy simulations:** a **5% benefit cut** produces only a **small** shift toward part-time acceptance (e.g. 40h frequency falls ≈1.3 pts; 20h rises ≈0.5 pts). Cutting the standard week from 40h→36h (swapping offer probabilities) raises the unemployment hazard by ≈**2.2%**; setting the offer distribution equal to the desired-hours distribution raises the hazard by ≈**1.3%**.
- **Benchmarking value for me:** these are duration/hazard and accepted-hours magnitudes, **not** welfare-spread or opportunity-share magnitudes, so they do **not** directly benchmark my opportunity share. The usable benchmark is qualitative: the **gap between the offer distribution and the desired/optimal distribution** is large and economically meaningful — i.e. hours constraints are first-order, supporting the premise that opportunity differences are non-trivial.

## 10. Estimators, theorems, or formal results
- **Reservation-utility equation (Appendix A, his Eq. 3 / A6):**
  $$
  \bar u(q) = u(b+m,0;\varepsilon) + \frac{\lambda(v)}{r+\delta}\sum_{l=1}^{L} p_l \int_{y(h_l,\bar u(q);\varepsilon)}^{\infty}\big[u(w h_l+m,h_l;\varepsilon)-\bar u(q)\big] f(w)\,dw.
  $$
  - *Assumptions:* stationary environment; Poisson offer arrivals at rate $\lambda$; exogenous layoff rate $\delta$; positive marginal utility of consumption; linear budget; offers i.i.d. from $f(w,h)$.
  - *Technique:* (i) write Bellman value of search and value of a job; (ii) take $\Delta t\to 0$ limits; (iii) eliminate the job value to get a fixed-point equation in $\bar u(q)$; (iv) invert utility to an hours-dependent reservation wage $y(h_l,\bar u(q);\varepsilon)$.
  - *Verdict for my layers:* **Not reusable** for my static log-sum estimator or welfare inversion — it is a search fixed point, not a sampled-choice index. Conceptually relevant to the idea of a feasibility-constrained value, but do not implement it.
- **Utility specification (Hausman form, his Eq. 5)** with implied **linear labour supply** $h^*(w,m)=m\beta+w\gamma+X\alpha+\varepsilon$ (his Eq. 6). *Reusability:* **No** — I use Box–Cox over consumption/leisure; the Hausman linear-supply form is incompatible with my preference block. Cite only as the preference functional form *he* used.
- **Marginal observed-hours distribution (his Eq. 15):** the identification expression in §8. *Reusability:* **Yes, as an identification-exposition device** — it is the cleanest formal statement that offer probabilities and preference parameters jointly determine observed hours, which I can adapt (with attribution) to motivate why my access/preference split needs more than observed hours.
- **Unbiased SSML simulator (his Eq. 14):** *Reusability:* **No** (different likelihood class), but methodologically reassuring that simulated integrals of this type are estimable.

## 11. Robustness and specification sensitivity
- **Three model variants compared:** "base model" (no desired-hours data), "optimal desired hours model" (desired hours = optimal hours + lognormal measurement error), and "hours satisfaction model" (uses the questionnaire's satisfied/not-satisfied routing with a tolerance band $\bar c$). The comparison *is* the paper's robustness/identification exercise.
- **What breaks:** the base model **fits observed hours best** (passes a Heckman $\chi^2$ goodness-of-fit test, statistic ≈17.9) precisely **because it is overparameterised** — it has no desired-hours discipline, so preferences and offer probabilities both flex to fit observed hours; but it then **fails to reproduce desired hours** and produces an implausible flat reservation-wage profile implying underemployment that contradicts the survey. The two desired-hours models reproduce the desired-hours location and the observed overemployment but **fail the observed-hours goodness-of-fit test at the individual level** (statistics ≈264 and ≈225, far above the 5% critical value ≈23.7). **The direct lesson for me:** *in-sample fit to the realised outcome can be maximised by exactly the specification that is least able to recover the underlying behaviour* — a clean external justification for my synthetic-recovery-over-fit standard of evidence (this echoes my project's own gender-split episode).
- **Stated sensitivities:** R=30 simulation replications (consistency needs R/N→∞); restrictions imposed on low-count hours categories; the common-across-individuals hours-offer assumption flagged as "possibly restrictive"; the model is **stationary** and the residual analysis reveals **neglected negative duration dependence** (longer spells fit poorly). 
- **For my robustness section:** stress-test choice-set/grid resolution and draw count; and treat "fits the data well" as necessary-not-sufficient — Bloemen is a citable precedent that the best-fitting variant was the least behaviourally identified.

## 15. Open questions and risks for my draft
- **The identification instrument gap.** Bloemen needs an *external* data source (desired hours) to do what my model asks functional form + synthetic recovery to do. The honest risk this surfaces: a referee who knows this literature will ask what plays the role of Bloemen's desired-hours data in my setting. Candidate answers I must commit to: (i) synthetic-recovery certification as the evidentiary standard; (ii) the proposed **region×year continuous opportunity shifters** (local unemployment / market conditions) as external identifying variation — Bloemen's paper is the strongest argument *for* pursuing that merge, since it shows structure-only identification is fragile exactly on the access/preference margin.
- **Hours-on-both-sides precedent.** Bloemen puts hours in both utility and the offer side and is explicit that this is the hard case requiring extra information. This both *licenses* my access/preference architecture (it is a recognised structure) and *warns* me (it is not free). State both.
- **Stationarity / no-panel.** His model needs spell durations and is stationary (with detected negative duration dependence). My cross-section sidesteps duration but loses his arrival/layoff identification entirely — I should be explicit that my "access" is an availability density, not an estimated arrival rate, and not claim the latter.
- **Singles-only, males-only.** His evidence is male-singles; my baseline includes single females and couples as joint deciders. Do not over-generalise his magnitudes to couples.
- **Fit-vs-recovery tension.** His goodness-of-fit result is a gift to my methodology section but also a caution: I should not lean on in-sample fit for my own baseline, consistent with my stated standard.

## 16. TL;DR for retrieval
Bloemen (2008, *JoLE*) is the canonical structural job-search-with-hours-restrictions paper: a (wage,hours) joint offer distribution plus arrival/layoff rates govern **access**, a Hausman utility governs **preference**, and the two are only parametrically identified from observed hours — so subjective **desired-hours** data are added to pin preferences and free the hours-offer probabilities. It is a prime citation for the **access** (hours-availability) channel, for the claim that opportunity-blind models inflate **preference** variance, and (by analogy) for individualised-wage / common-hours offer structure; it speaks weakly to **ability** and **not at all** to welfare or decomposition. Cite for the *problem* of separating tastes from hours-availability and for the fit-vs-identification lesson; do **not** cite as a money-metric, proposal-corrected RURO, or decomposition paper, and keep its education-*sector* covariate strictly clear of my occupation (`loc4`) / industry (`lindi`) objects.
