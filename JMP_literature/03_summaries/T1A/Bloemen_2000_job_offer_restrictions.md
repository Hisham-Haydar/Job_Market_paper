## 1. One-paragraph relevance to my JMP
This is a direct structural antecedent of my opportunity layer: a static labour-supply model in which the individual does **not** freely choose hours but instead draws a random number of **job offers**, each offer a $(w,h)$ package, and accepts the utility-maximising offer if it beats the reservation (non-work) utility (pp. 1–4). It is one of the earliest models to separate an **hours-availability channel** (a discrete hours-offer distribution $p_l$) from an **independent wage-offer channel** (a lognormal wage draw), which maps cleanly onto the **access** (hours/participation availability) and **ability** (wage technology) sub-objects of my $g$ — though Bloemen does not use that vocabulary, and the wage draw here is an *offer* distribution, not a returns-to-skill technology in my sense (§3, §5). Its single most consequential result for me is a **cautionary identification finding**: when the offer-arrival intensity $\lambda$ is made dependent on individual characteristics, the utility (preference) parameters can no longer be traced and the utility function becomes "flat" — the data cannot separately identify preferences from the offer-restriction mechanism (pp. 9, 12). It speaks to no welfare measure and to none of my $W^1$–$W^6$ objects.

## 2. Data and setting
- **Country / years:** Netherlands, 1985 (p. 6).
- **Dataset:** OSA (Organisatie voor Strategisch Arbeidsmarktonderzoek) labour-supply data (acknowledgements p. 0; p. 6).
- **Sample unit:** individuals — specifically **849 married women** (p. 6). Single-equation individual labour supply, **not** a joint couples unit.
- **Sample size:** 849 (p. 6).
- **Key variables:** weekly working hours $h$ (grouped into discrete categories; $h_l = 4\times l$, $l=1,\dots,m$, with $m=15$ so the max positive-probability hours is 60, p. 7); net/gross wage $w$; non-labour income $\mu$; utility-side characteristics $X$ = constant, log family size, dummy for children under 6 (p. 7); wage-side characteristics $x$ = log(age/17) and its square (age-earnings profile) plus four education dummies (p. 7); offer-intensity characteristics $z$ = constant, age, education-type and education-level dummies (p. 8, eq. 3.6).
- **Budget-set construction:** initially **linear** $y = wh + \mu$ (eq. 2.7, p. 3), tax ignored; later a progressive tax is approximated by entering hours (linear + quadratic) in the **wage equation** rather than via a fully structural tax schedule (eqs. 3.7–3.11, pp. 10–11), an approach attributed to Moffit (1984).
- **Transport to my France pooled 2015–2017 EUROMOD cross-section:** *partial.* Like me, it is **estimable on a single cross-section** (the model is "fully static," all offers arrive at one point in time, p. 2) — this is a genuine match and an explicit selling point of the paper. **Features Bloemen has that I do not / differ on:** he uses a **continuous** wage-offer density and a **parametric Poisson** offer-count mechanism estimated jointly with preferences, whereas my opportunity density is over a **sampled discrete alternative set** with an importance-sampling correction. **Features I have that Bloemen lacks:** a full EUROMOD tax-benefit budget (he only approximates tax through the wage equation, pp. 10–11), couples as a joint unit, occupation (`loc4`) as an access channel, region/year/urbanisation access shifters, and any welfare object. **Features neither has:** panel, administrative match, external opportunity instrument, vacancy/offer microdata (Bloemen's offers are *latent*, inferred from the accepted job, not observed — p. 1). He explicitly notes that observing offer **duration** would require a panel and is pursued in a companion sequential-search paper, Bloemen (1992) [the working paper in his own references] (p. 2, p. 9).

## 3. Model and objects (map object-by-object to mine)
- **Choice set = my latent-jobs set?** *Derived-by-analogy: yes, antecedent.* The individual receives $n$ job offers, each a $(w,h)$ pair, and chooses the highest-utility one or non-work (pp. 3–4). This is the conceptual ancestor of a latent feasible-job set, but the *mechanism* differs: a **Poisson-distributed count** $n$ of continuous-wage / discrete-hours draws, not a fixed sampled grid.
- **Deterministic utility = my preference utility $v$?** *Partial.* Utility $u(h,y)$ is the **Hausman (1980)** specification (eq. 2.8, p. 3), which yields a labour-supply function linear in wage and non-labour income, with an additive normal taste shock $e\sim N(0,\sigma_e^2)$. This is a preference block analogous to my $v$, but it is **Hausman-linear, not Box–Cox**, and it is defined over $(h,y)$ rather than my (consumption, leisure, children-time). It carries a single scalar unobserved taste term $e$, not my demographic-shifted Box–Cox structure.
- **Explicit opportunity / availability mechanism analogous to my $g$?** **Yes — this is the paper's core and its relevance to me.** Bloemen has an explicit availability mechanism with the following separable pieces:
  - **hours channel:** a discrete hours-offer distribution $P(h=h_l)=p_l$ (eqs. 2.1–2.2, p. 3), parametrised by nonnegative weights $\mu_l$ with $\mu_1$ normalised to 1 — the antecedent of my **access/hours-availability** channel;
  - **wage channel:** a **lognormal** wage-offer distribution $\ln w = x'\eta + v$, $v\sim N(0,\sigma_v^2)$ (eqs. 2.3–2.5, p. 3), the antecedent of my **ability/wage** sub-block (though as an *offer* draw, see flag below);
  - **market / participation channel:** the **Poisson offer count** $n$ with intensity $\lambda$ (eq. 2.9, p. 4), where $P(n=0)>0$ generates involuntary unemployment — the antecedent of my **market/participation-availability** channel. Later $\lambda_i=\exp(\theta'z_i)$ is made individual-specific (eq. 3.6, p. 8).
  The joint offer density is $f(w,h_l)$ (eq. 2.6, p. 3), the product of the lognormal wage density and the discrete hours probability $p_l$.
- **Does it separate hours / wage(ability) / market / occupation?** **Hours, wage, and market: yes, explicitly** (the three pieces above). **Occupation: NO** — there is no occupation channel; education enters both the wage equation (as human-capital proxy) and the offer-intensity equation, but there is no ISCO/`loc4`-type occupation-availability object. So Bloemen anticipates three of my four opportunity channels and is silent on occupation.
- **Budget map = my EUROMOD disposable income?** **No.** Linear $y=wh+\mu$ at baseline (eq. 2.7); progressive tax only *approximated* via hours-in-wage-equation (eqs. 3.7–3.11). No tax-benefit microsimulation.
- **FLAG — any attribute in BOTH utility and opportunity?** **Hours $h$ enters both the utility function and the opportunity (hours-offer) mechanism**, which is structurally unavoidable in a job-offer model: $h$ is simultaneously an argument of $u(h,y)$ and a drawn offer characteristic. This is *not* the pathological double-entry my contract warns against (an attribute appearing as both a taste and an availability shifter); it is the standard feature that the *same* hours value is both chosen-over and offer-restricted. **Wage $w$** similarly enters utility (through $y=wh+\mu$) and is an offer draw. Bloemen does not frame this as an identification concern per se; identification of preferences-vs-offers rests instead on functional form and the joint likelihood (see §8). **Education enters both the wage-offer equation and the offer-intensity ($\lambda$) equation** (pp. 7–8) — this *is* a place where one covariate drives two distinct opportunity channels, and the paper's central negative finding (§9) is precisely that loading characteristics onto $\lambda$ collapses identification of preferences.

## 4. Estimation method
- **Likelihood and estimator:** full-information **maximum likelihood**, all parameters (preferences, wage-offer, hours-offer, offer count) estimated **simultaneously** (p. 1, p. 12). A two-step procedure is explicitly **ruled out** because the observed-wage distribution depends on the whole acceptance structure (p. 1).
- **Choice-set construction:** *not* a sampled-alternatives design. The likelihood integrates analytically over the offer process: for a working individual the contribution is $n$ times the probability that the other $n-1$ offers are dominated, times the wage-offer density at the observed wage, times $p_{l_*}$; this is summed over the Poisson count $n$ and integrated over the taste shock $e$ (eqs. 2.16–2.22, pp. 5–6). For a non-worker the contribution is $\exp\{-\lambda[1-P(0\mid e)]\}$ integrated over $e$ (eqs. 2.20, 2.23, p. 6). Hours are a **fixed discrete grid** ($m=15$ categories at 4-hour spacing), but **wages are continuous** (lognormal), so there is no finite alternative grid in my sense.
- **Proposal / sampling density; prior/proposal correction:** **N/A — there is no sampling-of-alternatives correction.** Bloemen does not sample alternatives from a proposal and does not subtract a $\log\pi$ term; the offer distribution *is* the data-generating availability density, integrated in closed/analytic form. This is a fundamental architectural difference from my importance-sampling RURO engine (see §4b and §6b).
- **Normalisation / scale:** hours-offer weight $\mu_1\equiv 1$ (eq. 2.2); low-frequency hours categories are pooled by equality restrictions $p_1=p_2=p_3=p_4$, $p_6=p_7$, $p_{12}=p_{13}=p_{14}=p_{15}$ (eq. 3.2, p. 6). The Hausman-utility shock and the wage shock are separately scaled ($\sigma_e$, $\sigma_v$).
- **Numerical method / starting values / multistart:** **not described in detail** [verify]; standard ML, no multistart or starting-value protocol reported.
- **What pins preferences separately from the opportunity mechanism:** functional form and the joint likelihood, *as long as* $\lambda$ is constant. Once $\lambda$ is made characteristic-dependent, the separation **fails** (the central finding, §8–§9).
- **Verdict — reusable for my RURO/JAX pipeline?** **No, not as an estimation template — but yes as a structural blueprint and as a cautionary identification benchmark.** The *likelihood architecture* (Poisson count + continuous wage offer + analytic integration over $e$) is not reusable because my engine uses discrete sampled alternatives with an explicit $-\log\pi$ correction and analytic-in-shocks log-sum aggregation. The *channel decomposition* of opportunity (hours / wage / market) is directly reusable as conceptual scaffolding. The *finding* that $\lambda$-individualisation collapses preference identification is a benchmark I should cite when defending my own preference/opportunity separation.

## 4b. Proposal / sampling-of-alternatives correction  [extract even if brief]
**Not applicable in Bloemen's design.** There is no Monte-Carlo sampling of alternatives and therefore no McFadden-style correction and no per-alternative log-prior. The offer densities (lognormal wage $\times$ discrete hours $\times$ Poisson count) are the actual availability primitives, entered directly into the likelihood and integrated analytically over the taste shock and summed over the offer count (eqs. 2.16–2.23, pp. 5–6). **Relation to my concerns:** the closest analogue to my "partly individualised proposal" is Bloemen's choice to make the **wage-offer mean covariate-dependent** ($\ln w = x'\eta + v$, individualised through age and education) and to make the **offer-intensity $\lambda$ covariate-dependent** ($\lambda_i=\exp(\theta'z_i)$), while the **hours-offer probabilities $p_l$ are common across individuals**. That individualisation pattern — wage individualised, hours common — is *structurally the same split* as my proposal (wage/occupation individualised; hours/employment common), but in Bloemen it is a property of the **structural availability density itself**, not of an importance-sampling instrument. This is *derived-by-analogy*, not explicit-in-source: Bloemen never frames it as a proposal/importance-sampling distinction.

## 5. Opportunity mechanism  [MOST IMPORTANT — exhaustive; split by channel]
Bloemen's availability mechanism is an explicit density over $(w,h)$ offers plus a Poisson arrival count. Functional forms:

- **access — hours availability:** discrete hours-offer distribution, $P(h=h_l)=p_l$, $l=1,\dots,m$ (eq. 2.1), parametrised $p_l = \mu_l/\sum_k \mu_k$ with $\mu_1=1$ (eq. 2.2), $m=15$, grid $h_l=4l$ (eqs. 3.1, p. 6–7). **Does NOT vary with circumstances** at baseline — the $p_l$ are common across individuals. This is a *pure availability* object: peaks in $p_l$ at 20, 32, 40 hours are read as institutional/offer features, not tastes (p. 7).
- **access/market — participation availability:** Poisson offer count, $p(n)=\exp(-\lambda)\lambda^n/n!$ (eq. 2.9, p. 4); $P(n=0)=e^{-\lambda}>0$ generates **involuntary unemployment**. At baseline $\lambda$ is constant; in the extension $\lambda_i=\exp(\theta'z_i)$ (eq. 3.6, p. 8) with $z$ = age, education type, education level. A likelihood-ratio test **rejects** $\lambda$-homogeneity (LR $=83.58$ vs $\chi^2$ 5% critical $14.07$, p. 8). So circumstance-dependence of arrival intensity is statistically supported.
- **ability — wage technology:** lognormal wage-**offer** distribution $\ln w = x'\eta + v$, $v\sim N(0,\sigma_v^2)$ (eqs. 2.3–2.5), with $x$ = age-earnings profile (log(age/17) and square) and four education dummies. **Important caveat for my mapping:** this is a wage **offer** distribution (the dispersion $\sigma_v$ is offer-side dispersion, partly reflecting search/offer heterogeneity), **not** a pure returns-to-skill productivity technology. My **ability** sub-block is the structural returns to own education and experience plus residual productivity dispersion $\sigma$; Bloemen's wage draw is the closest classical antecedent but conflates returns-to-characteristics with offer dispersion. Map as *antecedent-with-caveat*, not as an identical object.
- **occupation:** **absent.** No occupation/ISCO/`loc4` availability object; no sector/industry object. There is therefore **no conflation of occupation with industry** in the source (it simply has neither). I should not read any occupation-access content into this paper.

**Cost of omissions for my access/ability/preference decomposition:** Bloemen has hours-access, market-access, and a wage-offer (proto-ability) channel but **no occupation-access channel and no region/year access shifters**, and his wage channel is an offer draw rather than a productivity technology. If I cite him as the antecedent of the opportunity layer, I must be explicit that my access sub-block is richer (occupation, region, urbanisation, year) and that my ability sub-block is a structural wage return, not an offer distribution.

This is a behavioural diagnostic, **not** a welfare metric and **not** a reference-set construction in my $W^k$ sense.

**Placement on my $W^1$–$W^6$ map: NONE.** The paper contains no measure that corresponds to any $W^k$, and the Ind-$y$ / Ind-$A$ classification does not apply to it. **Do not cite Bloemen as a welfare, equivalent-income, or $W^1$–$W^6$ source.** *Derived-by-analogy only:* his "desired hours" exercise is loosely thematically adjacent to the *idea* of evaluating a household against an unconstrained reference, but it is a positive simulation of behaviour, not a normatively classified money metric, and the analogy should not be pushed.

**Verdict:** incompatible as a welfare source; usable only as the opportunity-mechanism antecedent.

## 6b. Inclusive value and money-metric inversion

Relation to my analytic-in-shocks importance-sampling inversion: only the general lesson that the latent process can be integrated without simulating an argmax transfers; the specific construction does not.

## 8. Identification and the separation of preferences from opportunities  [STRICT]
This is the paper's most important content for my identification note, and it is a **negative** result I must not soften.

- **What identifies tastes vs constraints:** at baseline (constant $\lambda$), preferences (the Hausman-utility parameters and $\sigma_e$) are separated from the offer mechanism by **functional form** plus the **joint likelihood** of accepted $(w,h)$ pairs and non-participation. There is **no external exclusion restriction, no panel, no instrument** — identification is purely parametric/distributional (the Gaussian taste shock, the lognormal wage offer, the Poisson count, and the Hausman utility form together pin the parameters). This is the same identification basis I rely on (parametric/synthetic-recovery, no panel, no instrument), so the transport to my France pooled cross-section is **close** — and that is exactly why the failure mode below is a live risk for me.
- **The failure mode (explicit-in-source, central):** when $\lambda$ is made dependent on individual characteristics ($\lambda_i=\exp(\theta'z_i)$), the **utility parameters can no longer be traced**: the estimated utility function becomes "flat and approximately uniform," the random-taste standard deviation explodes, and the desired-hours distribution becomes nearly uniform (p. 9; conclusions p. 12, Tables 3.1 vs 3.3). Bloemen offers two readings: either (a) demand-side restrictions genuinely dominate behaviour, or (b) **"there is simply not enough information in the data set to trace down the underlying preference structure"** once the offer-intensity is individualised (p. 9). He leans toward (b)/under-identification. The static model also cannot interpret the *level* of $\lambda$ (estimated $\approx 37$ at baseline, Table 3.1) because it has no time dimension (p. 9).
- **Ability-vs-access within the opportunity side:** Bloemen does **not** attempt this finer cut; he separates hours-offer from wage-offer from arrival-count, but does not interpret the wage channel as "ability" vs the hours/arrival channels as "access," and does not test that boundary. So he gives no direct evidence on my ability/access separation — only on the coarser preference/offer separation.
- **Transport verdict:** the **lesson transports strongly and adversely**: making the opportunity mechanism richly covariate-dependent in a single cross-section can starve the preference block of identifying information. My defence — that the 47-param shared baseline is **certified by synthetic recovery**, that the gender-split was *rejected precisely because it failed synthetic recovery despite better in-sample fit*, and that the preference block is reported as "identified with imprecision, not non-identified" — is exactly the kind of discipline Bloemen's 1992 result shows is necessary. **Cite Bloemen as the historical warning; cite my synthetic-recovery gate as the answer to it.**

## 9. Key results and magnitudes
- **Baseline (constant $\lambda$), Table 3.1 (p. 13):** $\beta=-0.0287$ (s.e. 0.00688); $\gamma=6.473$ (1.605); offer intensity $\lambda=36.7$ (s.e. 32.26, i.e. very imprecise — "sizeable standard error," p. 7); wage-offer dispersion $\sigma_v=0.460$; taste-shock s.d. $\sigma_e=32.6$; log-family-size and young-children coefficients strongly positive on reservation utility ($\delta_2=-56.29$, $\delta_3=-27.23$ enter the reservation/utility block, pp. 7, 13). Hours-offer peaks at 20, 32, 40 hours (p. 7). Age-earnings profile peaks at age 33 (p. 7). Log-likelihood $-2002.47$.
- **Variable-$\lambda$, Table 3.3 (p. 15):** LR test of $\lambda$-homogeneity $=83.58 \gg 14.07$ (p. 8) — offer intensity is significantly characteristic-dependent. **But** the utility parameters blow up (e.g. $\delta_1=409.5$, $\delta_2=-421.3$, $\sigma_e=248.7$, all with enormous standard errors), the utility function goes flat, and preferences cease to be identified (pp. 8–9). Log-likelihood $-1960.68$.
- **Hours-dependent wages (tax-proxy), Table 3.5 (p. 17):** preferences play a "more pronounced" role, $\sigma_e$ falls substantially, $\lambda$ falls; the quadratic hours-in-wage term $\tau_2<0$ significant, $\tau_1$ insignificant; LR test of $\tau_1=\tau_2=0$ is $16.596 \gg 5.991$ (p. 11). Log-likelihood $-1985.87$. Interpretation: low frequency of >40h is driven by **low marginal income vs marginal disutility of leisure**, not (only) by low offer probabilities (pp. 10–12).
- **Model testing:** participation and the 20/32/40 peaks are predicted well in simulation (Tables 3.2, 3.4, 3.6), but **Andrews' (1988) chi-square test rejects all three specifications** on all three partitions (Table 3.7, p. 19) — the simulated and empirical hours distributions are formally not equal.
- **Magnitudes useful as plausibility benchmarks for me:** the *direction* of the desired-vs-observed participation gap (desired participation 0.473 vs observed 0.390 at baseline, p. 8) quantifies involuntary non-employment as ~8 percentage points in this 1985 Dutch sample — a rough order-of-magnitude for "access-driven" non-participation, **not** transportable as a number to France 2015–2017. No welfare, opportunity-share, or decomposition magnitudes exist to benchmark mine.

## 10. Estimators, theorems, or formal results
No theorems. The formal content is a **likelihood construction**, not an estimator with stated assumptions/asymptotics:
- **Working-individual likelihood contribution** (eq. 2.17, p. 5): conditional on the taste shock $e$,
  $$ l(w_*,h_{l_*}\mid e) = \lambda \exp\{-\lambda[1-P(w_*,h_{l_*}\mid e)]\}\, k(w_*,\eta'x,\sigma_v)\, p_{l_*}, $$
  for $u(h_{l_*},w_*h_{l_*}+\mu)>u_0$, where $P(\cdot\mid e)$ is the probability a drawn offer is dominated (eq. 2.11) and $k(\cdot)$ is the lognormal wage density. Integrated over $e$ on the acceptance region $B$ (eqs. 2.21–2.22).
- **Non-working contribution** (eqs. 2.20, 2.23): $l(h=0)=\int \exp\{-\lambda[1-P(0\mid e)]\}\,\tfrac{1}{\sigma_e}\phi(e/\sigma_e)\,de$.
- **Technique (3–5 bullets):** (i) condition on the unobserved Gaussian taste shock $e$; (ii) for each hours category compute the wage region $A_l(e)$ in which a draw is dominated by the observed offer (eqs. 2.10–2.14), using the lognormal CDF $\Phi$; (iii) aggregate over the Poisson offer count analytically (the $n[P]^{n-1}$ structure summed against the Poisson pmf gives the $\lambda\exp\{-\lambda[1-P]\}$ form); (iv) integrate over $e$ by one-dimensional quadrature; (v) introduce tax by splitting $P(w\in A_l)$ at bracket boundaries (p. 6) or by the hours-in-wage approximation.
- **Reusability verdict:** **maybe, as a derivation template only.** The analytic Poisson-sum and the "dominated-offer region" construction are elegant and could inform how I think about marginalising a latent count, but my engine does not have a Poisson offer count and does not integrate a continuous wage offer analytically — it samples discrete alternatives and corrects with $-\log\pi$. So: reusable for *intuition about integrating out a latent availability process*, not reusable as code or as a likelihood for my RURO specification.

## 11. Robustness and specification sensitivity
What Bloemen varies and what breaks:
- **Offer-intensity homogeneity vs heterogeneity:** the single most informative sensitivity. Individualising $\lambda$ **breaks preference identification** (utility flattens, $\sigma_e$ explodes) — Tables 3.1 vs 3.3. **Direct lesson for me:** richly covariate-dependent opportunity intensity in one cross-section can be over-flexible; this argues for my synthetic-recovery gate before adopting any richer opportunity specification, and is a precise analogue of my **rejected gender-split** (LR-favoured, better-fitting, but failed synthetic recovery).
- **Hours in the wage equation (tax proxy):** including linear+quadratic hours sharpens preferences and shrinks $\sigma_e$ (Table 3.5); the >40h low-frequency story flips from "not offered" to "not worth it at the margin" (pp. 10–12). **Lesson:** the budget/tax representation materially changes how much behaviour is attributed to offers vs preferences — reinforcing that my full EUROMOD tax-benefit budget (rather than a wage-equation proxy) is a strength, and that budget mis-specification can leak into the opportunity/preference split.
- **Choice-set / grid:** hours grid fixed at $m=15$ (4-hour spacing) chosen to match the max observed hours (60); low-frequency categories pooled by equality restrictions (eq. 3.2). **Lesson for my effective-sample-size concern:** Bloemen pools sparse hours cells to identify the $p_l$; this is the classical analogue of my worry that thin coverage (my low effective sample size for singles, median ~20/101) starves parts of the opportunity density — sparse regions of the offer/alternative space must be handled deliberately, not left to noise.
- **Combined hours-dependent-wage + variable-$\lambda$:** gives "similar results … a flat utility function" (p. 11) — i.e. the identification collapse from variable $\lambda$ dominates.
- **Formal predictive testing:** Andrews chi-square rejects every specification (Table 3.7) — a candid reminder that good participation/peak prediction does not imply distributional fit, relevant to how I should phrase certification claims (I certify by synthetic recovery, not by in-sample distributional fit, which Bloemen's rejections show is the right emphasis).

## 12. What I can cite this paper for
- That **static, cross-sectional** labour-supply models with **job-offer restrictions** — separating an **hours-offer (availability) channel** from an **independent wage-offer channel** and a **random arrival-count** generating involuntary non-employment — are a recognised structural antecedent of opportunity-layer modelling (pp. 1–4). *(Explicit-in-source.)*
- That such a model is **estimable on a single cross-section** because all offers are taken to arrive at one instant and the acceptance decision is immediate (p. 2). *(Explicit-in-source.)*
- That an **independent wage-offer effect** (wages varying offer-to-offer for reasons beyond an hours–wage link) is a distinct channel from hours restrictions, and motivates a joint wage–hours offer density (pp. 1, 3). *(Explicit-in-source.)*
- The **identification warning**: making the opportunity/offer mechanism richly dependent on individual characteristics in a single cross-section can leave **insufficient information to identify preferences**, flattening the utility function (pp. 8–9, 12). *(Explicit-in-source — cite as the historical precedent for my synthetic-recovery discipline.)*
- That **parameters of the wage and hours offer distributions must be estimated jointly** with the labour-supply (preference) parameters, because the observed-wage distribution is endogenous to acceptance — a two-step approach is invalid (p. 1). *(Explicit-in-source.)*
- That offer-restriction models can fit **participation and institutional hours peaks** while still being **formally rejected** as distributional descriptions (Table 3.7) *(explicit-in-source; supports my "certify by recovery, not in-sample fit" stance — but the recovery framing is mine, derived-by-analogy).*

## 16. TL;DR for retrieval
Bloemen (1992, CentER DP 9239; "2000" attribution [verify]) is a **static, cross-sectional ML labour-supply model with job-offer restrictions** in which individuals draw a Poisson number of $(w,h)$ offers — a discrete **hours-offer** availability distribution and an independent lognormal **wage-offer** distribution — and accept the utility-maximising offer or remain involuntarily non-employed; it is the classical **antecedent of my opportunity layer's access (hours/participation) and proto-ability (wage-offer) channels**, but has **no occupation channel, no tax-benefit budget, no welfare object, and no decomposition**. Its load-bearing result for me is a **negative identification finding** — individualising the offer-arrival intensity collapses identification of preferences ("flat" utility) — which is the historical precedent legitimising my synthetic-recovery certification discipline and exactly mirrors my rejected gender-split, and it must **not** be cited as a welfare, Shapley, $W^1$–$W^6$, or occupation/sector source.
