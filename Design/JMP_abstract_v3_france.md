1. Main abstract

This paper asks how much observed inequality in money-metric well-being reflects unequal job opportunities rather than heterogeneous preferences. The empirical design is a France-based structural labor-supply model estimated on SRCV / EUROMOD-input microdata, with labor supply modeled as joint choice among latent job packages in a RURO framework rather than as choice from a common unconstrained hours set. The paper’s contribution is to treat opportunities consistently across three stages. First, it estimates preferences and an opportunity mechanism within the same France implementation, using sampled job alternatives and tax-benefit microsimulation to map each job package into disposable income. Second, it constructs a money-metric welfare measure at the household level, conditional on constrained feasible sets. Third, it uses counterfactual equalizations and an order-independent decomposition rule to attribute welfare inequality to opportunity heterogeneity and preference heterogeneity. The central empirical question is whether standard structural labor-supply models overstate preference heterogeneity by absorbing unequal access to jobs into tastes. The normative interpretation is responsibility-sensitive but limited: unequal opportunities are treated as compensation-relevant circumstances, while the paper remains an empirical structural contribution rather than a separate axiomatic theory of well-being. Its core objective is decomposition, not welfare ranking.   

2. Slightly more empirical version

This paper develops a France 2016 prototype for estimating structural labor supply with constrained opportunities and using it to decompose welfare inequality. The empirical application is built on a household/couples RURO pipeline using SRCV / EUROMOD-input data, where each spouse chooses among sampled latent job packages defined by discrete wage-hours opportunities and the household budget is computed through tax-benefit microsimulation. Microsimulation is therefore a supporting input to the budget mapping, not the paper’s core contribution. The contribution instead lies in a single empirical pipeline that links three objects: estimation of preferences and opportunity distributions, construction of household money-metric welfare, and decomposition of welfare inequality. The first decomposition uses counterfactual equalization of opportunity distributions across circumstance groups such as region and education, alongside a preference-neutralization counterfactual, combined through a Shapley-Shorrocks rule. The paper does not claim that the France estimation problem is already fully resolved. Rather, it uses the existing France implementation to study whether unequal job opportunities account for a meaningful share of welfare inequality and whether standard models without explicit opportunity constraints misattribute that heterogeneity to preferences.   

3. Slightly more welfare-oriented version

This paper brings a responsibility-sensitive welfare perspective to a France-based structural labor-supply model with constrained opportunities. In the empirical prototype, households choose among latent job packages in a RURO framework, so welfare is evaluated relative to the feasible opportunities they face rather than to a universal labor-supply menu. The contribution is to connect estimation, welfare evaluation, and decomposition within one empirical design. Preferences and the opportunity mechanism are estimated jointly; tax-benefit microsimulation is used only to translate sampled job packages into disposable income; and household money-metric welfare is then computed conditional on constrained feasible sets. The paper’s central output is not a welfare ranking, but a decomposition of welfare inequality into parts associated with opportunity heterogeneity and preference heterogeneity under an order-independent rule. The normative interpretation is explicit but disciplined: unequal opportunities are treated as compensation-relevant circumstances, while the project remains distinct from the separate theory paper on axiomatic well-being measurement in a jobs world. Because the France implementation still faces unresolved normalization and convergence issues, the paper does not claim settled quantitative results yet. Its claim is narrower and stronger: the JMP builds a concrete France RURO platform for measuring whether unequal opportunities are an empirically important driver of welfare inequality.
