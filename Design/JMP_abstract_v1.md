1. Main abstract (188 words)

This paper studies how much observed inequality in money-metric well-being reflects unequal job opportunities rather than heterogeneous preferences. Labor supply is modeled as discrete choice among latent job packages, so individuals face heterogeneous feasible sets rather than a common unconstrained hours menu. The paper’s contribution is a structural framework that treats opportunities consistently across three stages: estimation of preferences and the opportunity mechanism, construction of money-metric welfare conditional on constrained feasible sets, and decomposition of welfare inequality. Disposable income for each job package is computed with tax-benefit microsimulation, but microsimulation serves only to map alternatives into budget constraints. The analysis then uses counterfactual equalizations of the opportunity mechanism and of preference heterogeneity, combined with an order-independent decomposition, to separate the shares of welfare inequality attributable to opportunities, preferences, and their interaction. The normative interpretation is responsibility-sensitive: unequal job opportunities are treated as compensation-relevant circumstances, while preference heterogeneity is interpreted separately rather than being conflated with constraints. By shifting the focus from reform incidence or welfare ranking to the structural decomposition of welfare inequality, the paper contributes to constrained discrete-choice labor supply, empirical welfare analysis, and inequality-of-opportunity measurement.   

2. Slightly more empirical version (185 words)

This paper develops a single-country structural labor-supply application to quantify the contribution of unequal job opportunities to welfare inequality. In the empirical design, individuals choose among latent job packages defined by discrete hours and wage categories rather than along a common continuous hours schedule. For each alternative, disposable income is computed under the tax-benefit system using microsimulation, which enters as a supporting input to the budget mapping rather than as the core contribution. The model separately estimates preferences over consumption and leisure and an opportunity mechanism that shifts the distribution of feasible job packages across observable circumstances such as region and education. On that basis, the paper constructs an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare measure and decomposes the welfare distribution using counterfactual equalizations of opportunities and of preference heterogeneity together with an order-independent decomposition rule. The empirical objective is to determine whether standard structural models over-attribute observed heterogeneity to tastes when unequal access to jobs is left implicit. The contribution is therefore not another reform simulation, but a concrete structural decomposition of welfare inequality into opportunity-driven and preference-driven components with a clear interpretation in observed labor-market environments.   

3. Slightly more welfare-oriented version (191 words)

This paper brings a responsibility-sensitive welfare perspective to structural labor-supply estimation with constrained opportunities. Labor supply is modeled as choice among latent job packages, so welfare is evaluated relative to the feasible set individuals actually face rather than to a universal opportunity set. Within a unified structural framework, the paper estimates preferences and an explicit opportunity mechanism, computes money-metric welfare levels conditional on constrained opportunities, and decomposes welfare inequality into components associated with opportunity heterogeneity, preference heterogeneity, and their interaction. The normative interpretation is central but empirically grounded: unequal job opportunities are treated as circumstances relevant for compensation, while heterogeneous preferences are not allowed to absorb constraints by construction. This matters because the same observed hours and income bundle may represent an unconstrained optimum for one individual and a constrained second-best for another. Microsimulation enters only as a supporting tool for translating job packages into disposable income under the tax-benefit system. The paper thus links constrained discrete-choice labor supply, empirical welfare analysis, and responsibility-sensitive inequality decomposition, and provides an empirical framework for assessing how much observed welfare inequality should be interpreted as arising from unequal opportunities rather than from heterogeneous tastes.
