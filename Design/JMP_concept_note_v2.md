**1. Provisional title**

**Opportunity Constraints as a Driver of Welfare Inequality: A Latent-Jobs Structural Decomposition Approach**

This title reflects the intended contribution more accurately than a generic well-being or cross-country framing. The paper is not primarily about ranking countries or evaluating a single reform. Its central object is the decomposition of welfare inequality in a structural labor-supply environment where feasible job opportunities are heterogeneous and partly latent.  

**2. Core question**

How much of observed inequality in money-metric well-being is attributable to unequal job opportunities rather than heterogeneous preferences, once labor supply is modeled as discrete choice over latent job packages and welfare is evaluated consistently with constrained feasible sets?

A sharper way to state the empirical issue is that standard structural labor-supply models may over-attribute observed heterogeneity to preferences because they do not model opportunity constraints sufficiently explicitly. The paper asks whether this matters quantitatively for welfare measurement and, in turn, for inequality decomposition.  

**3. Why this matters**

In the relevant structural labor-supply literature, jobs are often best understood as constrained opportunities rather than as freely chosen hours points on a common budget set. Once this is recognized, welfare comparisons become more delicate: the same observed bundle may reflect unconstrained choice for one individual and a constrained second-best for another. If opportunity heterogeneity is absorbed into preference heterogeneity, both the behavioral interpretation and the welfare analysis become distorted. 

This is precisely why the project should be organized around decomposition rather than ranking. A country-ranking or beyond-GDP exercise would treat welfare levels as the endpoint. Here, the scientific object is the mechanism generating welfare inequality. The paper therefore focuses on whether structurally estimated opportunity heterogeneity accounts for a quantitatively meaningful share of welfare inequality, and how that share should be interpreted under a responsibility-sensitive view in which opportunities are compensation-relevant.  

**4. Main contribution**

The paper’s contribution is to build a single empirical pipeline that treats opportunities consistently across estimation, welfare evaluation, and inequality decomposition.

First, it estimates a structural labor-supply model with constrained opportunities / latent jobs rather than a model that treats all heterogeneity as preference heterogeneity. Second, it constructs a money-metric welfare object that is defined relative to the constrained feasible set, not relative to a universal choice set. Third, it decomposes welfare inequality into an opportunity-driven component and a preference-driven component using explicit counterfactual equalizations and an order-independent decomposition logic.  

The novelty is therefore not “one more structural model” and not “one more welfare ranking.” It is the integration of a latent-opportunity labor-supply model with a welfare decomposition that gives opportunities an explicit, measurable role in explaining inequality. This also keeps the project distinct from the separate pure theory paper: the theory paper studies axiomatic well-being measurement in a jobs world, whereas the JMP is an empirical structural paper that estimates opportunities, computes welfare, and quantifies their contribution to inequality.  

**5. Empirical strategy**

The baseline design is a single-country structural application on working-age individuals or households, excluding the self-employed in the first pass. Labor supply is modeled as choice among discrete job packages defined minimally by hours categories and wage or earnings categories, with a limited extension to additional job attributes only if the data support it. Disposable income for each package is computed under the relevant tax-benefit rules, but microsimulation enters only as an input into the budget mapping; it is not the core contribution of the paper. 

The key estimated objects are: preferences over consumption and leisure; an opportunity mechanism governing the distribution of feasible job packages as a function of observable circumstances such as region and education; and, if needed, a parsimonious wage or earnings offer structure. The main empirical output is not a long list of simulations, but a small set of objects that discipline the paper’s contribution: baseline welfare inequality, welfare inequality under opportunity equalization, welfare inequality under preference neutralization, and the corresponding decomposition shares. 

**6. Relation to literature**

The paper sits at the intersection of four strands. The first is the constrained structural labor-supply literature that models work as choice over job packages or latent jobs rather than as unconstrained hours choice. This literature provides the positive foundation for treating opportunities as a first-class object. The second is the identification literature on restricted choices, latent jobs, and offer constraints, which shows that observed labor-supply outcomes confound tastes and opportunities unless the opportunity structure is modeled explicitly. The third is the welfare-analysis literature for discrete choice, which makes it possible to compute money-metric welfare objects rather than stopping at hours responses or elasticities. The fourth is the inequality-of-opportunity and fairness literature, which provides the normative language for interpreting unequal opportunities as compensation-relevant rather than merely descriptive heterogeneity.  

Relative to the existing literature, the paper is closest to structural studies that estimate constrained labor supply and compute welfare effects of reforms. The difference is that those papers typically treat welfare evaluation as the endpoint and opportunity structure as background modeling architecture. Here, the central object is the decomposition of welfare inequality itself. Relative to empirical welfare-comparison papers with heterogeneous preferences, the difference is that this project introduces an explicit opportunity mechanism and asks whether what is often labeled preference heterogeneity is partly an artifact of unmodeled opportunity inequality.  

**7. Main risk**

The main risk is identification. In this class of models, preferences and opportunities are difficult to separate unless the opportunity mechanism is disciplined by sufficient structure, credible exclusions, or robust empirical restrictions. If this separation is weak, the decomposition may appear mechanical rather than informative. A second risk is normative vulnerability: once welfare inequality is decomposed, the interpretation depends on a clearly stated responsibility stance. If that stance is implicit, the paper will look ad hoc.  

The appropriate response is not to broaden the project, but to keep the first version narrow: a parsimonious opportunity mechanism, a transparent welfare object, and a small number of decomposition exercises with explicit sensitivity checks. That is more defensible than a broader but weaker paper. 

**8. Immediate next step**

The immediate next step is to implement a prototype with the smallest defensible job-package definition: hours categories by wage or earnings categories, optionally augmented by one additional job attribute such as sector. Using that structure, I would estimate a simple opportunity mechanism varying by a limited set of circumstances, compute baseline welfare inequality, and produce one decomposition figure plus one robustness exercise under an alternative circumstance partition or opportunity parameterization. If the opportunity share of welfare inequality is quantitatively nontrivial and reasonably stable, the paper’s direction is locked.
