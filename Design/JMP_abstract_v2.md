1. Final working abstract (198 words)

This paper asks how much observed inequality in money-metric well-being is generated by unequal job opportunities rather than by heterogeneous preferences. In a single-country structural setting, labor supply is modeled as choice among latent job packages, so feasible options differ across individuals rather than being represented by a common unconstrained hours set. The contribution is empirical rather than axiomatic: it builds a unified framework that treats opportunities consistently at three stages. First, it separately estimates preferences and an opportunity mechanism governing the distribution of feasible job packages across observable circumstances. Second, it computes money-metric welfare conditional on those constrained feasible sets, with tax-benefit microsimulation used only to map each job package into disposable income. Third, it decomposes welfare inequality, using counterfactual equalizations and an order-independent decomposition rule, into components attributable to opportunity heterogeneity, preference heterogeneity, and their interaction. The central empirical question is whether standard structural labor-supply models overstate preference heterogeneity by absorbing unequal access to jobs into tastes. The normative interpretation is responsibility-sensitive but empirically grounded: unequal opportunities are treated as compensation-relevant circumstances, while preferences are interpreted separately. The paper thus shifts the focus from welfare ranking or reform incidence to a structural decomposition of welfare inequality.
