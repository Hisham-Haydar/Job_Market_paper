This paper studies how much inequality in money-metric well-being is driven by unequal job opportunities rather than by heterogeneous preferences. I model labor supply as choice among latent job packages rather than as choice from a common unconstrained set of hours, and I estimate both preferences over consumption and leisure and the distribution of feasible job opportunities across individuals. Using the estimated model, I construct an opportunity-sensitive welfare measure and decompose welfare inequality into components attributable to opportunity heterogeneity, preference heterogeneity, and their interaction. The project contributes to three literatures: constrained discrete-choice labor supply, empirical welfare analysis under heterogeneous preferences, and inequality of opportunity. Its central claim is that conventional structural labor-supply models without explicit opportunity constraints may overstate preference heterogeneity by absorbing unequal access to jobs into tastes. A first empirical prototype uses a single-country application with tax-benefit simulation to map job packages into disposable income and to quantify how much welfare inequality would fall under counterfactual equalization of opportunity distributions across circumstance groups such as region and education.
