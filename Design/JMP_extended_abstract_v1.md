**Opportunity Constraints as a Driver of Welfare Inequality: A Latent-Jobs Structural Decomposition Approach**

**1. Motivation**

Structural labor-supply models are routinely used to infer preferences and to evaluate welfare consequences of taxes, transfers, and labor-market reforms. Yet in many labor markets individuals do not choose hours from a common budget set. They choose among jobs that differ in hours, wages, and other attributes, and the feasible set itself varies across individuals. Once this is recognized, observed inequality in hours, earnings, and disposable income becomes normatively ambiguous: the same observed bundle may be an unconstrained optimum for one person and a constrained second-best for another. The project is motivated by the concern that standard models may absorb these opportunity differences into taste heterogeneity and thereby distort both behavioral interpretation and welfare analysis.  

**2. Main research question**

The paper asks how much observed inequality in money-metric well-being is generated by unequal job opportunities rather than by heterogeneous preferences, once labor supply is modeled as discrete choice among latent job packages and welfare is evaluated conditional on constrained feasible sets. Put differently, the empirical issue is whether conventional structural labor-supply models overstate preference heterogeneity because unequal access to jobs is left implicit.  

**3. Literature gap**

The project sits at the intersection of four literatures: constrained discrete-choice labor supply, latent jobs and restricted choices, empirical welfare analysis for discrete choice, and inequality-of-opportunity or fairness measurement. The gap is not that these strands do not exist; it is that they have not yet been assembled into a single empirical pipeline. Closest structural studies estimate constrained labor supply and compute welfare or reform effects, but typically treat welfare evaluation as the endpoint and the opportunity structure as background modeling architecture. Welfare-comparison papers with heterogeneous preferences, by contrast, often stop short of modeling latent job opportunities explicitly. What is especially missing is an empirical decomposition architecture that attributes welfare inequality to opportunities versus preferences in an order-independent way. That missing decomposition layer is precisely where the paper locates its contribution.   

**4. Core mechanism: opportunities versus preferences**

The paper treats opportunities and preferences as analytically distinct objects. Preferences determine how consumption and leisure are valued once a job package is available. The opportunity mechanism determines which job packages are feasible and how their distribution varies across observable circumstances such as region and education. If the opportunity side is omitted, the model has a built-in tendency to relabel constrained outcomes as tastes. The paper therefore treats opportunities not as a residual but as a first-class structural object. Under a responsibility-sensitive interpretation, unequal opportunities are compensation-relevant circumstances, while preference heterogeneity is separated analytically rather than allowed to absorb constraints. This keeps the normative interpretation disciplined without turning the project into a separate axiomatic theory paper.   

**5. Empirical strategy**

Empirically, the design is a single-country structural application built around a RURO or latent-jobs framework. Individuals choose among discrete job packages defined parsimoniously by hours categories and wage or earnings categories, with richer job attributes added only if the data support them. For each person–alternative pair, tax-benefit microsimulation maps the job package into disposable income; microsimulation therefore serves as an input into the budget mapping rather than as the paper’s core contribution. The estimated objects are the utility parameters governing consumption-leisure trade-offs, an opportunity mechanism governing the distribution of feasible job packages across individuals and circumstance groups, and, if needed, a parsimonious wage or earnings offer structure. The aim is to estimate both the demand side and the opportunity side within one unified structural system.   

**6. Welfare object**

The welfare object is money-metric and opportunity-sensitive. Rather than evaluating welfare relative to a universal hours menu, the paper computes welfare conditional on the constrained feasible set the individual actually faces. In the prototype, the intended object is equivalent disposable income at a reference state of non-employment and full leisure: the cash amount that makes the reference state utility-equivalent to the expected utility generated by the estimated opportunity set. This design keeps the welfare layer empirical and globally interpretable while remaining distinct from a separate theory project on the axiomatic measurement of well-being in a world of jobs.  

**7. Decomposition strategy**

Decomposition is the paper’s central output. The proposal is to compute inequality in individual welfare levels and then compare it with counterfactual welfare distributions obtained by equalizing the opportunity mechanism across circumstance groups, neutralizing preference heterogeneity, and applying both together. An order-independent Shapley-Shorrocks rule then attributes baseline welfare inequality to opportunity heterogeneity, preference heterogeneity, and their interaction. This is a stronger object than a welfare ranking. A ranking answers who appears better or worse off under a given metric; a decomposition answers what drives inequality in that metric and therefore which mechanisms matter quantitatively. That shift—from levels to mechanisms—is the reason decomposition is a stronger JMP contribution than a country ranking or another standard reform-incidence exercise.   

**8. Expected contribution**

The expected contribution is threefold. First, the paper adds to constrained discrete-choice labor supply by estimating latent job opportunities rather than treating all heterogeneity as preference heterogeneity. Second, it contributes to empirical welfare analysis by constructing a money-metric welfare object that is explicitly conditional on constrained opportunities. Third, it contributes to inequality analysis by making decomposition, rather than reform incidence or cross-country ranking, the central scientific object. The normative content remains limited but important: responsibility for opportunities justifies how the welfare metric and counterfactuals are interpreted, but the paper remains an empirical structural JMP rather than a general philosophical treatment of responsibility.   

**9. Main risk and identification challenge**

The main challenge is identification. In this class of models, opportunity heterogeneity and leisure preferences can be weakly separated, especially when the prototype uses coarse hours bins and wage terciles. A poor specification could fit observed choices by shifting taste parameters rather than opportunity probabilities, making the decomposition look mechanical rather than informative. For that reason, the paper needs a parsimonious opportunity parameterization, clear circumstance definitions, an explicit identification note, and sensitivity checks using alternative circumstance partitions or welfare inequality indices. The objective is not to claim more than the data can sustain, but to show that the opportunity component of welfare inequality is empirically meaningful and reasonably stable.   

**10. First empirical prototype**

The first prototype is deliberately narrow and concrete. It uses Belgium in a single-country EU-SILC plus EUROMOD setting, focusing initially on prime-age childless single women aged 25–54 and excluding students, retirees, the disabled, and the self-employed. Job choice is represented by 16 alternatives: non-employment plus five weekly-hours bins crossed with three gross hourly wage terciles. Opportunity heterogeneity varies by broad region × education cells, while preference heterogeneity is captured by observed age terms and two latent taste types. The baseline inequality object is the Gini of individual welfare levels. The first empirical output is one table reporting the welfare Gini under the baseline, under opportunity equalization, under preference neutralization, and under both, together with one stacked-bar figure showing the Shapley decomposition. The purpose of this prototype is not to settle every identification issue, but to establish whether the opportunity share of welfare inequality is quantitatively nontrivial and stable enough to justify the full paper.
