## Slide 1

**Title**
**Research question**

**Final bullet points**

* How much of observed inequality in money-metric well-being is due to **unequal job opportunities** rather than **heterogeneous preferences**?
* Labor supply is modeled as choice among **latent job packages**, not free choice on a common menu.
* The paper is about **decomposition**, not country ranking.
* Belgium is the baseline application; Europe is extension only.

**Speaker script**
Let me begin with the question exactly as I want the audience to keep it in mind throughout the talk. I ask how much of observed inequality in money-metric well-being is due to unequal job opportunities rather than heterogeneous preferences, once labor supply is modeled as choice among latent job packages. That wording matters. This is not primarily a paper about labor-supply elasticities, and it is not a welfare-ranking exercise across countries. The object of the paper is the decomposition of welfare inequality once jobs are treated as constrained opportunities rather than as freely chosen points on a common budget set. Empirically, I take Belgium as the baseline application and focus on prime-age childless single women. Conceptually, the contribution is to connect three layers that are often kept separate: a structural labor-supply model with latent opportunities, an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare measure, and a decomposition that attributes welfare inequality to opportunities and preferences. The central claim is not that preferences do not matter. It is that a meaningful part of what standard models may read as taste heterogeneity is better understood as unequal access to jobs, and that this changes how we measure welfare inequality.

## Slide 2

**Title**
**Paper in a nutshell**

**Final bullet points**

* Estimate a structural labor-supply model with **preferences + latent opportunities**.
* Compute **opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare**.
* Decompose welfare inequality into **opportunity** and **preference** components.
* Headline result: opportunities explain **0.039 Gini points**, or **43.8% of the explained component**.

**Speaker script**
This slide is the paper in one minute. First, I estimate a structural labor-supply model in which individuals differ not only in preferences, but also in access to latent job packages. Second, I use that estimated structure to compute an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare level, rather than relying on disposable income alone or on a welfare metric that assumes a common feasible set. Third, I decompose welfare inequality into an opportunity component and a preference component using counterfactual equalizations and a Shapley-Shorrocks attribution rule. The headline result in the Belgium baseline is that opportunities explain 0.039 Gini points of welfare inequality. That is 43.8 percent of the explained component and 21.0 percent of baseline welfare inequality. So the argument of the paper is already visible here: once feasible job sets are modeled explicitly, unequal opportunities emerge as a quantitatively important source of welfare inequality. The rest of the talk just opens that black box in a disciplined way.

## Slide 3

**Title**
**Why should we care?**

**Final bullet points**

* The same observed bundle can be a **chosen optimum** for one person and a **constrained second-best** for another.
* If opportunities are omitted, standard models risk absorbing them into **tastes**.
* Then both the **positive interpretation** and the **normative interpretation** of inequality are distorted.
* This matters for how we think about **compensation**, **responsibility**, and **redistribution**.

**Speaker script**
The reason this matters is simple, but it is not superficial. Take two women observed at the same hours-income bundle. One may be there because that bundle is genuinely her preferred option from a rich feasible set. The other may be there because better job packages are missing from her feasible set. If the model does not represent that distinction, it is forced to explain both cases through preferences. That distorts the science, because part of what looks like taste heterogeneity is really variation in opportunities. But it also distorts the welfare interpretation, because the same observed outcome is evaluated as if it came from the same choice environment. That is exactly where the social relevance comes in. If society cares about the distinction between what people choose and what they are able to access, then the welfare metric should care too. So this paper is not only about improving the fit of a labor-supply model. It is about making welfare measurement more faithful to the distinction between compensation-relevant inequality and inequality that may legitimately remain tied to preferences.

## Slide 4

**Title**
**What the literature does / what I do**

**Final bullet points**

* **Constrained labor-supply papers**: model behavior, usually stop before decomposition.
* **Welfare papers with heterogeneous preferences**: measure welfare, usually without explicit opportunity structure.
* **Fairness / IOp papers**: provide the normative language, but not this latent-jobs empirical pipeline.
* **I combine all three** in one structural decomposition framework.

**Speaker script**
The cleanest way to position the paper is against three adjacent literatures. First, constrained structural labor-supply papers already show that work should often be modeled as choice over discrete job packages rather than unconstrained hours points. But those papers usually stop at behavior, elasticities, or reform incidence. Second, there is a welfare literature with heterogeneous preferences that computes money-metric welfare objects, sometimes quite elegantly, but typically without an explicit opportunity mechanism. Third, there is the fairness and inequality-of-opportunity literature, which gives the normative language for thinking about responsibility and compensation, but usually not in the form of a latent-jobs structural labor-supply design. What I do is to combine these three pieces in one empirical framework. I estimate a model with preferences and opportunities, I compute welfare conditional on the feasible set, and I decompose welfare inequality into opportunity and preference components. So the contribution is not one more labor-supply model and not one more welfare metric in isolation. It is the integration of behavior, welfare, and fairness in one decomposition exercise.

## Slide 5

**Title**
**Baseline design: the clean first application**

**Final bullet points**

* Data: **Belgian EU-SILC + EUROMOD** baseline tax-benefit rules.
* Sample: **3,284** childless single women aged **25–54**.
* Labor supply over **16 job packages**: non-employment + **5 hours bins × 3 wage terciles**.
* Opportunity heterogeneity: **region × education**; preference heterogeneity: **age, age², two latent taste types**.

**Speaker script**
The baseline application is deliberately narrow, and I see that as a strength rather than a weakness. I use Belgian EU-SILC matched to EUROMOD baseline tax-benefit rules, and I restrict attention to 3,284 childless single women aged 25 to 54. This restriction removes three major sources of avoidable complexity in a first paper: joint household choice, childcare-related tax-benefit interactions, and self-employment measurement issues. Labor supply is modeled over 16 job packages: non-employment plus five hours bins crossed with three wage terciles. On the opportunity side, heterogeneity is allowed to vary with region and education. On the preference side, heterogeneity is disciplined through age, age squared, and two latent taste types. So the Belgium baseline is not meant to be the final scope of the agenda. It is meant to be the cleanest first environment in which the opportunity-versus-preference question can be identified and interpreted without the paper dissolving into generality too early. Europe matters as scale-up. Belgium is where the mechanism is first established.

## Slide 6

**Title**
**Identification and welfare in two sentences**

**Final bullet points**

* **Identification**: the model separates the **ranking** of job packages from **access** to job packages.
* Region × education disciplines the opportunity block; age and latent types discipline the preference block.
* **Welfare**: equivalent monthly disposable income at a **non-employment/full-leisure reference**, conditional on the feasible set.
* **Decomposition**: compare baseline welfare inequality with opportunity-equalized and preference-neutralized counterfactuals.

**Speaker script**
The identification logic can be stated very briefly. The model separates two margins: how individuals rank job packages, and which job packages are statistically available to them. Region and education discipline the availability side; age and latent types discipline the preference side. On the welfare side, I compute equivalent monthly disposable income at a common non-employment, full-leisure reference state, but conditional on the estimated feasible set. That gives me a welfare level that remains comparable across individuals while respecting the fact that they do not all face the same opportunities. Once I have those welfare levels, the decomposition is conceptually simple: I compare baseline welfare inequality with counterfactual inequality after equalizing opportunities, after neutralizing preferences, and after doing both. So this slide is meant to reassure the audience that the paper does have structure, but that structure can be explained in two sentences without drowning the main idea in notation.

## Slide 7

**Title**
**Main result**

**Final bullet points**

* Disposable-income Gini: **0.164**; opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini: **0.186**.
* Opportunity equalization lowers the welfare Gini to **0.151**.
* Preference equalization lowers it to **0.140**; equalizing both lowers it to **0.097**.
* Opportunity heterogeneity explains **21.0%** of baseline welfare inequality.

**Speaker script**
Now the main result. In the Belgium baseline, disposable-income inequality and welfare inequality are not the same object. The disposable-income Gini is 0.164, while the opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini is 0.186. So once unequal feasible job sets are brought into the welfare measure, measured inequality rises. I then ask what happens under counterfactual equalizations. If I equalize opportunities, the welfare Gini falls from 0.186 to 0.151. If I equalize preferences, it falls to 0.140. If I equalize both, it falls to 0.097. Under the Shapley-Shorrocks decomposition, opportunity heterogeneity accounts for 0.039 Gini points, which is 43.8 percent of the explained component and 21.0 percent of baseline welfare inequality. Preferences still explain slightly more, at 0.050 Gini points. That is exactly the balance I want the audience to take away. The paper is not claiming that opportunities dominate everything else. It is claiming that once they are modeled explicitly, they explain too much of welfare inequality to be treated as background friction. If the talk stopped here, that would already be the complete message: the question, the contribution, the identification logic, and the main empirical result are all on the table.
