## Slide 15 — Welfare inequality exceeds income inequality once opportunities enter the welfare metric

**Suggested on-slide bullets**

* Mean monthly welfare: **€1,718**; median: **€1,681**
* 10th percentile welfare: **€1,079**; 90th percentile: **€2,452**
* Disposable-income Gini: **0.164**
* Opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini: **0.186**

**Speaker script**
Having shown that the model behaves like a real structural labor-supply model, let me return to the main contribution. The first payoff is that welfare inequality and income inequality are not the same object once opportunities enter the welfare metric. In the Belgium baseline, the opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini is 0.186, while the disposable-income Gini is 0.164. So measured inequality rises when I evaluate people not only by what they earn, but by what they can actually access in the labor market. That is the key economic point. Two individuals with similar disposable income can have different welfare once one reaches that outcome from a much poorer feasible set. So this slide is the bridge from structural estimation to welfare measurement: once feasible job sets are part of the model, the welfare distribution no longer coincides with the income distribution.

## Slide 16 — The decomposition comes from transparent counterfactuals, not from residual accounting

**Suggested on-slide bullets**

* Baseline welfare Gini: **0.186**
* Opportunity equalized: **0.151**
* Preference equalized: **0.140**
* Both equalized: **0.097**

**Speaker script**
The next step is to show that the decomposition is built from transparent objects. I start from the baseline welfare Gini of 0.186. I then compute three counterfactuals. In the first, I equalize opportunities and the welfare Gini falls to 0.151. In the second, I equalize preferences and it falls to 0.140. In the third, I equalize both and it falls to 0.097. So before I ever attach shares or labels, the audience can already see the full set of welfare distributions behind the decomposition. That matters because the attribution is not coming from a black-box regression residual. It is coming from explicit structural equalization exercises. This is also where the Shapley-Shorrocks logic earns its role: once those counterfactuals are defined, I can attribute the explained component in an order-invariant way rather than making the result depend on the sequence in which I remove factors.

## Slide 17 — The main decomposition result is balanced, which is why it is credible

**Suggested on-slide bullets**

* Opportunity contribution: **0.039** Gini points
* Preference contribution: **0.050** Gini points
* Residual/common component: **0.097**
* Explained component: **43.8% opportunity / 56.2% preference**

**Speaker script**
This is the main decomposition slide in its most compact form. Opportunity heterogeneity contributes 0.039 Gini points. Preference heterogeneity contributes 0.050. The residual or common component after both equalizations is 0.097. Put differently, the explained component splits 43.8 percent to opportunities and 56.2 percent to preferences. I think the result is strongest precisely because it is balanced. The paper is not trying to prove that everything is opportunity-driven. It is showing that once opportunities are modeled explicitly, they explain a large and previously hidden share of welfare inequality. That makes the empirical message both stronger and more defensible. Opportunities matter a great deal, but the result does not depend on claiming that preferences are negligible.

## Slide 18 — The level of the opportunity contribution depends on responsibility assumptions, but it does not disappear

**Suggested on-slide bullets**

* If full **region × education** is compensable: opportunity component = **0.039**
* If only **education** is compensable: **0.031**
* If only **region** is compensable: **0.026**
* Opportunity still explains about **14–21%** of baseline welfare inequality

**Speaker script**
At this point, the natural question is whether the opportunity share is simply an artifact of one ethical choice. So here I make the responsibility-sensitive element explicit. If I treat the full region-by-education opportunity gradient as compensable, the opportunity component is 0.039 Gini points. If I tighten the criterion and compensate only education, it falls to 0.031. If I compensate only region, it falls further to 0.026. That movement is exactly what I want to see. It shows that the measured opportunity contribution is criterion-dependent, which is what the fairness literature would lead us to expect. But the important point is that it does not vanish. Across these reasonable responsibility-sensitive definitions, unequal opportunities still explain about 14 to 21 percent of baseline welfare inequality. So the paper’s contribution is not one privileged fairness rule. It is the decomposition of welfare inequality in a latent-jobs labor-supply setting under explicit and transparent responsibility assumptions.

## Slide 19 — This is why decomposition matters more than welfare levels alone

**Suggested on-slide bullets**

* A welfare level tells us **where** people stand
* A decomposition tells us **why** inequality exists
* Policy implications differ if inequality is driven by **job access** rather than **preferences**
* That is why the paper’s main object is a decomposition, not a ranking

**Speaker script**
This slide is really about why I think the paper’s object is scientifically stronger than a welfare ranking. A welfare level tells us where people stand. A decomposition tells us why inequality exists. That distinction matters because the policy implications differ depending on the source of dispersion. If inequality is mostly driven by limited access to decent job packages, then the relevant policy margin is not the same as if inequality is mostly tied to preferences. This is also why I deliberately avoid turning the paper into a cross-country ranking exercise. Papers like Bargain and coauthors make clear that welfare rankings are highly sensitive to preferences and metric choice. My paper is after a different object: a mechanism question. What component of welfare inequality is opportunity-driven once opportunities are modeled explicitly? That is what makes decomposition more informative than levels alone.
