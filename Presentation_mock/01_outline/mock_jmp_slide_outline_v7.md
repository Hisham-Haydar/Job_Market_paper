Below is a tightened 14-slide structure that moves the **question**, **paper-in-a-nutshell**, **contribution**, and **main result** earlier, while keeping the first 7 slides fully self-contained. The sequencing also matches the more mature seminar logic in your workshop deck’s early “Introduction / What we do / Our contribution” arc: question first, contribution early, then model and payoff. 

## Core 15-minute talk: Slides 1–7

### Slide 1 — Research question

**Bullet points**

* How much of observed inequality in money-metric well-being is due to **unequal job opportunities** rather than **heterogeneous preferences**?
* Labor supply is modeled as choice among **latent job packages**, not free choice on a common menu.
* The paper is about **decomposition**, not country ranking.
* Belgium is the baseline application; Europe is extension only.

**What visual goes there**

* One clean title visual: **latent jobs and opportunities → welfare measurement → inequality decomposition**.

**Main takeaway**

* The paper asks whether welfare inequality is partly misread as taste heterogeneity when opportunities are not modeled explicitly. 

### Slide 2 — Paper in a nutshell

**Bullet points**

* Estimate a structural labor-supply model with **preferences + latent opportunities**.
* Compute **opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare**.
* Decompose welfare inequality into **opportunity** and **preference** components.
* Headline result: opportunities explain **0.039 Gini points**, or **43.8% of the explained component**.

**What visual goes there**

* A three-column “paper in a nutshell” slide: **Model / Welfare / Result**, with the decomposition figure thumbnail on the right.

**Main takeaway**

* Once feasible job sets are modeled, unequal opportunities explain a quantitatively large share of welfare inequality. 

### Slide 3 — Why should we care?

**Bullet points**

* The same observed bundle can be a **chosen optimum** for one person and a **constrained second-best** for another.
* If opportunities are omitted, standard models risk absorbing them into **tastes**.
* Then both the **positive interpretation** and the **normative interpretation** of inequality are distorted.
* This matters for how we think about **compensation**, **responsibility**, and **redistribution**.

**What visual goes there**

* Two-person schematic: same observed hours-income point, different feasible job sets.

**Main takeaway**

* Opportunity constraints matter because they change both the science of labor-supply heterogeneity and the welfare meaning of observed inequality. 

### Slide 4 — What the literature does / what I do

**Bullet points**

* **Constrained labor-supply papers**: model behavior, usually stop before decomposition.
* **Welfare papers with heterogeneous preferences**: measure welfare, usually without explicit opportunity structure.
* **Fairness/IOp papers**: provide the normative language, but not this latent-jobs empirical pipeline.
* **I combine all three** in one structural decomposition framework.

**What visual goes there**

* Three-column literature map labeled **Behavior**, **Welfare**, **Fairness**, with a highlighted center box: **This paper**.

**Main takeaway**

* The contribution is integration: a latent-jobs labor-supply model, an opportunity-sensitive welfare metric, and a decomposition of welfare inequality in one design. 

### Slide 5 — Baseline design: the clean first application

**Bullet points**

* Data: **Belgian EU-SILC + EUROMOD** baseline tax-benefit rules.
* Sample: **3,284** childless single women aged **25–54**.
* Labor supply over **16 job packages**: non-employment + **5 hours bins × 3 wage terciles**.
* Opportunity heterogeneity: **region × education**; preference heterogeneity: **age, age², two latent taste types**.

**What visual goes there**

* Belgium map plus a compact design table and a tiny 16-package grid.

**Main takeaway**

* The Belgium baseline is narrow by design so the opportunity-versus-preference question is identified and interpretable in a clean first-pass setting.

### Slide 6 — Identification and welfare in two sentences

**Bullet points**

* **Identification**: the model separates the **ranking** of job packages from **access** to job packages.
* Region × education disciplines the opportunity block; age and latent types discipline the preference block.
* **Welfare**: equivalent monthly disposable income at a **non-employment/full-leisure reference**, conditional on the feasible set.
* **Decomposition**: compare baseline welfare inequality with opportunity-equalized and preference-neutralized counterfactuals.

**What visual goes there**

* One very simple flow chart: **Estimate model → compute welfare → equalize opportunities/preferences → decompose Gini**.

**Main takeaway**

* The identification logic and the welfare construction are simple enough to explain quickly and both point directly to the decomposition result.

### Slide 7 — Main result

**Bullet points**

* Disposable-income Gini: **0.164**; opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini: **0.186**.
* Opportunity equalization lowers the welfare Gini to **0.151**.
* Preference equalization lowers it to **0.140**; equalizing both lowers it to **0.097**.
* Opportunity heterogeneity explains **21.0%** of baseline welfare inequality.

**What visual goes there**

* Main stacked-bar decomposition figure, with a tiny inset table showing baseline, opportunity-equalized, preference-equalized, and both-equalized Ginis.

**Main takeaway**

* Opportunity heterogeneity is quantitatively important — not dominant over preferences, but far too large to be treated as background friction.

## Continuation: Slides 8–14

### Slide 8 — The empirical pattern the model must explain

**Bullet points**

* Employment ranges from **62.4%** for low-education women in Brussels to **86.5%** for high-education women in Flanders.
* Non-employment is **22.4%** of the sample; standard-hours work is the dominant mode.
* So the raw data already show large **region × education** gradients in market attachment.
* A good model must fit these gradients, not just average participation.

**What visual goes there**

* Left: heatmap of observed employment by region × education.
* Right: observed distribution across labor-supply states.

**Main takeaway**

* The opportunity block is motivated by strong, structured gradients already visible in the raw data. 

### Slide 9 — Opportunity estimates: unequal access to jobs is economically structured

**Bullet points**

* Relative offer intensity is lowest for **low-education women in Brussels and Wallonia**.
* Implied total offer intensity ranges from **0.65** to **1.16** across region × education cells.
* **Standard-hours** packages are much more available than fringe-hour packages.
* **Top-wage** packages are relatively scarcer on average.

**What visual goes there**

* Heatmap of implied relative offer intensity plus a small bar chart for package composition.

**Main takeaway**

* The opportunity mechanism is not a residual term; it is an estimated, economically interpretable map of unequal access to job packages. 

### Slide 10 — Preference estimates: heterogeneity is real, but disciplined

**Bullet points**

* Log disposable income enters positively: coefficient **2.946**.
* Two latent taste types: the more leisure-oriented type makes up **39.0%** of the sample.
* Work fixed cost is sizable: **-1.324**.
* The marginal value of leisure rises with age, but concavely.

**What visual goes there**

* Compact coefficient plot or stripped-down table for the preference block.

**Main takeaway**

* Preferences matter, but once opportunities are modeled explicitly they no longer need to absorb the whole heterogeneity pattern. 

### Slide 11 — What the benchmark misses

**Bullet points**

* Full model pseudo-R²: **0.271** versus **0.214** in the common-choice-set benchmark.
* Exact 1-of-16 package hit rate: **39.2%** versus **33.8%**.
* Employment classification hit rate: **82.4%**.
* The main gain is recovering the **region × education** gradients the benchmark compresses.

**What visual goes there**

* Two-panel figure: observed-versus-predicted employment by region × education on the left; benchmark-fit comparison table on the right.

**Main takeaway**

* Explicit opportunities materially improve fit exactly where the omitted-opportunity problem should matter most. 

### Slide 12 — Behavioral validation: labor-supply elasticities

**Bullet points**

* Uncompensated arc elasticities from a **1% proportional wage increase**, holding job-offer probabilities fixed.
* Overall elasticities: **0.21** extensive, **0.11** intensive, **0.32** total hours.
* Total hours elasticities by education: **0.43 low**, **0.33 medium**, **0.23 high**.
* This is a **behavioral-validation** result, not the paper’s main contribution.

**What visual goes there**

* Stacked bar chart by education group, with extensive margin at the bottom and intensive margin at the top.

**Main takeaway**

* The model behaves like a real structural labor-supply model, but the paper’s payoff remains the welfare decomposition, not the elasticities.

### Slide 13 — Responsibility-sensitive decomposition

**Bullet points**

* If the full **region × education** gradient is compensable, the opportunity component is **0.039** Gini points.
* If only **education** is compensable, it falls to **0.031**.
* If only **region** is compensable, it falls to **0.026**.
* So the exact level is criterion-dependent, but the opportunity contribution remains nontrivial.

**What visual goes there**

* Three-bar responsibility-sensitivity figure.

**Main takeaway**

* The measured opportunity contribution depends on responsibility assumptions, but across reasonable criteria it remains economically important. 

### Slide 14 — What the committee should remember

**Bullet points**

* This is a completed structural labor-supply paper with a **latent-jobs** opportunity block and a **behaviorally validated** model.
* Its main contribution is an **opportunity-sensitive decomposition of welfare inequality**.
* Belgium is the clean baseline application.
* Broader EUROMOD Europe is the next step in scale-up, not the identity of the paper.

**What visual goes there**

* Closing slide with four boxes: **Question / Method / Result / Broader agenda**.

**Main takeaway**

* The contribution is not a ranking and not a design memo; it is a completed empirical decomposition of welfare inequality in a latent-jobs labor-supply setting.
