## Slide 1 — Opportunity constraints and welfare inequality

* I ask how much welfare inequality is driven by unequal job opportunities rather than heterogeneous preferences.
* The paper studies labor supply as choice over latent job packages, not over a common unconstrained hours set.
* The central output is a decomposition of welfare inequality into opportunity and preference components.
* Belgium is the baseline application; cross-country extension is only a future scalability margin.

**Visual:** One clean title slide figure: “Observed choices” feeding into “preferences + latent opportunities,” feeding into “welfare inequality decomposition.”

**Speaker notes:**
This paper starts from a simple but important concern: labor-supply data confound what people want with what they are actually able to access. In standard structural models, too much of that heterogeneity is often loaded onto tastes. I show that this is not just a behavioral issue; it changes welfare measurement and inequality decomposition. The paper therefore asks a mechanism question, not a ranking question.  

**Estimated speaking time:** 2.0 minutes

## Slide 2 — Why this question matters

* The same observed bundle can be first-best for one person and constrained second-best for another.
* If opportunity heterogeneity is hidden inside taste heterogeneity, both estimation and welfare interpretation are distorted.
* This makes decomposition more informative than country ranking.
* Under a responsibility-sensitive view, unequal opportunities are compensation-relevant.

**Visual:** Two individuals at the same observed hours-income point, but with different feasible job sets drawn behind them.

**Speaker notes:**
The intuition is straightforward. Suppose two women end up in the same low-hours, low-income package. One may genuinely prefer it; the other may be there because better packages are missing from her feasible set. If the model does not distinguish those two cases, it will misread opportunity deprivation as preference for leisure. That is why I organize the paper around decomposition rather than around welfare rankings.  

**Estimated speaking time:** 2.0 minutes

## Slide 3 — What is missing in the literature

* Constrained structural labor-supply papers usually stop at reform incidence or welfare effects.
* Welfare-comparison papers with heterogeneous preferences usually do not model opportunities explicitly.
* Inequality-of-opportunity papers provide the normative language, but not this structural estimation-to-decomposition pipeline.
* My contribution is to connect estimation, welfare measurement, and decomposition in one framework.

**Visual:** Four-box literature map: constrained labor supply, latent jobs/identification, welfare analysis, fairness/equality of opportunity.

**Speaker notes:**
I am building on four literatures, but the contribution is in their integration. Existing structural papers often include opportunity restrictions in the model, but treat them as background architecture rather than as an inequality mechanism. Existing welfare-comparison papers take preference heterogeneity seriously, but are weaker on explicit opportunity sets. My paper treats opportunities as a first-class object all the way through: estimation, welfare evaluation, and decomposition.  

**Estimated speaking time:** 2.0 minutes

## Slide 4 — Baseline empirical design

**Visual:** Compact design table with sample, alternatives, opportunity shifters, preference shifters, and welfare object.

**Speaker notes:**
The first version is intentionally narrow. I use Belgium because it is feasible, structurally coherent, and consistent with the RURO-style application already in the corpus. I restrict to prime-age childless single women because this strips out joint household choice, childcare interactions, and self-employment measurement problems. The design is narrow by construction because identification is easier to defend when the prototype is disciplined.  

**Estimated speaking time:** 2.0 minutes

## Slide 5 — Model logic: latent jobs and opportunity-sensitive welfare

* Each individual chooses one package from a latent feasible set of job opportunities.
* Utility depends on disposable income and leisure, with observed and latent taste heterogeneity.
* Welfare is measured as equivalent disposable income at a non-employment/full-leisure reference.
* Crucially, welfare is computed conditional on the constrained feasible set, not on a universal menu.

**Visual:** One slide with a simple model flowchart plus the welfare-object equation in words, not heavy algebra.

**Speaker notes:**
The key modeling move is to separate utility from opportunity availability. The model does not say everyone chooses from the same set of hour points. It says individuals choose from latent job packages whose availability varies systematically with observed circumstances. Once I estimate that structure, I translate expected utility into an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare level defined relative to a common non-employment/full-leisure reference. That gives me a welfare object with a clear economic interpretation.  

**Estimated speaking time:** 2.5 minutes

## Slide 6 — Identification and estimation strategy

* The main empirical challenge is separating leisure tastes from opportunity probabilities.
* I keep the opportunity side parsimonious: region × education cells.
* I keep the preference side disciplined: age shifters and two latent classes.
* The paper compares the full model to a common-choice-set benchmark to show what explicit opportunity modeling adds.

**Visual:** Split diagram: utility block on the left, opportunity block on the right, common-choice-set benchmark underneath.

**Speaker notes:**
The paper’s main risk is weak separation between preferences and opportunities. That is exactly why I do not start with rich random coefficients or a very broad job taxonomy. Instead, I use a deliberately parsimonious opportunity mechanism and a tightly controlled preference structure. The benchmark comparison matters because it lets me show that explicit opportunity heterogeneity is not just decorative; it improves fit and changes the decomposition.  

**Estimated speaking time:** 2.0 minutes

## Slide 7 — Main results: opportunities explain a substantial share of welfare inequality

* The baseline welfare Gini is 0.186, above the disposable-income Gini of 0.164.
* Equalizing opportunities reduces the welfare Gini to 0.151; equalizing preferences reduces it to 0.140.
* Equalizing both reduces the welfare Gini to 0.097.
* In the Shapley decomposition, opportunities explain 0.039 Gini points, or 43.8% of the explained component; preferences explain 0.050.

**Visual:** Main figure: stacked bar decomposition of the baseline welfare Gini, with one small inset table for the four Gini numbers.

**Speaker notes:**
This is the core result, and this is the natural stopping point for a 15-minute talk. Welfare inequality is larger than income inequality once the welfare object respects constrained opportunities. Equalizing opportunities compresses welfare inequality materially, though preferences still account for slightly more. The key message is therefore balanced rather than rhetorical: opportunities matter a great deal, but they do not swamp preferences. That is exactly the kind of result that supports a serious structural decomposition paper. 

**Estimated speaking time:** 2.5 minutes
