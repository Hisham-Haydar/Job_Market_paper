## Slide 1 — The question: how much welfare inequality is really opportunity inequality?

* I study welfare inequality in a labor-supply setting with constrained, latent job opportunities.
* The core question is whether standard models over-attribute heterogeneity to preferences because they under-model opportunity constraints.
* I ask how much inequality in money-metric well-being is due to unequal job opportunities rather than tastes.
* The paper’s payoff is a structural decomposition of welfare inequality into opportunity and preference components.  

**Visual:** One clean conceptual diagram: observed choice/outcome on the left, latent opportunities and preferences in the middle, welfare inequality decomposition on the right.

**Speaker notes:**
This paper starts from a simple concern: observed labor-supply choices confound what people want with what they are actually able to access. In standard structural work, that confound is often absorbed into preference heterogeneity. My claim is that this matters not only behaviorally but normatively, because welfare inequality can then be misread. So the paper asks a decomposition question rather than a ranking question: how much of welfare inequality is really generated by unequal opportunities?  

## Slide 2 — Why this matters

* The same observed bundle can reflect unconstrained choice for one person and constrained second-best for another.
* If opportunities are latent and unequal, welfare comparisons based on a common feasible set are distorted.
* This matters for both estimation and welfare measurement.
* It is also normatively central if opportunities are treated as compensation-relevant circumstances.  

**Visual:** Two-individual illustration: same observed hours/income point, but different feasible job sets.

**Speaker notes:**
The key intuition is that identical observed outcomes need not have identical welfare content. One woman may choose a low-hours package because it is her preferred option; another may choose it because better job packages are not in her feasible set. If I ignore that distinction, I risk coding opportunity deprivation as taste for leisure. That contaminates both the structural interpretation of the model and the welfare interpretation of inequality.  

## Slide 3 — The literature gap

* Structural labor-supply papers with constrained choices typically stop at reform incidence or welfare effects.
* Welfare-comparison papers with heterogeneous preferences typically do not model opportunities explicitly.
* Inequality-of-opportunity papers provide the normative language, but not this structural welfare pipeline.
* What is missing is one framework that treats opportunities consistently in estimation, welfare evaluation, and decomposition.  

**Visual:** Four-box literature map: constrained labor supply, identification of latent jobs, welfare analysis for discrete choice, equality of opportunity/fairness.

**Speaker notes:**
I am not claiming to be the first person to model constrained labor supply, and I am not claiming to be the first person to compute welfare from structural models. The gap is the lack of integration. Existing papers usually treat opportunity structure as background architecture rather than as an attributable driver of inequality. My contribution is to move that opportunity mechanism into the foreground and make decomposition the headline object.  

## Slide 4 — Model: latent jobs, not a universal hours choice set

* Each individual chooses one job package from a latent feasible set.
* Job packages are discrete alternatives defined by non-employment plus hours-category × wage-tercile combinations.
* The prototype uses 16 alternatives.
* Utility depends on disposable income, leisure, observed characteristics, and latent taste heterogeneity; opportunities depend on circumstances.  

**Visual:** Table of the 16 job packages: non-employment, then hours bins crossed with 3 wage terciles.

**Speaker notes:**
I model labor supply as choice among latent job packages rather than as free choice over hours on a common budget line. In the prototype, the menu is deliberately narrow: non-employment plus five hours bins crossed with three wage terciles, giving sixteen alternatives. Preferences govern the ranking of accessible packages, while the opportunity mechanism governs which types of packages are statistically available. That separation is exactly what lets the paper ask how much estimated heterogeneity is really about opportunities.  

## Slide 5 — Identification: separating opportunities from preferences

* Identification comes from jointly modeling utility and the opportunity mechanism.
* Opportunity heterogeneity varies parsimoniously with region × education.
* Preference heterogeneity is kept disciplined: age, age squared, and two latent taste types.
* The main empirical risk is weak separation between leisure tastes and opportunity probabilities.  

**Visual:** Schematic likelihood decomposition or parameter map: utility block versus opportunity block.

**Speaker notes:**
The hardest empirical issue is not estimation per se; it is separation. With limited data, a model can fit choices by shifting leisure preferences when the real object of interest is the opportunity distribution. That is why I keep the first version deliberately parsimonious: a simple circumstance partition on the opportunity side and a tightly controlled heterogeneity structure on the preference side. The paper is strongest when it is narrow and transparent rather than over-flexible.  

## Slide 6 — Welfare and decomposition framework

* Welfare is measured as opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare, defined relative to a non-employment/full-leisure reference state.
* Welfare is computed conditional on the constrained feasible set, not a universal menu.
* Baseline inequality is the Gini of individual welfare levels.
* Decomposition uses a two-factor Shapley-Shorrocks rule: opportunity heterogeneity versus preference heterogeneity.   

**Visual:** Equation-style slide with three lines: welfare object, welfare Gini, Shapley decomposition.

**Speaker notes:**
The welfare object is crucial. I do not evaluate individuals as if everyone faced the same feasible set; I compute money-metric welfare relative to the opportunity set implied by the estimated model. Once I have individual welfare levels, I summarize inequality with the Gini and then ask how much of that inequality disappears when I equalize opportunities, when I neutralize preferences, and when I do both. The Shapley rule matters because it avoids making the decomposition an artifact of sequencing.  

## Slide 7 — Data and empirical design

* Setting: Belgium, single-country EU-SILC + EUROMOD application.
* Sample: prime-age childless single women, ages 25–54.
* Exclusions: self-employed, students, retired, disabled, and observations with implausible hours or wages.
* Microsimulation is used only to compute disposable income for each person–alternative pair.  

**Visual:** Sample construction funnel plus a small Belgium map highlighting region × education cells.

**Speaker notes:**
I chose Belgium because it is a feasible single-country prototype with an existing RURO-style empirical basis and a tax-benefit engine that can map job packages into disposable income. I restrict attention to prime-age childless single women because this strips out joint household choice, childcare-related interactions, and self-employment measurement issues. That is not a claim that singles are the whole story; it is a claim that this is the cleanest first prototype. Microsimulation enters here as infrastructure, not as the paper’s identity.  

## Slide 8 — Main results

* Opportunity equalization materially reduces baseline welfare inequality.
* Preference neutralization also reduces inequality, but less than opportunity equalization in the baseline specification.
* The decomposition assigns a nontrivial share of welfare inequality to unequal job opportunities.
* Standard models that absorb these constraints into tastes would overstate the preference component of inequality. This is the core empirical message targeted by the design memo.  

**Visual:** Main figure as a stacked bar with Shapley shares; optional inset table with four welfare-Gini numbers: baseline, opportunity equalized, preference neutralized, both. 

**Speaker notes:**
The headline result is not a country ranking and not a reform incidence table. It is that a meaningful portion of welfare inequality appears to be opportunity-driven once the opportunity set is modeled explicitly. In the baseline decomposition, equalizing the region-by-education opportunity mechanism compresses welfare inequality by more than neutralizing the estimated taste distribution. The empirical interpretation is that conventional structural models likely misclassify some opportunity inequality as preference heterogeneity.  

## Slide 9 — Robustness and interpretation

* The main concern is weak identification of opportunities relative to leisure tastes.
* I therefore vary the circumstance partition and the opportunity parameterization in sensitivity checks.
* The paper’s interpretation is explicitly responsibility-sensitive: opportunities are compensation-relevant, preferences may not be.
* The aim is not to prove a unique moral metric, but to show that the opportunity component is empirically non-negligible under transparent assumptions.   

**Visual:** Robustness panel with two or three bars showing stable opportunity share under alternative partitions/specifications.

**Speaker notes:**
Any audience member will ask whether the decomposition is just mechanically produced by a particular parameterization. That is the right question. My answer is to keep the baseline transparent and then show that the opportunity share remains nontrivial under modest changes to the circumstance partition or opportunity specification. Normatively, I am explicit that the decomposition is read through a responsibility stance rather than smuggled in implicitly.  

## Slide 10 — Conclusion

* Unequal job opportunities are not just background friction; they are a measurable source of welfare inequality.
* A latent-jobs structural model changes both the interpretation of heterogeneity and the measurement of welfare inequality.
* The central contribution is an opportunity-sensitive decomposition, not a ranking exercise and not a microsimulation paper.
* The natural next step is to extend the baseline to richer opportunity definitions or policy counterfactuals once the core decomposition is established.  

**Visual:** One-slide takeaway box with “Estimation → Welfare → Decomposition” and one sentence under each.

**Speaker notes:**
The paper’s claim is conceptually simple but, I think, important. Once jobs are treated as constrained opportunities, welfare inequality should be decomposed using those opportunity constraints rather than burying them inside tastes. That delivers a more credible account of what part of inequality is compensable and what part is not. The broader agenda can later incorporate richer opportunity sets and reforms, but the hiring-committee value of this paper is the clean decomposition core.
