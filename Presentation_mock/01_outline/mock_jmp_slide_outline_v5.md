## Slide 1 — Main question: how much welfare inequality is really opportunity inequality?

**Bullet points**

* Main question: how much of observed inequality in money-metric well-being is attributable to unequal job opportunities rather than heterogeneous preferences, once labor supply is modeled as choice among latent jobs?
* The paper’s object is decomposition, not country ranking.
* Belgium is the baseline application; broader EUROMOD coverage is extension only.
* The paper combines structural labor supply, welfare measurement, and inequality decomposition in one design.

**What visual goes on the slide**

* A compact three-step visual: latent jobs and opportunities → opportunity-sensitive welfare → decomposition of welfare inequality.

**Speaker notes**

* I would open with the main question exactly as the paper is framed, because that immediately locates the contribution.
* This is not a generic labor-supply paper and not a generic welfare-ranking exercise.
* The central scientific object is the mechanism generating welfare inequality once job opportunities are treated as constrained and heterogeneous.
* Belgium is the empirical baseline, but the contribution is more general than the country application.

**Estimated speaking time**

* 2.0 minutes.  

## Slide 2 — Subquestion 1: do standard models overstate preference heterogeneity?

**Bullet points**

* The same observed bundle can be a chosen optimum for one person and a constrained second-best for another.
* If the model ignores constrained opportunities, it risks absorbing opportunity differences into tastes.
* That distorts both behavioral interpretation and welfare interpretation.
* The paper tests this by comparing an opportunity-sensitive model to a common-choice-set benchmark.

**What visual goes on the slide**

* Two-person schematic: same observed hours-income point, but different feasible job sets.

**Speaker notes**

* The first subquestion is really an identification problem with welfare consequences.
* Once feasible job sets differ across individuals, observed labor-supply choices no longer map cleanly into preferences alone.
* A standard common-choice-set model is then likely to explain constrained outcomes by inflating preference heterogeneity.
* That is why the opportunity mechanism is not a technical embellishment but a core structural object.

**Estimated speaking time**

* 2.0 minutes.

## Slide 5 — Model and identification in a world of latent jobs

**Bullet points**

* Each woman chooses one package from 16 alternatives: non-employment plus five hours bins crossed with three wage terciles.
* Utility depends on disposable income, leisure, age, age squared, and two latent taste types.
* Opportunity heterogeneity enters through region × education differences in job availability.
* Identification comes from jointly fitting package choices and systematic region × education gradients in employment and hours.

**What visual goes on the slide**

* Matrix of the 16 job packages, with a side bracket separating the preference block from the opportunity block.

**Speaker notes**

* The model treats labor supply as choice among job packages rather than free choice over hours on a universal budget set.
* That is the core structural move needed to answer subquestion 1.
* I keep both the opportunity block and the preference block parsimonious so that the decomposition does not look mechanically generated by excessive flexibility.
* The main empirical risk is weak separation between leisure tastes and offer probabilities, so transparency matters more than saturation.

**Estimated speaking time**

* 2.5 minutes.  

## Slide 6 — Subquestion 2: how do I compute welfare when feasible job sets differ?

**Bullet points**

* Welfare is equivalent monthly disposable income at a non-employment/full-leisure reference state.
* Welfare is computed from expected utility conditional on the estimated feasible set.
* The baseline inequality object is the Gini of individual welfare levels.
* Decomposition uses a two-factor Shapley-Shorrocks rule: opportunity heterogeneity versus preference heterogeneity.

**What visual goes on the slide**

* Flow chart: estimated model → expected utility → equivalent monthly disposable income → counterfactual equalizations → welfare Gini decomposition.

**Speaker notes**

* The second subquestion is about welfare measurement, not estimation.
* Once opportunities differ across people, welfare cannot be computed as if everyone faced the same feasible set.
* I therefore use an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare object defined at a common non-employment/full-leisure reference.
* That gives me a welfare measure that is comparable across individuals without pretending that the opportunity environment is identical for everyone.

**Estimated speaking time**

* 2.0 minutes.  

## Slide 7 — Subquestion 3: how much welfare inequality is opportunity-driven?

**Bullet points**

* Disposable-income Gini: 0.164; opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini: 0.186.
* Opportunity equalization lowers the welfare Gini to 0.151.
* Preference equalization lowers the welfare Gini to 0.140; equalizing both lowers it to 0.097.
* Opportunity heterogeneity explains 0.039 Gini points, or 43.8% of the explained component and 21.0% of baseline welfare inequality.

**What visual goes on the slide**

* Main stacked-bar decomposition figure, with a small inset table reporting baseline, opportunity-equalized, preference-equalized, and both-equalized Ginis.

**Speaker notes**

* This slide is the full answer to the main question, and it is the natural stopping point for a 15-minute version.
* Welfare inequality is higher than income inequality once constrained opportunities are recognized explicitly.
* Preferences still explain slightly more than opportunities, which makes the result balanced and credible rather than rhetorical.
* But the opportunity component is far too large to be treated as background friction, and that is the paper’s central empirical takeaway.

**Estimated speaking time**

* 2.5 minutes.

## Slide 12 — Subquestion 1 revisited: what the common-choice-set benchmark misses

**Bullet points**

* Full model pseudo-R²: 0.271 versus 0.214 in the common-choice-set benchmark.
* Exact package hit rate rises from 33.8% to 39.2%.
* Employment classification hit rate reaches 82.4%.
* The benchmark compresses the region × education employment gradients that the full model recovers.

**What visual goes on the slide**

* Left panel: observed versus predicted employment by region × education. Right panel: fit comparison table between the full model and the benchmark.

**Speaker notes**

* This is the main empirical evidence behind the first subquestion.
* I do not claim to observe directly how much taste heterogeneity the benchmark overstates in parameter space, because the completed-results pack does not report that object.
* But the fit evidence is exactly what one would expect if omitted opportunities were being misread as preferences: the benchmark fits worse and systematically compresses the cross-cell gradients.
* So the paper’s interpretation is that explicit opportunity modeling reduces the scope for wrongly attributing constrained outcomes to tastes.

## Slide 13 — Subquestion 3 revisited: decomposition and responsibility sensitivity

**Bullet points**

* Baseline opportunity component: 0.039 Gini points, or 21.0% of baseline welfare inequality.
* If only education is compensated, the opportunity component falls to 0.031.
* If only region is compensated, it falls to 0.026.
* Even under stricter responsibility stances, opportunities still explain about 14–17% of baseline welfare inequality.

**What visual goes on the slide**

* Three-bar responsibility-sensitivity figure, with one reminder line showing the baseline decomposition.

**Speaker notes**

* The main challenge to any opportunity-based decomposition is that it may look normatively loaded.
* I therefore vary the definition of compensable opportunities.
* As expected, the opportunity contribution falls when the compensation principle is tightened, but it does not disappear.
* That is an important result because it shows that the paper’s main conclusion is not an artifact of one very expansive ethical stance.

**Estimated speaking time**

* 4.5 minutes. 

## Slide 14 — Conclusion: answers to the main question and the three subquestions

**Bullet points**

* Main question: a substantial part of welfare inequality is attributable to unequal job opportunities in the baseline Belgium application.
* Subquestion 1: models without explicit constrained opportunities risk misreading opportunity gradients as taste heterogeneity.
* Subquestion 2: welfare should be computed conditional on the feasible set, not on a universal menu.
* Subquestion 3: under reasonable responsibility-sensitive views, opportunities account for a large but not dominant share of welfare inequality; broader EUROMOD coverage is the natural extension.

**What visual goes on the slide**

**Speaker notes**
