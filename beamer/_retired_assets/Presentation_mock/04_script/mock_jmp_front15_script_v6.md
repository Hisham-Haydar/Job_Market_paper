## Slide 3

**Title**
**Economists should care because outcomes do not mean the same thing for everyone**

**Final bullet points**

* The same observed bundle can be a **chosen optimum** for one person and a **constrained second-best** for another.
* If opportunities are omitted, standard models risk absorbing them into **tastes**.
* Then both the **positive interpretation** and the **normative interpretation** of inequality are distorted.
* This matters for how economists think about **compensation**, **responsibility**, and **redistribution**.

**Visual note**
Insert a two-person schematic: same observed hours-income point, but different feasible job sets behind each person.

**Speaker script**
The reason economists should care is that the same observed bundle can mean very different things. One person may be at a given hours-income point because it is her preferred option from a rich feasible set. Another may be at the same point because better packages are missing from her feasible set. If the model ignores that distinction, it is forced to explain both cases through tastes. That distorts the science, because part of what looks like taste heterogeneity is really variation in access to jobs. It also distorts the welfare interpretation, because the same observed outcome is treated as if it came from the same opportunity environment. So this is not a technical detail. It matters for how economists think about compensation, responsibility, and redistribution.

## Slide 7

**Title**
**Unequal opportunities explain a large share of welfare inequality**

**Final bullet points**

* Disposable-income Gini: **0.164**; opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini: **0.186**.
* Opportunity equalization lowers the welfare Gini to **0.151**.
* Preference equalization lowers it to **0.140**; equalizing both lowers it to **0.097**.
* Opportunity heterogeneity explains **21.0%** of baseline welfare inequality.

**Visual note**
Insert the main stacked-bar decomposition figure, with a small inset table showing baseline **0.186**, opportunity-equalized **0.151**, preference-equalized **0.140**, and both-equalized **0.097**.

**Speaker script**
Now the main result. In the Belgium baseline, disposable-income inequality and welfare inequality are not the same object. The disposable-income Gini is 0.164, while the opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini is 0.186. So once unequal feasible job sets are brought into the welfare measure, measured inequality rises. I then ask what happens under counterfactual equalizations. If I equalize opportunities, the welfare Gini falls from 0.186 to 0.151. If I equalize preferences, it falls to 0.140. If I equalize both, it falls to 0.097. Under the Shapley-Shorrocks decomposition, opportunity heterogeneity accounts for 0.039 Gini points, which is 43.8 percent of the explained component and 21.0 percent of baseline welfare inequality. Preferences still explain slightly more, at 0.050 Gini points. That is exactly the balance I want the audience to take away. Opportunities matter a great deal, but the paper does not claim they explain everything. It claims they explain too much to be treated as background friction.
