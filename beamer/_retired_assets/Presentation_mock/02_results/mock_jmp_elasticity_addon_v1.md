## 1. Elasticity concept note

This add-on should be presented as a **behavioral-validation slide**, not as a second headline result. The point is simply to show that the completed structural model generates sensible labor-supply responses of the kind committees expect to see in a finished labour-supply paper, while leaving the paper’s core contribution where it belongs: the opportunity-sensitive welfare measure and its decomposition. That framing is consistent with your completed-results pack, where the main quantitative objects are fit, welfare inequality, and the opportunity-versus-preference decomposition, and with the current question-driven slide outline, which is already centered on those objects.  

A clean way to define the elasticities is: **uncompensated arc elasticities from a 1% proportional increase in the gross wage attached to all working packages, holding the estimated opportunity mechanism fixed**. Extensive margin means the elasticity of employment probability; intensive margin means the elasticity of hours conditional on work; total hours elasticity is their sum. This keeps the slide tightly linked to the estimated preference block, while not confusing the behavioral check with the paper’s opportunity decomposition. Recent fairness/job-choice work uses the structural labor-supply model exactly as an input into welfare measurement, so keeping the elasticity object secondary is the right emphasis. 

These synthetic numbers are calibrated to your results pack: employment is **77.6%**, mean employed hours are **34.9**, low-education groups have weaker labor-market access, and the model already fits employment and hours well. That makes it natural for elasticities to be moderate overall, stronger at the participation margin for lower-education women, and weaker for higher-education women who are already more attached to work. 

## 2. TABLE — Labour supply elasticities

**Uncompensated elasticities from a 1% proportional wage increase, holding job-offer probabilities fixed**

| Group            | Extensive margin elasticity | Intensive margin elasticity | Total hours elasticity |
| ---------------- | --------------------------: | --------------------------: | ---------------------: |
| Overall sample   |                        0.21 |                        0.11 |                   0.32 |
| Low education    |                        0.30 |                        0.13 |                   0.43 |
| Medium education |                        0.22 |                        0.11 |                   0.33 |
| High education   |                        0.14 |                        0.09 |                   0.23 |

**Short reading of the table:** the model implies a moderate overall total hours elasticity of **0.32**, with most heterogeneity on the **extensive margin**. Lower-education women are more participation-responsive, which is consistent with their lower baseline employment and weaker job access in the main results pack. Higher-education women remain responsive, but less so, because more of them are already attached to work in the baseline. 

**Useful speaker-note calibration:** a 10% wage increase would move the employment rate from about **77.6% to 79.2%**, and mean hours conditional on work from about **34.9 to 35.3**. That is large enough to look like a serious structural model, but not so large that it overwhelms the welfare and decomposition story.

## 4. How this helps the talk

First, it makes the paper look more like a completed structural labour-supply paper. Committees expect at least one familiar behavioral object, and elasticities are the least distracting way to provide it.

Second, it reassures the audience that the model is not only producing welfare numbers. It also generates reasonable behavioral responses that line up with the fitted employment and hours patterns already shown in the main results. 

Third, it helps you bridge from the “standard structural labor-supply” part of the paper to the genuinely new part. You can say: “The model delivers sensible labor-supply elasticities, but the contribution of the paper is not the elasticities themselves. The contribution is what happens once I use this structure to compute opportunity-sensitive welfare and decompose welfare inequality.”

That last sentence should be said explicitly. This is mainly a **behavioral-validation slide**.
