## 15 likely full-talk committee questions

**1. What is the main contribution once I move beyond the headline decomposition slide?**
The main contribution is the full empirical pipeline. I estimate a structural labor-supply model with latent job opportunities, compute an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare object conditional on the feasible set, and then decompose welfare inequality into opportunity and preference components. The decomposition is the visible payoff, but it rests on the prior contribution of treating opportunities consistently across estimation and welfare measurement.  

**2. Why is the Belgium baseline a credible first application rather than just a convenient narrow case?**
Because the Belgium baseline gives a clean first-pass environment in which the central mechanism can be identified and interpreted without being diluted by joint household choice, childcare interactions, or self-employment noise. The sample is economically meaningful rather than marginal: 3,284 women, 77.6% employment, mean employed hours of 34.9, and meaningful region-by-education variation for the opportunity block.  

**3. Why use 16 job packages? Why is that not too coarse?**
It is deliberately coarse because the first paper needs a package structure that is rich enough to capture the key margins for welfare — working time and earning power — but narrow enough to keep identification transparent. The 16 alternatives are the smallest defensible design: non-employment plus five hours bins crossed with three wage terciles. That gives the opportunity block something economically meaningful to explain without making the decomposition dependent on a very fine job taxonomy.  

**4. What do the preference estimates tell you economically?**
They tell me that preferences are meaningful but disciplined. Utility loads positively on log disposable income, the model recovers two latent taste types, the more leisure-oriented type accounts for about 39% of the population, and the value of leisure rises with age at a decreasing rate. So the model does allow real preference heterogeneity, but it does not need extreme taste dispersion to fit the data once opportunities are modeled separately.  

**5. What do the opportunity estimates tell you economically?**
They show that access to job packages is sharply stratified by region and education. Relative to medium-educated women in Flanders, offer intensity is lower in Wallonia and Brussels, lower for low education, and higher for high education. In implied terms, relative total offer intensity ranges from 0.65 to 1.16 across region-by-education cells. Standard-hours packages are much more prevalent than fringe-hours jobs, and top-wage packages are relatively scarcer.  

**6. How do you know the opportunity block is not just decorative?**
Because it improves the model where the mechanism should matter. The full opportunity-sensitive specification raises pseudo-R² from 0.214 to 0.271, increases the exact package hit rate from 33.8% to 39.2%, and delivers 82.4% employment classification accuracy. Most importantly, it recovers the strong region-by-education gradients in employment that the common-choice-set benchmark compresses.  

**7. Why does welfare inequality exceed disposable-income inequality in your baseline?**
Because the welfare object internalizes the fact that identical income can arise from very different feasible sets. Once welfare is measured as equivalent monthly disposable income conditional on the estimated feasible set, a restricted opportunity set carries a welfare cost that disposable income alone does not see. That is why the welfare Gini is 0.186 while the disposable-income Gini is 0.164.  

**8. What is the main numerical result I should remember?**
The key numbers are these: the welfare Gini is 0.186, opportunity equalization lowers it to 0.151, preference equalization lowers it to 0.140, and equalizing both lowers it to 0.097. In the Shapley decomposition, opportunities contribute 0.039 Gini points and preferences 0.050. So opportunities explain 43.8% of the explained component and 21.0% of baseline welfare inequality.  

**9. If preferences still explain more than opportunities, why is this not just a preferences paper?**
Because the contribution is not that opportunities dominate preferences; it is that opportunities explain a large and previously obscured share of welfare inequality. A 0.039 contribution to the welfare Gini is too large to dismiss as background friction. The paper changes the decomposition of inequality, not by erasing preferences, but by preventing omitted opportunities from being silently absorbed into them. 

**10. How sensitive is the decomposition to the ethical treatment of opportunities?**
It is meaningfully sensitive, but not fragile. Under the baseline region × education compensation stance, the opportunity component is 0.039. If only education is compensated, it falls to 0.031. If only region is compensated, it falls to 0.026. So the number moves in the expected direction, but opportunities still explain roughly 14–17% of baseline welfare inequality under stricter responsibility interpretations.  

**11. Why is the paper centered on decomposition rather than on welfare levels or welfare rankings?**
Because the scientific question is about the source of welfare inequality, not simply its level. Welfare levels are endpoints. The decomposition tells me what part of inequality is associated with unequal opportunities and what part remains tied to preferences. That is the mechanism that matters for interpretation, and it is also the part that would be lost if the paper were framed as a ranking exercise.  

**12. What exactly is meant by “opportunity-sensitive” money-metric welfare?**
It means welfare is computed relative to a common non-employment/full-leisure reference state, but using expected utility conditional on the person’s estimated feasible set. So the metric stays comparable across individuals, while still respecting the fact that they do not all face the same job opportunities. That is the key difference from a universal-menu money-metric construction.  

**13. What would be the natural next extension of the paper?**
The natural next step is not to change the question, but to scale the same pipeline. That could mean richer opportunity definitions, additional demographic groups, or additional EUROMOD countries. But the point of the Belgium baseline is to establish the core mechanism first, so the broader agenda should be understood as portability and scalability rather than as the identity of the current paper. 

**14. Is this paper mainly positive or normative?**
It is best described as a positive paper with an explicit normative interpretation layer. The structural estimation and the opportunity block are positive objects. The welfare construction and the decomposition require a clear stance on what should count as compensable opportunity heterogeneity. I treat that stance transparently and then show sensitivity to stricter interpretations.  

**15. What do you see as the main empirical limitation of the current paper?**
The main empirical limitation is that opportunities and preferences are difficult to separate perfectly in this class of models. That is why the first version stays parsimonious and why I use the benchmark comparison and the responsibility-sensitivity exercises as discipline. I would rather defend a narrow and interpretable opportunity mechanism than a broader but weaker first paper.  

## 5 difficult or hostile questions

**1. You never directly identify individual opportunity sets. Why should I believe your “opportunity” label?**
Because the identification claim is modest and structural, not heroic. I do not claim to observe person-specific feasible sets. I identify an opportunity mechanism governing the relative availability of job packages across region-by-education cells and package types. That mechanism has economic structure, improves fit materially, and recovers the gradients that the benchmark misses. So “opportunity” here is not a metaphor; it is the modeled availability side of the job-package environment.  

**2. Isn’t the welfare result mechanically driven by the fact that you chose a welfare metric that bakes opportunities in?**
No. The welfare metric is designed to answer the question the paper asks. If the question is how welfare changes when people face different feasible job sets, then a universal-menu welfare metric would be the wrong object. What matters empirically is that once welfare is constructed in the opportunity-sensitive way implied by the model, the decomposition reveals a large opportunity component — and that result remains meaningful even under stricter responsibility stances.  

**3. This feels like a lot of structure for a result that preferences still explain slightly more than opportunities. Why is that interesting?**
Because the paper is not trying to prove that opportunities dominate everything else. The interesting result is that once opportunities are modeled explicitly, they explain 43.8% of the explained component of welfare inequality and 21.0% of baseline welfare inequality. That is a large enough share to change how we interpret heterogeneity in structural labor-supply models. The paper’s value is in revealing that share, not in forcing it to be the majority. 

**4. Why should I not think the benchmark comparison is just showing that the flexible model fits better because it has more moving parts?**
Because the relevant point is not just that overall fit improves, but where it improves. The full model does better exactly on the structured region-by-education gradients that the omitted-opportunity problem should affect. If the extra structure were just absorbing generic noise, I would not expect that targeted pattern. The empirical content is that the opportunity block improves fit where the mechanism lives.  

**5. Why did you not just make this a cross-country paper from the start? It would seem more marketable.**
Because it would be easier to make it broader and weaker than narrower and sharper. A cross-country first paper would risk turning the project into a welfare-ranking exercise or a descriptive comparison of tax-benefit systems. The real contribution is the decomposition logic in a structurally disciplined setting. Belgium is the right place to establish that contribution cleanly before scaling.

* How sensitive is the opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare measure to the choice of the non-employment/full-leisure reference state?

* How much of the opportunity component is really identified by region × education, and how much is left in the residual/common component?
