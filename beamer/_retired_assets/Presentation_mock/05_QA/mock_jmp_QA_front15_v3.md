Below is a question-and-answer pack built to match your front-loaded 15-minute core and the completed-results pack. The answers are written to sound like a strong candidate defending a finished paper, while staying disciplined about what the Belgium baseline actually shows.  

## 12 likely committee questions

**1. What is the paper actually about in one sentence?**
It is a structural labor-supply paper that asks how much welfare inequality is driven by unequal job opportunities rather than heterogeneous preferences, once labor supply is modeled as choice among latent job packages and welfare is measured conditional on those constraints. 

**2. Why is decomposition the central object rather than welfare levels?**
Because the scientific question is not just who is better off, but what generates welfare inequality. A welfare level or ranking is an endpoint; the decomposition tells me how much of inequality is coming from opportunities versus preferences, which is the mechanism that matters for interpretation and for policy relevance. In the baseline, opportunity heterogeneity explains 0.039 Gini points, or 43.8% of the explained component, which is exactly the kind of result a decomposition can reveal and a ranking cannot.  

**3. Why do you need a latent-jobs framework instead of a standard discrete-hours model?**
Because the core problem is that people do not face the same feasible menu. A standard common-choice-set model is forced to explain differences in observed outcomes largely through tastes. The latent-jobs framework lets me separate the ranking of packages from access to packages, which is precisely what the paper needs in order to measure welfare conditional on feasible sets and then decompose inequality. 

**4. Why Belgium?**
Belgium is the clean first application because it combines a credible disposable-income mapping through EUROMOD with a tractable structural environment for a first-pass latent-opportunity model. The paper needs a narrow, defensible baseline more than a broad one. Once that contribution is established, the framework can scale to other EUROMOD countries, but the Belgium baseline is where identification and interpretation are cleanest.  

**5. Why restrict to prime-age childless single women?**
Because that restriction removes three sources of avoidable complexity in the first paper: joint household choice, childcare-related tax-benefit interactions, and self-employment income measurement. The goal is not to claim singles are the whole labor market; it is to isolate the opportunity-versus-preference problem in the cleanest possible environment.  

**6. What exactly are the opportunities in your model?**
Opportunities are not directly observed individual job sets. What is identified is an opportunity mechanism governing the relative availability of job packages. In the baseline, that mechanism varies by region × education and determines both total offer intensity and the composition of offered packages across hours and wage terciles. 

**7. How do you know you are not just relabeling tastes as opportunities?**
I do not claim perfect separation at the individual level. The point is that once I allow an explicit opportunity block, I fit the data materially better exactly where omitted opportunities should matter: the full model reaches a pseudo-R² of 0.271 versus 0.214 in the common-choice-set benchmark, the exact package hit rate rises from 33.8% to 39.2%, and the model recovers the strong region × education employment gradients that the benchmark compresses. That is the empirical reason to take the opportunity block seriously. 

**8. What is the welfare object exactly?**
It is opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare, expressed as equivalent monthly disposable income at a non-employment/full-leisure reference state. I compute expected utility conditional on the estimated feasible set and then convert that into the cash amount at the common reference point that makes the person indifferent. That is what lets welfare remain comparable while still respecting different opportunity sets.  

**9. What is the headline result numerically?**
The disposable-income Gini is 0.164, while the opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini is 0.186. Equalizing opportunities lowers the welfare Gini to 0.151; equalizing preferences lowers it to 0.140; equalizing both lowers it to 0.097. In the Shapley decomposition, opportunity heterogeneity explains 0.039 Gini points, or 43.8% of the explained component and 21.0% of baseline welfare inequality. Preferences explain slightly more, at 0.050 Gini points. 

**10. Why does welfare inequality exceed disposable-income inequality in your results?**
Because disposable income does not capture the welfare cost of facing a more restricted feasible set. Two people can have similar income but very different welfare once one of them reaches that outcome from a much poorer set of opportunities. Once the welfare object is made opportunity-sensitive, the Gini rises from 0.164 to 0.186. 

**11. What do the opportunity estimates say economically?**
They say access to jobs is sharply stratified by region and education. Relative to medium-educated women in Flanders, total offer intensity is lower in Wallonia and Brussels, lower for low education, and higher for high education. The implied relative offer intensity ranges from 0.65 to 1.16 across region × education cells. The composition results also show that standard-hours jobs are much more prevalent than fringe-hours jobs, while top-wage packages are relatively scarcer. 

**12. How robust is the opportunity result to a different responsibility stance?**
It is meaningfully smaller under stricter responsibility definitions, but it does not disappear. In the baseline, the opportunity component is 0.039 Gini points. If only education is treated as compensable, it falls to 0.031. If only region is treated as compensable, it falls to 0.026. So the exact number moves, as it should, but opportunities still explain about 14–17% of baseline welfare inequality even under stricter interpretations. 

## 5 difficult or hostile questions

**1. This just looks like a highly parameterized relabeling exercise. Why should I believe the opportunity component is real?**
Because the opportunity block earns its keep empirically. It improves fit relative to the common-choice-set benchmark, and it improves fit precisely where omitted opportunities should matter most: the benchmark compresses the region × education gradients in employment, while the full model recovers them. So I am not just relabeling a residual; I am showing that an explicit opportunity mechanism explains structured variation that the standard model handles less well. 

**2. You say standard models overstate preference heterogeneity, but you never show a direct decomposition of taste variance across models. Isn’t that an overclaim?**
I would state it carefully. What I show directly is that a model without explicit opportunity heterogeneity fits worse and systematically compresses the cross-cell gradients that the full model matches. That is exactly the pattern one would expect if omitted opportunities were being absorbed into tastes. So the paper’s claim is not that I nonparametrically identify “true tastes.” The claim is that explicit opportunity modeling materially reduces the need to explain structured labor-market differences through preferences alone.  

**3. Why should anyone care about Belgium if you could have made this a Europe-wide paper?**
Because breadth is not the same thing as contribution. The contribution here is the structural decomposition of welfare inequality into opportunity and preference components. That requires a clean first application more than a large country panel. Belgium is the right place to establish the mechanism. Once that is credible, scaling across EUROMOD countries is straightforward as an agenda, but doing that first would risk turning the paper into a diffuse ranking exercise rather than a clear mechanism paper. 

**4. Your normative results depend on what counts as compensable opportunities. Doesn’t that make the paper too ethically fragile?**
No. It means the paper is being explicit where many papers are implicit. I show the baseline decomposition under region × education as compensable opportunities, and then I tighten the stance. The opportunity component declines from 0.039 to 0.031 or 0.026 depending on the definition, but it remains economically meaningful. So the paper does not rest on one extreme ethical choice; it shows that the main conclusion survives across a reasonable range of responsibility-sensitive interpretations. 

**5. If preferences still explain more than opportunities, why is the paper not just another preferences paper?**
Because the central finding is not that opportunities dominate preferences; it is that opportunities explain a large and previously obscured share of welfare inequality. In the baseline, opportunities account for 43.8% of the explained component and 21.0% of baseline welfare inequality. That is too large to treat as background friction. The contribution is precisely that once opportunities are modeled explicitly, the decomposition changes in a quantitatively important way. 

## One-minute answer: What is the key contribution of the paper?

The key contribution is to build one empirical pipeline that treats opportunities consistently across estimation, welfare measurement, and inequality decomposition. On the estimation side, I model labor supply as choice among latent job packages rather than as free choice on a common menu. On the welfare side, I compute an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare object conditional on the estimated feasible set. On the decomposition side, I use counterfactual equalizations and a Shapley-Shorrocks rule to attribute welfare inequality to opportunities and preferences. The result is not just a welfare level or a country ranking. It is a structural answer to the question of what drives welfare inequality. In the Belgium baseline, opportunities explain 0.039 Gini points, or 43.8% of the explained component of welfare inequality. That is the paper’s main contribution: it makes opportunities a measurable source of welfare inequality rather than a hidden modeling residual.  

## One-minute answer: What exactly is identified as opportunities rather than preferences?

What is identified is not a fully observed individual feasible set. What I identify is an opportunity mechanism that governs the relative availability of job packages. In the baseline, that mechanism varies with region and education and has two parts: total market access and the composition of offered packages across hours and wage groups. Preferences then rank packages conditional on access. So the identification claim is modest but meaningful. I am not saying I can observe “true opportunities” person by person. I am saying that once I allow a separate opportunity block, the model recovers structured heterogeneity in job access that would otherwise be pushed into tastes. Empirically, that is supported by the improvement in fit and by the ability of the full model to recover the strong region × education gradients in employment that the common-choice-set benchmark compresses. 

## One-minute answer: Why do you need a latent-jobs / RURO framework?

I need a latent-jobs framework because the whole question of the paper is about constrained opportunities. A standard discrete-hours model assumes that everyone faces essentially the same menu and differs mainly in preferences. That is exactly the structure I want to test against. The latent-jobs or RURO logic lets me separate two things that the standard model conflates: how people rank packages, and which packages are statistically available to them. Without that separation, I cannot answer subquestion 1, because omitted opportunities are forced into tastes. I also cannot answer subquestion 2 correctly, because welfare would be computed as if everyone faced a universal menu. And without both of those steps, the decomposition in subquestion 3 would not be interpretable. So I do not use RURO because it is fashionable. I use it because the question of the paper logically requires a framework with heterogeneous feasible sets. 

## One-minute answer: Why is the decomposition result the main contribution rather than the welfare level itself?

Because welfare levels tell you where people stand, but decomposition tells you why inequality exists. A welfare level or welfare ranking is an endpoint. It can be informative, but it does not isolate the mechanism driving dispersion. My paper is about whether unequal opportunities are being hidden inside preferences and how much that matters for welfare inequality. That is inherently a decomposition question. In the baseline, the welfare Gini is 0.186 and the income Gini is 0.164, but the most informative fact is not simply that welfare inequality is higher. It is that equalizing opportunities lowers the welfare Gini to 0.151, and that the Shapley decomposition assigns 0.039 Gini points to opportunities. That moves the paper from “one more welfare number” to a structural statement about the sources of inequality. That is why decomposition, not welfare level per se, is the core contribution. 

## One-minute answer: Why Belgium first if you have all European EUROMOD input data?

Because the first paper should establish the mechanism cleanly before scaling it broadly. If I started with all EUROMOD countries, the project would be much more likely to drift into a welfare-ranking or descriptive comparison paper, which is not the contribution I want. Belgium gives me a disciplined first application: one tax-benefit system, one clean sample, one opportunity structure based on region × education, and a tractable 16-package design. That is exactly the right environment to show that explicit opportunity modeling changes welfare measurement and decomposition in a quantitatively important way. Once that mechanism is established, the framework is naturally portable to other EUROMOD countries. So the European coverage is valuable, but as scalability and agenda. Belgium comes first because the contribution of this paper is decomposition, not scope.
