## 12 likely committee questions

**1. What is the paper’s key contribution?**
The contribution is a unified empirical pipeline. I estimate a latent-jobs labor-supply model with both preferences and opportunities, compute an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare level, and then decompose welfare inequality into opportunity and preference components. Relative to Aaberge et al. and Dagsvik & Jia, I do not stop at fit or reform simulation; relative to Bargain et al., I do not stop at welfare measurement under heterogeneous preferences; relative to Roemer-type opportunity work, I do not stay at reduced-form circumstance partitions.

**2. Why do you need a latent-jobs or RURO framework here?**
Because the core question is about constrained opportunities. A standard common-choice-set model is built to explain observed differences mainly through tastes. The RURO or latent-jobs structure lets me separate the ranking of packages from access to packages, which is exactly what I need if I want welfare to be conditional on the feasible set rather than on a universal menu. That is why papers like Aaberge et al. and Dagsvik & Jia are the right positive foundation for this project.

**3. Why Belgium, and why this sample of childless single women?**
Because the first paper needs a clean environment more than a broad one. Belgium gives me a coherent tax-benefit environment through EUROMOD, and the sample restriction removes joint household choice, childcare interactions, and self-employment measurement issues. The point is not that singles are the whole labor market; it is that this is the narrowest defensible design for identifying and interpreting the opportunity-versus-preference decomposition.

**4. What exactly is identified as “opportunities” in your model?**
I do not claim to observe each individual’s full feasible set. What is identified is an opportunity mechanism: the relative availability of wage-hours job packages, through job-offer intensity and package composition. In the baseline, that mechanism varies with region and education, and it generates economically meaningful gradients in access to work packages across cells.

**5. How do you separate opportunities from preferences?**
Structurally, I separate ranking from access. Preferences are disciplined by age, age squared, and latent taste types; opportunities are disciplined by the region × education offer mechanism. Empirically, the defense is that the opportunity-sensitive model improves fit precisely where omitted opportunities should matter most: it recovers the strong region-by-education gradients that the common-choice-set benchmark compresses.

**6. Why is decomposition the main contribution rather than welfare levels or ranking?**
Because welfare levels tell you where people stand, but decomposition tells you why inequality exists. A ranking cannot tell whether low welfare reflects constrained access to decent jobs or differences in preferences. The paper’s scientific object is exactly that mechanism question, and that is why I use structural counterfactual equalizations plus a Shapley-Shorrocks rule rather than stopping at a level comparison.

**7. What is the main quantitative result?**
The main result is that the opportunity-sensitive welfare Gini is 0.186, compared with a disposable-income Gini of 0.164. Equalizing opportunities lowers the welfare Gini to 0.151, equalizing preferences lowers it to 0.140, and equalizing both lowers it to 0.097. In the Shapley decomposition, opportunities explain 0.039 Gini points, or 43.8 percent of the explained component and 21.0 percent of baseline welfare inequality. 

**8. If preferences still explain slightly more than opportunities, why is the result important?**
Because the contribution is not that opportunities dominate everything. It is that opportunities explain a large and previously hidden share of welfare inequality once they are modeled explicitly. A 0.039 contribution to the welfare Gini is too large to treat as background friction, and the result is stronger because it is balanced rather than rhetorical. 

**9. Why is welfare inequality larger than income inequality in your baseline?**
Because income does not capture the welfare cost of facing a narrower feasible set. Two people can have similar disposable income but arrive there from very different opportunity environments. Once I evaluate them through an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare metric, that missing constraint information shows up, and the welfare Gini rises from 0.164 to 0.186.

**10. What does the benchmark comparison add beyond fit statistics?**
It shows that the opportunity block is doing meaningful empirical work rather than just adding flexibility. The full model improves pseudo-R² and hit rates, but more importantly it recovers the strong region × education gradients in employment that the common-choice-set benchmark compresses. That is exactly the pattern one would expect if omitted opportunities were otherwise being re-labeled as tastes.

**11. How important is the responsibility-sensitive part of the paper?**
It is important, but it is not the whole paper. I make explicit that the level of the opportunity component depends on what one treats as compensable opportunity heterogeneity. When I tighten the criterion from full region × education compensation to education-only or region-only compensation, the opportunity component falls from 0.039 to 0.031 or 0.026, but it does not disappear. So the result is not pinned to one single ethical stance.

**12. What is the next step after this paper?**
The next step is scale-up, not a change of question. The framework is portable to richer opportunity definitions, broader demographic groups, and additional EUROMOD countries. But I see those as extensions of the same core contribution, not as substitutes for getting the Belgium baseline right first.

## 5 difficult or hostile questions

**1. You do not observe opportunity sets. Why should I believe your “opportunity” interpretation at all?**
Because the identification claim is modest and structural. I do not claim to recover person-by-person feasible sets. I claim to recover a job-offer mechanism over wage-hours packages that varies systematically with observables and improves fit exactly where omitted constraints should matter. In this setting, “opportunities” means modeled availability, not directly observed menus.

**2. Isn’t this just relabeling unobservables as opportunities?**
No. A pure relabeling story would improve fit mechanically without adding economic structure. Here the opportunity block has interpretable content: it produces lower offer intensity for low-education women in Brussels and Wallonia, higher offer intensity for highly educated women in Flanders, and a realistic concentration of work in standard-hours packages. The benchmark comparison then shows that this structure matters exactly where the omitted-opportunity problem should show up. 

**3. Your normative result depends on arbitrary responsibility assumptions. Why should anyone take the decomposition seriously?**
Because the paper is explicit where many papers are implicit. I show how the opportunity contribution changes when I change what counts as compensable opportunity heterogeneity. The contribution falls when I tighten the responsibility criterion, which is what it should do, but it remains economically meaningful. That is a strength, not a weakness: the paper makes the normative dependence transparent rather than hiding it.

**4. Why should a hiring committee care about Belgium and a narrow sample? This sounds too local.**
The narrowness is strategic. The value of this paper is the mechanism, not the scope. Belgium gives me one coherent environment in which I can show that explicit opportunities change welfare measurement and decomposition in a quantitatively important way. Once that is established, broader EUROMOD portability is straightforward, but breadth should come after credibility, not before it.

**5. Why is this not just a nice seminar idea rather than a strong JMP?**
Because it has all three things a JMP needs: a clear economic question, a disciplined empirical design, and a quantitative result that changes how we interpret a familiar object. The paper takes a standard structural labor-supply framework, modifies the opportunity side in a meaningful way, and delivers a committee-legible claim: opportunities explain 0.039 Gini points of welfare inequality, or 43.8 percent of the explained component. That is a real empirical contribution, not just a framing exercise.

## One-minute answer — What is the key contribution of the paper?

The key contribution is to build one empirical pipeline that treats opportunities consistently across estimation, welfare measurement, and inequality decomposition. On the estimation side, I model labor supply as choice among latent job packages rather than free choice on a common menu. On the welfare side, I compute an opportunity-sensitive money-metric welfare level conditional on the feasible set. On the decomposition side, I use structural equalization counterfactuals and a Shapley-Shorrocks rule to attribute welfare inequality to opportunities and preferences. Relative to Aaberge and Dagsvik-style latent-jobs work, I do not stop at fit or behavioral simulation. Relative to Bargain-style welfare work, I do not stop at welfare levels under heterogeneous preferences. Relative to Roemer-type opportunity work, I do not stay at reduced-form circumstance partitions. The contribution is their integration. Empirically, that delivers a clear headline: in the Belgium baseline, opportunities explain 0.039 Gini points, or 43.8 percent of the explained component of welfare inequality.

## One-minute answer — What exactly is new relative to the existing literature?

What is new is not structural labor supply by itself, and it is not money-metric welfare by itself. The novelty is that I treat the estimated opportunity mechanism as a first-class distributive primitive. Aaberge et al., Dagsvik et al., and Dagsvik & Jia show why latent jobs matter for behavior and identification. Bargain and related work show how welfare comparisons depend on preference heterogeneity and metric choice. Roemer and Trannoy, Ferreira and Gignoux, and Fleurbaey and Maniquet give the right ethical language about opportunity and responsibility. Shorrocks provides the right decomposition tool. But no paper in this architecture takes estimated job opportunities, translates them into opportunity-sensitive welfare, and then decomposes welfare inequality into opportunity and preference components inside one empirical pipeline. That is the gap I am filling. So the innovation is not one extra block in a model. It is the integration of four literatures into one committee-legible empirical object.

## One-minute answer — Why do you need a latent-jobs / RURO framework?

I need a latent-jobs framework because the central question of the paper is about constrained opportunities. A standard common-choice-set model is built to explain differences in behavior mainly through preferences, conditional on a menu that is effectively common. That is exactly the structure I want to move beyond. The RURO or latent-jobs logic, in the Aaberge–Dagsvik–Jia tradition, lets me separate two things: how individuals rank wage-hours packages, and which packages are statistically available to them. Without that separation, I cannot answer the first question of the paper, because omitted opportunities are automatically pushed into tastes. I also cannot answer the welfare question correctly, because welfare would be computed as if everyone faced the same menu. So the latent-jobs structure is not optional embellishment. It is the positive foundation required by the normative and decomposition questions the paper asks.

## One-minute answer — Why is decomposition the main contribution rather than welfare levels or ranking?

Because welfare levels tell you where people stand, but decomposition tells you why inequality exists. A welfare level can be useful, but it is still an endpoint. My paper is after a mechanism question: how much of welfare inequality reflects constrained access to job packages, and how much reflects preferences? That is why decomposition is the central object. This is also why I explicitly avoid turning the paper into a ranking exercise. Bargain and coauthors show that welfare rankings can move substantially with preference heterogeneity and metric choice. I want a different object: one that survives exactly because it is built from transparent structural counterfactuals and then attributed with an order-invariant Shapley-Shorrocks rule. In the Belgium baseline, the welfare Gini rises above the income Gini once opportunities enter the metric, and the decomposition shows that 0.039 Gini points are opportunity-driven. That is a much more informative empirical statement than simply saying who is ranked above whom.

## One-minute answer — How do you separate opportunities from preferences?

I separate them structurally, not by pretending I observe everyone’s true feasible set. Preferences are the ranking side of the model: they are disciplined by age, age squared, and latent taste types, and they govern how individuals value wage-hours packages. Opportunities are the access side of the model: they are disciplined by the region × education offer mechanism and govern the relative availability and composition of job packages. So the model distinguishes ranking from access. Then I ask whether that distinction matters empirically. The answer is yes, because the opportunity-sensitive model improves fit exactly where omitted constraints should matter most: it recovers the strong region-by-education employment gradients that the common-choice-set benchmark compresses. So I do not claim perfect point identification of each person’s true opportunity set. I claim that the model cleanly separates preference heterogeneity from a structured opportunity process well enough to change both welfare measurement and the decomposition of welfare inequality.
